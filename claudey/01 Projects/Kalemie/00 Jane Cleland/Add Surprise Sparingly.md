# CLELAND — ADD SURPRISE, SPARINGLY

> A story to me means a plot where there is some surprise. Because that is how life is—full of surprises.

## Hitchcock's Bomb Under the Table

- A bomb exploding with no warning = fifteen seconds of surprise. A bomb the audience can see ticking down while characters chat obliviously = fifteen minutes of suspense. Suspense beats surprise because it's built on the audience being *fully informed* — the dread is in the anticipation, not the shock.
- Surprise still has value: it delights on its own, and it's a reliable way to launch readers into a new patch of heightened tension. The two aren't opposed — a good surprise is often the ignition for the next stretch of suspense.

## The Anatomy of a Working Surprise

- Must be logical, not contrived — set up in advance by showing (not stating) a trait or fear, so when the surprise lands, it feels inevitable rather than plucked from nowhere.
- Three reliable forms: a first occurrence of an unexpected event; an anomaly (a clown that isn't benevolent); the revelation of a previously unknown fact.
- Avoid false urgency in the prose itself — cut *suddenly*, *out of nowhere*, *unexpectedly*, and most uses of the verb *to be*. Let the incident carry the shock, not the adverb.
