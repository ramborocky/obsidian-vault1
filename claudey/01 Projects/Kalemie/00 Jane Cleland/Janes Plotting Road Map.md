# CLELAND — JANE'S PLOTTING ROAD MAP

> Without an organizational scaffold, ideas are likely to slip away while confusion snowballs.

## The Highway and Two Service Roads

- The **highway** is your primary plot's path. **Service Road #1** and **Service Road #2** run alongside it — your two subplots (see [[Layer in Two Subplots]]).
- In a ~300-page novel: four **TRDs** (twist/reversal/danger) on the highway roughly every 70–90 pages, plus a subplot beat alternating SR1/SR2 roughly every 40 pages. For short fiction, the ratios scale down but the *rhythm* — plot beat, subplot beat, plot beat — still applies.

## Define Your Terms

- **Twist**: takes the reader in an *unexpected* direction.
- **Reversal**: takes the reader in the *opposite* direction of what's expected.
- **Danger**: a moment of heightened stakes, physical or emotional — doesn't have to change plot direction at all.
- Mix these three. A story that's all danger and no reversal reads as relentless without being surprising.

## Write to the Next TRD, Not the Whole Book

- You don't need total clarity on the ending to start. Plot only as far as your next TRD, write to it, then plot the next one. This keeps momentum without demanding the whole shape up front.
- A secondary benefit: writing point-to-point instead of "write 5,000 words" makes an intimidating project feel achievable in chunks.

## For Kalemie

- Scaled to a short story, Kalemie's "highway" already has natural TRD candidates logged across the Craft Log's scene list: Morisho's arrest (danger/reversal — the reader expects Jean Luc to be the threat, not Morisho), the BMW/Jean Luc encounter (twist — a stranger becomes central), the rain/twenty-seconds scene (danger, emotional), the police-station bribery (reversal — justice mechanism revealed as corrupt). Naming these explicitly as TRD types clarifies which beat is doing which job, and whether any two adjacent beats are accidentally doing the *same* job (all danger, no reversal, for instance).
- The story doesn't have room for two full service-road subplots in the classic sense, but Cleland's *purpose* test still applies: does a given digression (Anitha and the two-thousand-shillings scene, the "ghost kids" observations) exist to reveal character/mirror the theme, or is it scene-setting that could be cut or folded into the main line? The Craft Log already flags the neighbourhood paragraph in Scene 2 as exactly this kind of loose thread.
- Writing to the next TRD rather than the whole story matches how the Craft Log's own revision history has actually unfolded — scene by scene, correcting course as new information (like the AI Laboratory draft) surfaced.

## Questions for AI

1. Label every existing scene as T, R, or D (or a subplot beat) — are three of the same type stacked in a row anywhere, and if so, is that flat pacing or intentional accumulation of dread?
2. Does the Anitha/two-thousand-shillings beat and the "ghost kids" observation each pull weight as a mirror of Djamela's arc, or are either just atmosphere that could be compressed?
3. What is the next TRD after the current draft's final scene (the thin envelope) — has one been chosen, or is the ending still searching for its last turn?
