# CLELAND — THE DEVIL MADE ME DO IT (RED HERRINGS)

> Human nature is not black and white but black and grey.

## Three Categories of Red Herring

- **Human nature**: what people predictably think and do — the halo effect (kind to animals → assumed good through and through) and devil effect (sarcastic → assumed bad through and through) let you make the innocent look guilty and the guilty look innocent, purely through readers' own extrapolation habits.
- **Details**: what's included or left out. A detail hidden in the *middle* of a list (not the start or end, which readers remember best) is a fair-play way to plant a clue readers will forget noticing.
- **Expertise**: what a character knows that a layperson wouldn't — or the inverse, trusting an expert (doctor, lawyer) who turns out to be wrong. Society's cultural halo around professionals is itself a red herring device.

## The Rules of Fair Play

- Red herrings are a magician's tool, not a liar's tool — sleight of hand, not deception. If you smirch an innocent character's reputation to misdirect readers, you're obligated to restore their good name by the end.
- Repeating a small event (a missing knife, reappearing in odd places) layers suspense reliably — readers register the pattern before they understand it.

## For Kalemie

- Morisho is Kalemie's central red-herring case study, and the Craft Log already names the exact risk Cleland describes: "how do we calibrate this scene so Morisho reads as suspicious but not obviously guilty." The devil effect is already doing real work on him unprompted — a school dropout dala dala conductor handing cash to underage schoolgirls reads as devil-effect guilty by *appearance and role*, not by evidence, which is precisely the mechanism Cleland says to exploit and then must ethically resolve (his good name restored, or his actual guilt confirmed, by the end — not left ambiguous by accident).
- Jean Luc runs the opposite exploit — an inverted halo effect via claimed authority ("Directeur. Le consul général de Belgique") rather than earned goodness. Cleland's "trusting an expert who's wrong" category applies directly: Djamela and the reader are meant to extend him credibility because of a title, not because of demonstrated character — the same mechanism as trusting a doctor or lawyer by default.
- Expertise-based red herring: Daudi's physics vocabulary (entropy, gravity) is *his* expertise, and it currently reads as narrative voice/motif — but Cleland's chapter suggests it could also function as a red herring device: what does Daudi notice, because of his scientific habit of mind, that he misreads or dismisses precisely because it doesn't fit a tidy physical law? That's an unused red-herring opportunity specific to this narrator's expertise, distinct from Morisho and Jean Luc.
