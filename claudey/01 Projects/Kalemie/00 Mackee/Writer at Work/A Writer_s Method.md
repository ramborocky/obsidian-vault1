# MCKEE — A WRITER'S METHOD

> The difference between writers who succeed and writers who struggle is rarely talent — it's whether they work outside-in or inside-out.

## Writing From the Outside In (the trap)

- The struggling method: dream up an idea, go straight to full prose/pages, finish a full draft, then show it to friends. The feedback is always the same shape: "I loved these three scenes, but something about the middle/ending/opening doesn't work."
- The fatal next move: try to **keep the beloved scenes** and rebuild a new draft around them, again and again, draft after draft — clinging to favorite material instead of questioning whether the underlying story design works at all. Years can pass this way with the same structural flaw surviving every rewrite, because the writer never steps back from the finished prose to test the skeleton underneath it.

## Writing From the Inside Out (the discipline)

### 1. Step-Outline First — Before Any Prose

- Reduce the whole story to **one or two-sentence cards**, one per scene: *"He enters expecting to find her home, but instead finds her note saying she's left for good."*
- Mark, for each card, what structural job it's doing — setup, Inciting Incident, Act Climax, etc. — for the Central Plot and any subplot.
- The point of staying this cryptic for weeks or months: **you must be willing to destroy 90% of what you generate.** Working at the level of one-sentence cards makes it cheap to throw out a scene, a sequence, even a whole act — the same decision made after writing full prose feels catastrophic and gets resisted.
- Only once the *design* survives scrutiny — once you can find the Story Climax and work backward to confirm every card earns its place — do you move on.

### 2. Pitch It Before You Write It

- Tell the story out loud, from the step-outline, to someone whose reactions you trust — not asking them to "read a draft," just ten minutes of their attention.
- Watch their face, not just their words: are they leaning in at the Inciting Incident? Do their eyes wander in the middle? Does the ending actually land?
- **If a story can't work told in ten minutes, it won't work at full length either.** Whatever's wrong with it small will be ten times as wrong once it's expanded. Don't proceed past a pitch that doesn't earn a genuine, quiet "wow" — polite encouragement doesn't count.

### 3. Treatment — Full Scene-by-Scene Prose, No Dialogue Yet

- Expand each one-sentence card into a full paragraph of present-tense, moment-by-moment description of action *and* subtext — what's really being thought and felt underneath what's done — but **still without writing actual dialogue.**
- This step exists because writing down what a character truly thinks and feels, moment to moment, reveals things you didn't know you knew about them — "we may think we know what our characters are thinking, but we don't know we know until we write it down."
- Expect the treatment to reveal scenes that need cutting, reordering, or reworking, even though the *overall design* has already proven itself in pitching.

### 4. Draft Last, Not First

> The premature writing of dialogue chokes creativity.

- Writing dialogue/prose-voice too early makes a writer fall in love with their own sentences and reluctant to cut or restructure around them — "all improvisation ceases and rewriting becomes tinkering with speeches."
- Done in the right order, the final draft moves fast, and the resulting voice is sharper — because the underlying beats and subtext are already fully worked out, the writer isn't discovering the story *and* polishing sentences at the same time.
- If a real structural fault surfaces once real prose exists, the fix is rarely a local rewrite — it usually means going back to rework the setup/payoff at the outline or treatment level, not patching the surface.

## Questions for AI

1. Can I state this story's whole design in one or two sentences per scene, in order, without any prose polish yet?
2. If I pitched this story out loud in two minutes, would the ending land, or would my listener's attention drift somewhere in the middle?
3. Is there a scene I'm reluctant to cut mainly because I like the sentences in it, rather than because it's structurally necessary?
4. Does this scene's subtext (what's really being thought/felt) actually exist yet, or have I only worked out what's said and done on the surface?
