# MCKEE — CRISIS, CLIMAX, RESOLUTION

> The Chinese ideogram for Crisis combines Danger and Opportunity: the wrong choice loses forever what you want; the right choice achieves it. This is the dangerous, final decision — no tomorrow, no second chance.

## Crisis

- The protagonist has exhausted every action but one. The Crisis is the **Obligatory Scene** the whole story has been promising since the Inciting Incident — face to face with the most powerful, focused antagonism in their life.
- It must be **true dilemma**: irreconcilable goods, lesser of two evils, or both at once, under maximum personal pressure.
- **How they choose is the deepest possible reveal of true character** — and it clarifies, if it was ever in doubt, which value the whole story was actually about.
- Decisions, not actions, cost the most willpower — in life we dread deciding far more than we dread the doing, once the decision is made. Write the *decision* itself, don't skip to the action.
- **The Crisis Decision must be a deliberately static, held moment.** Don't rush past it or put it offscreen — the audience needs to suffer the dilemma alongside the protagonist. Delay is a tool (*Thelma & Louise*'s stuttering "go?... go." before the leap) — compressed anxiety is what makes the release into Climax land.

## Crisis Within the Climax — the Best Endings Turn Twice

- The strongest climaxes crack one more Gap open *during* the climactic action itself — the protagonist commits to a choice, but the world still doesn't cooperate the way expected, forcing one final improvisation (McKee's contrast: *Star Wars*'s Crisis→Climax is a single clean action; *The Empire Strikes Back* corkscrews through three successive Crisis Decisions in one scene as Vader's revelation keeps re-opening the dilemma).

## Placement Patterns

- Usually Crisis and Climax sit together, in the same final scene.
- Sometimes the Crisis Decision turns the **Penultimate** Act Climax, and the entire last act becomes one continuous climactic action (*Casablanca*: Rick's decision to give up Ilsa happens before the airport scene, which then plays out the consequences).
- Rarely, the Crisis Decision follows almost immediately after the Inciting Incident, and the whole story becomes one long climactic action (Bond accepting the mission; *Leaving Las Vegas*'s protagonist deciding to drink himself to death). High risk of repetitiveness if attempted without real variation.
- If Crisis and Climax happen in different locations, **cut directly between them** — cutting away to other material (a subplot) in between drains the built-up tension into anticlimax.

## Climax

> Meaning produces emotion. Not money, not sex, not special effects, not movie stars.

- **Meaning** = an absolute, irreversible swing in value (positive/negative/ironic) at maximum charge. The action that creates it must be **pure and self-evident** — if it needs dialogue or narration to explain it, it has failed as drama.
- Scale is irrelevant: it can be a battle (*Glory*) or a woman quietly packing a suitcase and walking out a door (*Ordinary People*) — what matters is the size of the value-shift, not the size of the event.
- **Stories are built backward from the Climax.** The ending is often imagined first (an unsupported flash of imagination), then everything else is engineered to earn it. Any scene that could be cut without weakening the ending's impact **must be cut.**
- Give the audience the emotion you've promised throughout (up, down, or ironic) but **not in the way they expected it.** Aristotle: the ending must be simultaneously "inevitable and unexpected" — inevitable in hindsight, unforeseeable in the moment.
- **Key Image**: the strongest endings crystallize into one single image that, remembered later, brings the whole film back with a jolt (Truffaut's "spectacle and truth" — spectacle meaning written for the eye, truth meaning the Controlling Idea made visible). McKee's examples: *Greed*'s McTeague chained to the corpse he just killed in the desert; *The Conversation*'s Harry Caul gutting his own apartment searching for a bug that isn't there.

## Resolution

- Whatever comes after Climax, with three legitimate jobs:
  1. **Close a subplot** that couldn't be folded into the main Climax (best done by giving the Central Plot one last small twist inside the Resolution scene, rather than a flat, separate coda).
  2. **Show the spread of effect** across a wider cast — a gathering scene (wedding, party) that lets the audience see how the ripple reached everyone they came to know.
  3. **The "slow curtain."** Even with nothing left to resolve, give the audience a beat to come down from the emotional peak before the lights go up — courtesy, not padding.

## For Kalemie

- The police-station scene is Daudi's Obligatory Scene / Crisis: frame the dilemma explicitly. Per the Dashboard's Controlling Idea, this is likely a **lesser-of-two-evils** choice (accept the thin envelope and the injustice it represents, vs. push further and risk what little safety/family stability he has) — write the *decision itself* as a held, static moment before cutting to whatever action follows.
- Test the ending's Key Image ("finds the envelope, nails it, smiles") against Truffaut's spectacle-and-truth standard: is this a *visual* image that alone, remembered later, carries the whole Controlling Idea (inaction as complicity / choosing to witness) — or does it currently need a line of narration to explain what it means? If it needs explaining, it isn't there yet.
- Consider a Crisis-within-Climax pass: after Daudi decides to nail the envelope up rather than act, does one more small gap open — some detail of the envelope, some interruption — that forces one last, smaller improvisation? A double-turn here would lift the ending from "clean" to "corkscrewed" the way *Empire Strikes Back* does.
- Because Kalemie is a short story, Resolution should likely be minimal — a few lines at most, a slow curtain rather than a full scene, since there's unlikely to be a subplot needing separate closure.
