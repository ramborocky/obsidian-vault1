# MCKEE — ACT DESIGN

> Nothing moves forward in a story except through conflict. Conflict is to storytelling what sound is to music — the instant it stops, the audience becomes aware of time passing, and it leaves.

## Progressive Complications & Points of No Return

- Each action the protagonist takes provokes antagonism and opens a Gap. Once a gap opens, that **magnitude of action is used up** — a point of no return. The story can never again solve the problem with an action of that size; only bigger, riskier ones from here on.
- The common "soft belly in the middle" failure (a film drags 20–30 minutes) is almost always the writer recycling actions of a size already spent in Act One. The audience's instincts register: *that didn't work before, why would it work now.*
- Fix: over-generate. Invent hundreds of possible scenes, then select the ~40–60 that build into acts that keep topping each other. If you only invent exactly as many scenes as the page count needs, the draft is structurally guaranteed to be repetitive.

## The Law of Conflict

- Reality is scarcity (Sartre) — never enough food, love, justice, or time. Story is a metaphor for that condition, so conflict is not decoration, it *is* the medium.
- Two failure modes at the extremes: **glut** (meaningless violence/spectacle from writers uninterested in real struggle) and **vacancy** (Pollyanna dramas that flinch from conflict, believing harmony is achievable and interesting). Removing conflict from one level of life doesn't remove it — it bulges out on another level (external harmony breeds inner boredom).

## Complication vs. Complexity

- **Complication** = conflict piled onto a *single* level (inner / personal / extra-personal). Produces either Stream-of-Consciousness prose, Soap Opera, or pure Action/Adventure — each needs an ever-larger cast and more locations to keep escalating on that one axis.
- **Complexity** = conflict on **multiple levels at once**, often in the same small scene (McKee's model: *Kramer vs. Kramer*'s French-toast scene runs inner conflict + personal conflict + extra-personal conflict simultaneously in three wordless minutes).
- Advice for most writers: **relatively simple but complex** — a contained cast and world, richly layered conflict, rather than proliferating characters and locations to manufacture scale.

## Act Structure — the Minimum Is Three

- Beats build scenes (each ideally a Turning Point). Scenes build sequences (moderate turn). Sequences build acts (major reversal). Acts build the story.
- Two reversals are never enough — "things were bad, then worse, the end" always feels like something's missing. **Three major reversals is the historical minimum** for a full-length work to reach "the end of the line."
- Typical feature rhythm: Act One ≈ 25% (Act One Climax ~20–30 min in); Act Three wants to be the *shortest* act, with rising acceleration, not stretched out.
- **The long Act Two problem** has two standard fixes: (1) add subplots, each with their own compact act structure, to multiply reversals and deepen involvement; (2) add more acts (a Mid-Act Climax splits Act Two into two, e.g. *Shine*). Beyond ~4–5 acts, more turns start to invite cliché and repetition — "major" only means something relative to "minor"; scream every 15 minutes and the audience goes numb to it.

## Act Rhythm — Climaxes Must Not Repeat Charge

- The Penultimate and Ultimate act climaxes usually sit close together and **must carry opposite charges**, or the second event's power is halved. Up-ending stories need a negative penultimate turn; down-ending stories need a positive one. Irony (both charges in one action) must still *lean* one way — perfectly balanced irony cancels into blandness.

## False Ending

- A scene late in the story that feels so complete the audience believes it's over, before the real climax hits (*Aliens*, *Terminator*'s double fake-out, Hitchcock's early "endings" in *Vertigo*/*Psycho*). Powerful but genre-specific — most stories are better served by the Penultimate Climax simply intensifying "now what?"

## Subplots — Four Legitimate Jobs

A subplot earns its place only if it does one of these; otherwise it "splits the story down the middle" and the audience loses the thread trying to unify it:

1. **Contradicts** the Central Plot's Controlling Idea → adds irony.
2. **Resonates** with it → variation on the same theme (or, with no Central Plot at all, becomes a Multiplot unified by an idea instead of a spine).
3. **Sets up** a late-arriving Inciting Incident, carrying the audience while the protagonist and world are established.
4. **Complicates** the Central Plot directly — an extra source of antagonism for the same protagonist (the Love Story tucked inside a Crime Story, raising what the protagonist stands to lose).

## Questions for AI

1. Is this scene's action bigger/riskier than the last time Daudi tried something similar, or is it a repeat at the same scale?
2. Am I stacking conflict on only one level here (just plot mechanics) or also personal and inner conflict in the same beat?
3. Does the scene right before my ending carry the opposite emotional charge from the ending itself?
4. If I added or cut this subplot beat, which of the four legitimate subplot jobs would it be doing — and if none, should it be cut?
