# MCKEE — COMPOSITION

> Composition is the ordering and linking of scenes — a composer choosing notes and chords. What to include, exclude, and put before or after what.

## Unity and Variety

- Test: the sentence **"Because of the Inciting Incident, the Climax had to happen"** should hold for your story. The Spine is the cement between them.
- Unity is necessary but not sufficient — within it, maximize *variety* (tone, genre-blend, mood-counterpoint) so no two scenes sound alike. Comes from research/authorial knowledge, not decoration.

## Pacing — Tension and Release

- Life itself rhythms between wanting serenity (too much = boredom) and wanting challenge (too much = exhaustion). Story must breathe the same way: **tighten, then release, then tighten further and top the last peak** — never a flat monotonic climb (audience goes numb) and never a flat calm (audience leaves).
- Practically: build tension to an Act climax, then *deliberately lower it* at the start of the next act (comedy, romance, a quieter counterpoint) so the audience can "catch its breath" before the next rise — cycles, not a straight line, get you to the biggest possible final release.

## Rhythm and Tempo

- **Rhythm** = scene *length*. Average scene ≈ 2–3 minutes of screen time; if every scene runs the same length (all ~5 pages), the story reads sluggish regardless of content. Vary short and long deliberately.
- **Tempo** = level of activity *within* a scene (quiet pillow-talk = low tempo; a riot = high tempo).
- As a story nears an act climax, generally **shorten scenes and raise tempo** to accelerate — but the climax scene itself should be allowed to breathe and slow down; "climactic" means profound change, not necessarily short and fast. Earn the pause: if everything before it was already slow, the big scene has nothing to contrast against and falls flat (Diminishing Returns again).

## Two Ways to Build Progression: Wide or Deep

- **Social Progression** — start intimate, let the consequences ramify outward into the wider world, family → community → society (why lawyers, doctors, cops, and politicians are overrepresented protagonists: their private trouble can plausibly expand outward).
- **Personal Progression** — when the setting can't go wide, go *deep*: drive down into intimate relationships and inner life, toward the buried secret behind the public mask (*Ordinary People*: a fixable-seeming tension between mother and son excavated down to a mother's confessed hatred of her own child).
- The strongest stories (McKee's example: *Chinatown*) do **both at once** — widening into society while simultaneously plunging into the protagonist's private wound.

## Symbolic Ascension

- Start with images, settings, and roles that mean only themselves; let them **gather symbolic weight gradually** as the telling progresses, until by the climax they carry universal/archetypal charge.
- Never label the symbol ("how symbolic!") — pointing at it kills it. It must land subliminally, the way dream-images do.

## Ironic Ascension — Six Patterns

Irony (McKee: "life is just like that") lives in the gap between action and result, not in a character noticing and naming it. Six recurring shapes:

1. **Gets what he always wanted — too late to have it.** (*Othello*)
2. **Pushed further from the goal — only to discover he's been led straight to it.**
3. **Throws away what he later finds indispensable.**
4. **Takes the exact steps needed to reach a goal — that unwittingly lead him away from it.** (*Tootsie*)
5. **The action taken to destroy something becomes what destroys the one who took it.**
6. **Gets something certain to bring misery, fights to get rid of it — discovers it's the gift of happiness.** (*Bringing Up Baby*)

Common thread: a protagonist who is *certain* of the plan (A→B→C→D→E) — irony is life running the letters in reverse.

## Principle of Transition

- Between any two scenes, find a **third element** — something they share, or something they oppose — to hinge the cut: a trait, an action, an object, a repeated word, a quality of light, a sound, an idea. Common-thread transitions feel fluid; opposed-element transitions feel like a jolt. Either is a deliberate choice, never an accident.
