# MCKEE — CLASSICAL DESIGN (ARCHPLOT)

> Classical design is a mirror of the human mind.

## The Definition

**CLASSICAL DESIGN** means a story built around an active protagonist who struggles against primarily external forces of antagonism to pursue his or her desire, through continuous time, within a consistent and causally connected fictional reality, to a closed ending of absolute, irreversible change.

- McKee calls this cluster the **Archplot** — "eminent above others of the same kind," not "old-fashioned." (See [[Story triangle]] for the full seven-way comparison against Miniplot and Antiplot.)
- These principles are "classical" in the strict sense: **timeless and transcultural.** When *Gilgamesh* was carved in cuneiform ~4,000 years ago, Classical Design was already fully formed. It is not a Western convention — Asian storytelling traditions have used the same Archplot principles for millennia, "enriching their tellings with a unique wit and irony." Archplot is neither ancient nor modern, Eastern nor Western: **it is human.**

## Why Classical Design Works — the Mirror-of-Mind Argument

- When you remember your own past, you don't recall it as fragmented or plotless — you shape memory into causal, closed, active-protagonist form. When you daydream about the future, the same thing happens.
- Classical design displays the temporal, spatial, and causal patterns of ordinary human perception. Deviating from it isn't neutral — it asks the audience to work *against* how memory and anticipation already function, which is exactly why Miniplot and Antiplot narrow the audience: not because they're worse, but because they run against the grain of how most people already organize experience.

## The Politics of Story Design — Practical Consequences

- **"Hollywood film" vs. "art film" is a real but overstated divide** — most Hollywood output isn't the archplot-only spectacle its critics claim, and most "art film" isn't as static/inconsistent as its champions claim. But the underlying philosophical split is real: an (often naive) optimism about the *possibility* of change vs. an (often equally naive, "fashionably") pessimism that change ever happens or means anything.
- **The practical law: as design moves away from Archplot, the audience shrinks — and so must the budget.** This isn't a quality judgment (masterpieces exist at every corner of the triangle) — it's simply that fewer people experience their own lives as open-ended, passive, coincidence-driven, or inconsistent, so fewer people connect with stories built that way.
- If you intend to earn a living from writing, this is a fact to plan around, not an insult to the other corners of the triangle.

## Master Classical Form First

- Miniplot and Antiplot are not independent inventions — they are **reactions to** the Classical, one shrinking it, the other reversing it. You cannot meaningfully bend or contradict a form you haven't mastered.
- Every writer who succeeded at the minimalist or antistructural corners of the triangle **started in the Classical**: Bergman wrote love stories and social dramas for twenty years before *The Silence* or *Persona*; Fellini made straightforward films before *Amarcord* or *8½*; Godard made *Breathless* before *Weekend*. First, the masters mastered the Archplot.
- "You'll know you understand it when you can do it" — familiarity from watching films is not the same as command of the craft.

## Believe in What You Write

- Don't choose a corner of the triangle for **political** reasons (wanting to seem like "not Hollywood," avoiding closure/causality/active protagonists just to signal artistic seriousness). That produces pretentiousness, not vision.
- The honest test: do you genuinely believe life brings little real change, or that events are fundamentally random and disconnected? If yes, minimalism or antistructure is your honest form. If the honest answer is no, writing that way anyway is "the literary equivalent of a temper tantrum" — difference for its own sake is as empty as slavish commercialism.

## For Kalemie

- Per [[Story triangle]], Kalemie is already positioned as Miniplot-leaning-Archplot (internal conflict meeting external conflict, open/ironic ending) — this note's mirror-of-mind argument explains *why* that's the right call rather than a compromise: readers will still bring classical, causal expectations to the story even though its ending is open, so causality and consequence should stay tight even where the ending itself stays unresolved.
- The "master classical form first" principle is worth taking literally here: before leaning further into ambiguity or a more literary, internalized register, make sure the causal chain (Inciting Incident → Progressive Complications → Crisis → Climax) is airtight and legible. An open/ironic ending earns its ambiguity only if everything leading to it was clear.
- "Believe in what you write" is a useful gut-check for the ending specifically: is the open/ironic quality of "finds the envelope, nails it, smiles" earned by genuine belief that this is how injustice actually resolves (or doesn't) — or is it reaching for literary ambiguity because a cleaner, closed ending would feel less "serious"? If the honest answer is the former, the current direction is right; if the latter, it's worth re-testing against the Controlling Idea.
