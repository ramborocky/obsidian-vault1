## 2. SENTENCE STRUCTURE — THE 20-WORD RULE
[[Sentence Check]]

### The Rule (Cleland)

- Average sentence length: **under 20 words**
- **Long sentences** (30–70 words): create mood, encapsulate, allow reflection
- **Short sentences** (4–10 words): catapult action, create tension, land punches
- **Fragments**: use sparingly; they hit like a closed fist

### The Formula

You can have a 42-word sentence if the sentences around it average out. What you cannot have is **five 40-word sentences in a row**. That's not lyrical — it's a fog.

```
Long sentence  → mood, world, interiority
Short sentence → action, shock, revelation
Fragment       → the thing that can't be finished
```

### Example — Before/After Applied to Kalemie Draft

**Before (from draft):**

> "It was only because Djamela didn't want to go alone, especially at Ave de Bobozo police station, fearing that she might not get out of there quickly."

This is 28 words doing 10 words of work. It buries the fear.

**After:**

> "Djamela didn't want to go alone. Not to Ave de Bobozo. You don't always come back out quickly from that place."

Now Daudi's voice is in it. The dread is in the third sentence, not stated but implied.

---

**Before (from draft):**

> "She had lost most of her pre-conceived hopes a rags to riches life that Jean Luc was to give her."

Run-on, unclear. Preconceived is one word. The idea is strong but the sentence fights itself.

**After:**

> "She had lost most of it by then. The rags-to-riches version of herself that Jean Luc was supposed to deliver."

Short sentence lands first. Long sentence carries the weight of what was expected.

---

### Sentence Rhythm — The Kalemie Register

Daudi's voice is: **scientific, clipped, then suddenly lyrical**. That's the rhythm. Short when observing. Long when feeling. Fragment when the feeling exceeds language.

```
Observing:   "Her fingernails, polished every Friday. Removed every Monday."
Feeling:     "It took only twenty seconds."
Exceeding:   "I gasped for air."
```

## 3. WHISPER, DON'T SHOUT — SOUND & SILENCE

### The Rule (Cleland)

> _His screams led people to think he was crazy. His silence led people to understand his fear._

The rule: **in moments of high emotion, go quiet**. Don't tell the reader what to feel. Let the small sound do it.

- Noise in the midst of silence → rupture, danger
- Silence in the midst of noise → dread, wrongness
- The click you hear before the gun → more terrifying than the shot

### The Pavlovian Sound Link

Plant a sound early. Return to it later under different circumstances. The reader will feel the shift without being told to.
#sound #silence

**Kalemie already has this:**

- _Mia tano mia tano_ — Morisho's voice, the dala dala, the normal world
- In the opening: no dala dala, no Morisho's voice. **The absence of that sound is the horror.**

This is already working. It just needs to be made more deliberate.

|Sound|Scene|What it signals|
|---|---|---|
|_Mia tano mia tano_ (Morisho)|Dala Dala|Normal, the world as it should be|
|Fally Ipupa rhumba from the SUV|Jean Luc appears|The world Djamela wants to enter|
|Boda boda horns greeting Djamela|Jean Luc appears|Male claim, street-level threat|
|One-fingered typewriter|Police station|Institutional rot, slow justice|
|The broken fan|Police station|Heat, pressure, things not working|
|Rain on the tin roof|Rain scene|Intimacy, the world contracted to four walls|
|PK bubble gum / lip gloss smell|Rain scene|Djamela alive, close, real|
|**Silence at the dala dala stop**|Opening|Everything wrong before a word is said|

**Missing sound:** What does the thin envelope sound like when Djamela takes it? Paper. The slightest rustle. That's the sound of justice depleted.

### Whisper Technique Applied
#whisper #finalscene

**Before (shouting):**

> "The officers looked happily at each other as though they had won the lottery. With Djamela's head and back bent down in a stoop as if something demeaning had happened to her."

**After (whispering):**

> "The officers exchanged a look. Djamela came out ahead of Jean Luc. Her back was bent — not the way you bend when you're tired, but the other way. The envelope she carried looked thinner than when it went in."

One factual observation per sentence. No adjectives telling you how to feel. The reader feels it without being told.

## 4. AVOID LABELLING WORDS — SHOW DON'T TELL

### The Banned List (Cleland)

Eliminate or drastically reduce these **passive sensing verbs**:

|Banned|Replace with|
|---|---|
|saw / looked|Put the thing in front of the reader directly|
|heard / listened|Describe the sound and the body's reaction|
|felt|Show what the body does|
|noticed / observed|Cut entirely; just describe what's there|
|realized / understood|Show the moment of realization through action|
|knew|Show what knowing looks like in the body|

### Examples from the Kalemie Draft
#keychanges
#sentences

**Before:**

> "He looked up and saw Djamela's face, but couldn't help himself make a grunting sound."

**After:**

> "He looked up. His jaw moved before any words did. A sound came out — not words."

---

**Before:**

> "I realized how the lake pulls things down whilst engaged in the futile pastime of skimming pebbles."

**After:**

> "Skimming pebbles on the lake surface. A temporal experiment of gravity. The stone skips once, twice — then the lake takes it. That was the first time I understood what D'juma meant."

_(Note: "understood" is used once, deliberately, to mark the moment of realisation — not to describe every moment of perception.)_

---

**Before:**

> "The change in the policeman's face was immediate, as if something changed the air."

**After:**

> "He set down his pen."

_(One action. Silence where there was noise. The pen down is more threatening than any description of his face.)_

## 5. METAPHOR, SIMILE & FIGURATIVE LANGUAGE

### The Rule

A metaphor should:

- Come from the character's **world and vocabulary** (Daudi thinks in physics; Djamela thinks in desire)
- Be **unexpected but inevitable** — surprising, then obviously right
- **Do work** — not decorate, but reveal something that literal language can't

A simile (X _like_ Y) is easier but weaker. A metaphor (X _is_ Y) is harder but more powerful.

### The Kalemie Metaphor System

The story already has a governing metaphor: **the lake**. Everything maps onto it:

```
The lake = entropy / memory / women / death / depth / colonial history
Skimming stones = trying to resist gravity (entropy)
Djamela = the lake (D'juma says so explicitly)
Jean Luc = the thing the lake swallows
The fob key = the thing left behind after the lake takes something
```

**The risk:** if the lake metaphor is named too explicitly (D'juma's speech), it becomes theme stated rather than theme felt. It needs to live in the imagery, not just in dialogue.

### Simile Inventory — What's Working / What Needs Work

|Current simile|Verdict|Suggestion|
|---|---|---|
|"rang in my mind like a school bell"|Weak — generic|_"rang like a call-and-response, the kind you answer before you know why"_|
|"like Leopold never wanting to leave Brazzaville"|Strong — specific, political, exact|Keep|
|"as if he was making his own wishes" (D'juma)|Good — understated|Keep|
|"as though they had won the lottery" (officers)|Cliché|Replace: _"the way men look when they've been given something they didn't earn"_|
|"like a precious tablet with vital inscriptions" (Djamela's phone)|Overwrought|Cut or simplify|
|"the bioluminescent stream of pure everything"|Reaching|_"the thing that happens in deep water when something passes through it"_ — maps to the lake|

### Building New Metaphors from Daudi's World

Daudi is a physics student. His metaphors should come from:

- **Entropy** (disorder as default)
- **Gravity** (things pulled down)
- **Light / refraction** (things that appear differently than they are)
- **Conduction / heat** (the thing that passes between bodies)

**Example:** The moment Djamela takes Jean Luc's number:

> _"She took the piece of paper the way a conductor takes heat — not choosing it, just being the thing it moves through."_

This maps to physics, to Djamela's passivity/agency question, and to the lake (heat transfer in water).

## 6. ONOMATOPOEIA — SOUND WORDS

### The Rule

Onomatopoeia: words that **sound like what they describe**. They create verisimilitude — the reader hears it, not just reads it.

Used well: the reader is _in_ the scene. Used poorly: it reads like a children's book.

### Kalemie Sound-Word Opportunities

The draft currently uses almost no onomatopoeia. Here's where it's missing:

|Moment|Current|Onomatopoeia option|
|---|---|---|
|Morisho managing the dala dala|"He continued using his skill"|_"Mia tano mia tano — the words came out like a rattle, like dice in a cup, worn smooth by use"_|
|Jean Luc's SUV horn|"His horn blazed"|_"his horn cut through — not the bleat of a boda boda, a single low thud of it"_|
|The window of the SUV|"lowered with a swish"|Already onomatopoeic. Keep. _Swish_ vs _clink_ — the contrast between wealth and normal is in those two syllables.|
|One-fingered typewriter|"poking the typewriter"|_"peck... peck... peck — each letter arriving like a reluctant verdict"_|
|Rain on the tin roof|"The droplets seeping through"|_"the rain knocked at the tin roof — not asking, not urgent, just persistent"_|
|The envelope|nothing|_"the paper whispered as she folded it"_|

### The Dala Dala as Sound World

The dala dala is the story's loudest space — and should be its richest in sound. Currently the sound is _Mia tano mia tano_ and horns. What else is in that minivan?

```
The engine: grinding, coughing, complaining into each gear
Passengers: shifting, the creak of the bench, someone's bag hitting the door
The door: the cling (not swish) when someone opens it
Music leaking from a phone: tinny, half a song
School bags thudding on laps
```

All of this is the world Djamela is trying to escape. The SUV is _swish_. The dala dala is _cling_. That contrast is the whole class divide in two syllables — and it's already half in the draft.

> [!AI] Onomatopoeia Questions
> 
> 1. The typewriter scene — rewrite the officer's one-fingered typing using at least two onomatopoeic words that carry the weight of institutional slowness and indifference.
> 2. The rain scene — what does the tin roof sound like specifically? Write two sentences of rain sound that also feel like Daudi's internal state.
> 3. The SUV window: "swish not like the cling" — this contrast is already working. How can we extend it? What else in the dala dala world _clings_, and what else in Jean Luc's world _swishes_?
> 4. The kiss (twenty seconds) — it's described in light and smell. What does it _sound_ like? Write three onomatopoeic options, each using a different register (gentle / electric / devastating).

## 7. THE CONCLUSION — WHAT'S MISSING

### The Problem

The story ends at: _"Djamela held the brown envelope... Making it look more thinner."_

That's a betrayal landing — but it's not an ending. It's the end of the police station scene. What follows is either:

- **The moment Daudi finds out she's dead** (the story circles back to the opening)
- **The moment he understands what he failed to do**
- **Both**

### Options for the Ending

**Option A — Full Circle** Return to the dala dala stop. Daudi stands where we met him in the opening. No dala dala. No Morisho. He finally says her name — the name the boda boda boy wouldn't say. This is the ending _Immaculate_ uses: the coffin, the red Saab, the girl in uniform watching. Small. Precise. Devastating.

**Option B — The Lake** Daudi goes to the lake. He skims a stone. It goes under. D'juma's voice: _"The lake puts things back in order. It remembers everything in its right place."_ The stone is at the bottom. Djamela is at the bottom. The lake has won. Entropy has won. But the lake remembers.
#ending

**Option C — The Fob Key** Daudi finds the fob shaped like a cartoon lion. He goes to the lakeside hotel. He uses the access card. He finds something — or finds nothing, which is worse. The investigation that the police won't do, he does. Alone. With a cartoon lion in his hand.

### The Ending Must Answer

- The narrative question: _what happened to Djamela?_ (not necessarily who did it — the point may be that justice is impossible)
- The thematic question: _does entropy win, or does something hold?_
- Daudi's question: _did he look away when he should have acted?_

> [!AI] Conclusion Questions

> 1. Which ending option (A, B, or C above) best honours the entropy motif without stating it explicitly?
> 2. The story opens with Daudi listing the things pinned to his mind about Djamela. Should the ending return to this list — one item resolved, one item still open?
> 3. D'juma's words: "the lake puts things back to order." Does the ending vindicate or disprove this? The answer determines the story's moral position.
> 4. Write the last paragraph of this story. Daudi's voice. Under 100 words. No emotion words. No labelling verbs. Let the final image do everything.
