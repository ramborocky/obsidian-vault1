[[Scene 1.md]]

Port de Kalemie smelled of colonial coffee and rotten carcasses, hidden under the guise of the mines. The mines that had swallowed children aged twelve to seventeen. Not much older than me.

In our neighborhood they called me "the collector of dead things." Also "the eccentric scientist." Both true.

That morning I couldn't find Djamela. Instead I found a note on my shelf, in her handwriting: *Fix my Commodore 64. Take the motherboard to the fundi.* The same machine her benefactor had given her two days before — broken, important, sitting on my floor, waiting for me to bring it back.

Next to it on the shelf: a hubcap from a Peugeot 504, found by the lakeside hotel. An antelope's skull, found in the bushes near the mine — the same mine that had made ten children of Kalemie disappear. A Sherlock Holmes book, pulled from the rubbish outside a fat white man's compound on Boulevard de Villiers.

The antelope's head was also the emblem on the front of the school minibus. Most mornings it was already at the stop by 6:30. That Monday it wasn't.

A crowd of us stood in uniform outside Mama Lishe's stall instead, waiting, while she sold uji into the cold.

"It's usually here by 6:30. I wonder what happened."
"Hahaha, he should be singing by now."
"Mia tano, mia tano — Mihogoni to Lubuye de Bulevard."

Mama Ntilie joined them.

"Morisho — the conductor — arrested this morning."
"A young girl. Found—"
"In his bus. The front—"
"All bruised. Like something had been done to her."
"Had the uniform. Lubuye de Bulevard."

I went to that school. I heard every word.

Then quiet. Thoughts of Djamela ran through me, fast, in no order. Where was she.

Friday, two days before, Morisho had handed her ten thousand francs.
"Go and buy chips kuku. Our own style — better than the KFC in Kinshasa."
That same week her benefactor sent the Commodore 64. The perfume she wore that day — Shelys, by Remy de Marquis — stayed in my head so long I dreamed it.

I stayed up all night trying to revive the machine. My stomach turned. My saliva ran metallic.
"That's like trying to make ChatGPT have a wet dream," I told her.
"It will never work," she said, and laughed, and said goodnight anyway.

Rain came through the tin roof that night. Drops hit the dry ground and made a smell I can still name. Shelys, on her kanga, the last time she sat on the foam mattress on my floor.

The fat man on Boulevard de Villiers wore that same perfume. So did the man they called Jean Luc. I knew that smell on both of them before I knew either of their names.

Mama Ntilie's gossip always circled back to the fat Belgian — the things he was rumored to do, never said outright, only implied in a sentence left unfinished. Jean Luc brought girls from my class to watch films at his house. The Belgian was always there too. Morisho ran their errands in the mini bus.

*Go buy the kids chocolate.*
*Take the school boys to the mine in the bush.*

I didn't go to school that Monday. I went looking for Djamela instead.
