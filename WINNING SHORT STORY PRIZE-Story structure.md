Port de Kalemie, The air thickly filled with an infusion of colonial coffee and rotten carcasses hidden under the guise of mineral mines, full of corpses aged twelve to seventeen, yet not much younger than me.

In our neighborhood they called me ‘the founder of dead things’.

Besides finding Djamela, I found a motherboard of a satellite phone, probably used by the militia, a hub cap of a BMW X5 on the side of the lakeside hotel and there was a head of an antelope that whilst running away from some hunters had kicked along the path in the bushes. All of which have all been neatly nailed on the rough un painted wall of my bedroom.

These are some of the things that having found about Djamela have been pinned to the walls of my mind.

Her fingernails meticulously painted and polished fingernails every Friday after school, and removed it on Mondays before school.

She had a small faded vaccination found on the lower part of her arm, near the elbow, Vaccinations in our province are usually found on the upper part of the arm. These vaccinations are usually done six months before giving birth.

The smell of a Shalis by Remy Marquis cologne, which is mostly used by Congolese men. The smell was somewhere at the back of my mind which failed to recollect.

In her left pocket nobody seemed to notice a fob shaped like cartoon lion, attached to it was a door access card, like the ones that open up expensive apartment buildings. Like the ones found at the lakeside hotel.

The last posted image was a blurry out of focus selfie, on her phone posted was tagged #blessed. Which was recovered on a backup account. The picture had a man’s hand adorned with a gold ring, rested on her shoulder.

She had written my name, "Daudi," in the dust on the inside of her school locker the week before she left. It was the last thing she erased before walking out for the final time.

D’jamela’s father, D’juma’s words have rang in my mind like a school bell. Although incomprehensible to me at first, yet I’m beginning to understand them as the days go by.

“Some women resemble the lake.” He said.

“Daudi the lake is cold just like some women. Many people come and go, yet it remains the same. Dark, deep and keeps swallowing things down”. He said.

“And knows more secrets than anybody” he said.

I realized how the lake pulls things down whilst engaged in the futile pastime of skimming pebbles on the lake surface. A temporal experiment of gravity. Pleasurable until the 5th or 6th throws, right where boredom starts to sets in and it becomes clear that gravity does exist. But I had yet to experience how a woman would pull you in like the lake.

The thoughts of holding things together was the subject of our physics class on entropy. Yet it made me wonder, whether things were held together or pulled like those stones at the bottom of the lake.

The down part of town is where we resided. Thronged fishermen and boda boda drivers(Motorcycle drivers) looking for an easy catch. A lively population of a mixture of the people of the lake who seem to wonder throughout the countries adjacent to the lake. The lake seemed to accord immunity from any passports or visa restriction, you seldom knew which person came from which country.  Things like someone’s pointed nose, or how short or tall, or if you believed in Jesus didn’t matter to someone who came from Kalemie. Djamela took her mother’s figure, she was tall and beautiful just like her mother.

D’jamela is probably the closest person that I have been next to, besides my mother. Who had cared and caressed me during infancy and childhood.

The last time me and Djamela were together was on a dala dala, a 16 seater Japanese Toyota Mini Hiace. A cheap and affordable favoured transport for the locals. The conductor, a school dropout, called Morisho.

“Mia tano mia tano”. He continued using his skill of managing 30 passengers already in the vehicle, and yet managed to magically lure more in the vehicle.

There would be occasional brushes with teenage school girls, which often led to their skirts stained with a frothy fluid of sorts on the back of their skirts.

“Mototo muzuri, soma shule usije ukawa konda kama mimi” said Morisho.

“Chukua hii elfu mbili ukatumie shule” he said as he took out a bank note from his stack of notes he had neatly arranged in the side of his draws.

Anitha took the note, and hid it inside her bra, as though it was a treasure to be protected. She said nothing but coyly looked at him as a thirteen-year-old school girl would who thought she was being given something precious. Anitha dropped off at the Ecole Secondaire de Kitumaini stop as she usually did before seven thirty, but today she was early as it was her class that cleaning the school yard according to the timetable.

As Anitha got off the dala dala. Her best friend Djamela her followed her with eyes of envy.

“She’s eating chips kuku today.”said Djamela

“The chicken and chips will be digging her own grave.” I whispered to D’jamela. And added “HIV” motioning my eyes to Morisho.

A Toyota Prado SUV parked besides on the traffic lights stop on the double road along Ave de Industirelle.

“Look. Look at them” said D’jamela excitedly in haste, before the lights turn green.

“The way they wear those checkered skirts with their ties and blazers” pointing the school children quietly sitting at the back of the vehicle.

“They go to Monseigneur André Kaseba Catholic School” I told her pointing to their checkered uniform.

“My mother said it’s the most expensive school in town” I said like I knew everything about the school.

I had always thought on how we hardly meet up with them on the dala dalas, and how maybe through entropy or gravity, we might be pulled together with them.

“Once they entered their parent’s SUVs they went to their school, and then entered their red clay roofed mansions guarded by gates and security guards.” Said Morisho.

“Those children are ghosts to you, you will never see them nor talk to them” He said in a clear voice making sure it sank deep in our hearts.

The ghost kids weren’t the subject of envy, especially on my part. But envied the way they were secured and safe living in luxury. Probably eating European things like baguettes, pizzas and burgers, everything European. Not the ugali dagaa from our school.

Anitha was first to turn thirteen, barely three months before D’jamela’s birthday, which we had celebrated a few weeks ago.

My first lesson on how women can pull things happened when D’jamila turned thirteen. As she made her wish and blew the candle.

The watermelon supposedly symbolizing the birthday cake.

“Baba surely you could have gotten me a cake, even a cheap one” said D’jamela

D’jamela’s father worked at the fruit market in Ave de Tanganyique for most of the mornings, before handing the stall to her mother in the evening whilst he attended his traditional African healing customers.

“Watermelons are more, healthier and natural.” said her father D’juma.

“Besides its more African. And we don’t have to celebrate birthdays with cakes, that’s a western thing” he said.

“Look at this way watermelons are sweet like you D’jamela, they symbolize the natural element of sweetness and love ” said her mother trying to convince D’jamela the truth of her father’s words.

“Make a wish and blow” said her father D’juma looking as if he was making his own wishes.

“To be Kim Kardashian, amen” said D’jamela

“What is Kim Kardashian” asked D’juma.

“A social media influencer” said D’jamela’s mother quickly attempting to disregard the name.

With her eyes closed as if culminating a ritual, she blew the candle. The smoke partially rose up gathering into a dark cloud just above shiny corrugated iron roof. It coagulated, like Leopold never wanting to leave Brazaville. She handed me a piece of the red watermelon, and in those moments of devouring its sweet juice, trying to bite off every inch of it, down to its bare green skin. It tasted like it was preserving itself in sugar.

To me it felt as though it was Djamela’s father’s desperate attempt to stave off the inevitable rot of the whole situation.

Later, on the porch, we looked out the at the Lake. With its rusted colonial ships.

"He thinks I want things," she said, in a low and steady voice.

"I want to control things; I want to conquer like the Europeans”

When we were children six or seven years old she told me that the lake was a mirror;

“If you stare long enough, you can see your own death” she told me looking steadily with her directed to the farthest point of the lake.

“Don’t you see Daudi, we can beat them at their own game. No nuclear weapons or school degrees, just followers” she continued.

An empty stomach, the strong smell of the freshly fried dagaa from mama ntilie vendors that brought me back to the moment. Realising we had reached our stop.

“They call them anchovy” said D’jamela. She always made things seem more chic using trendy European words.

I nodded in return.

“You mean the dagaa” pointing out the obvious.

“Shusha konda” I said banging the side of the minivan to signal the driver to stop at the next stop.

“Sawa shemeji” said Morisho, giving Djamela a five-hundred-shilling coin.

“Ya kwako hiyo shemeji yangu” said Morisho.

As we were about to disembark from the white Japanesse vehicle, the boda bodas started greeting us with their horns. It was as though they were awaiting our arrival.

-Mambo mrembo

-Mototo wa mujini.

-Mototo muzuri.

They all said whilst gesturing at Djamela’s beauty.     

Usually my voice would be Squirmish trying to appeal to their unwanted comments, but then something stopped me.

A man, gestured to me from the other side of the tinted glass of a dark black SUV, like the ones the ghost kids from Monseigneur André Kaseba Catholic School. We pretended not to notice him and kept on walking.

His horn blazed in our direction, a different tune to the the boda bodas and dala dalas. The air suddenly changed from roasted dagaa from the fresh lake catch to perfumes from Dubai as he lowered the windows with a swish not like the cling.

The car radio was blazing soft rhumba of Fally Ipupa. The man lowered the window with a quiet swish, “Wow just look at that BMW X5” said Djamela, as we made a U-turn from the school direction and started looking at the beast. In between the beats of Fally Ipupa a sound emitted from the interior;

“Je me appele Jean Luc” said the man

“Tu es du ou ma belle?” said the man in his mixed French and Lingala(Congolese) language.

“Rue Saint Michel” answered Djamela with a different flavor to her voice.

The ghost children came from Rue Saint Michel, they wore the checkered uniforms, not like us, we came from Mihogoni or Manzese but not Rue Saint Michel.

“Mama alisema” I started to say visualizing the a script taught to us at our Sunday school at St. Augustine Parish along Ave Kabanyo Mute.

“We shouldn’t talk to stranger” said Jean Luc, as though he had also learned the same from Sunday school.

As he made the depassement with his black beast on the curb and drew us in to his world of leather upholstery and smells of colognes from Dubai.

“Le contreir mon garcon”

“Directionaire, le consul general de Belgique” he said

Listening to his broken lines of French and street Congolese- Lingala.

“Ask the boda bodas they know the town like a google map.” I pointed out to him

The boda boda drivers are probably school dropouts, yet they’ve studied the art of transporting people to every corner of Kalemie.

“Le petit garcon” he called me. His smelled of cologne and leather upholstery didn’t deter me to continue my policy of not talking to strangers.

Remembering the words of my Sunday school teacher at St. Augustine Parish.

“Many men bring ruin to young school girls and abandonment of dreams”.

D’jamela should have listened to me, but she continued with the dialogue of the man who called himself Jean Luc.

He took out a piece of paper and wrote down his phone number and covered a ten thousand shillings note with it.  

Djamela’s eyes  widened. It wasn’t the ten thousand shillings note that astonished her. It could have been the SUV. The fact that she had wanted to be somebody, like those kids that rode in the SUVs. Like Kim Kardashian.

The look in D’jamela’s eyes reminded of the incident when we lived near Marche le Lubuye. The time shared a moment in the rain. A cordial event compared to what was happening now. It had a new vibe to it, like that of the Kim Kardashian lookalike D’jamela wished to be.

“Je me appele?” he asked D’jamela

“Chantal” she said as her eyes widened with her body emitting a new frequency, the new signature vibe.

Then the sudden flashover of that rainy night that shorted out my mindfulness for twenty seconds came back to me. That was the only moment I had seen her eyes widen like that.

The ironic moment of des’vu, yet it memorable, and even enjoyable. Devoid of the sounds of boda boda and dala dala horns, that sweet aroma of damp clothing infused with perfumed khangas filled the air of my lowly dwellings. It felt as if all life existed in the confines of those four walls of baked bricks. The half soaked dark brown foam mattress laid out on the floor. The two feet sealed window provided little if no lighting. Yet D’jamela shone with an aura that was unforgettable, as she stood at me bedroom door running from the rain.

The droplets seeping through the tin roof bounced off her shoulders. The rest collected itself in the bucket that I meretriciously targeted.

“Come in D’jamela” I said to her cautioning her not to kick the buckets. I motioned her to the only sensible part of the room, a dry patch of the brown mattress.

“Do you understand entropy?” she posed the question looking at the papers spread out on the mattress along with the half torn physics book spread out on the grey sheets that covered the mattress. The sheets devoid of the fresh smell of detergent and smeared with patches of evidence of dreams and nightmares.

“It’s a natural state” trying to sound like my everyday vocabulary.

“Order requires energy; disorder is it’s default.” I told her.

“It’s like holding on to something, and once unbearable you have to let go” said Djamela

“My father says the Lake is the opposite. He says it puts things back to order. It remembers everything in its right place.” She continued.

“Your father sounds like a wise man, we could learn a lot from him.” I said to her looking for signs of a reaction. I saw none.

“It’s a fusion of Hydrogen and Oxygen. It doesn’t have any intelligence” continuing the usage of my new found vocabulary.

Moments of pauses passed by. Then it came without warning, a surprise that scattered notes on entropy and the physics book that was also placed on the mattress.

She knelt down on the mattress, sat next to me with her feet touching a wet patch of floor now laminated with droplets of rain that bounced off the edge of the bucket.

Her eyes flowed, yet restful and un attentive to everything but the space between us.

“Then what do you want to remember, Mr. Scientist? - Encrypted data, Binary codes? or just a colourful moment without meaning” she said

Sitting there in those few moments, seemed like a dream that should never have ended. The edge of my knee had accidently touched her left hand thumb that supported the weight of her body. My mind told me that she felt it and had wanted to me to do something. A soft touch, a tender word.

Not a word came out of my mouth but silence.

Tell her to go home it was late. This isn’t right you shouldn’t be here. Do you want me to touch you?.

With all the pre meditation and calculations. Nothing happened except motionless head chatter.

Then that unpredicted glow from her eyes happened, the biolumiscent stream of pure everything.

The air along with the perfumed wet kangas now had an added aura of a minty after smell of PK bubble gum and lip gloss, with the darkness of the room stared at us. Lost in the stream of subliminal moment things dictating themselves as if they had life in them.

Tangent and sweet it felt at first. Then perfect and seamless, as if awaiting a timestamp. Right there in the midst of inconsistent heartbeats.

It took only Twenty seconds.

 I grasped for air.

Things went back to normal, with a heart-breaking realization of the Des ‘vu that took place.

She pulled away. Her breath, short and sharp. The glow faded from her eyes, replaced by a sudden, vulnerable uncertainty.

She looked at me as if she had revealed the secret backdoor to the universe.

“See?” she whispered, her voice barely audible.

"Not everything falls apart”

She was wrong, of course. Entropy always wins. But in that moment. In the dusty rainy moment of obliviousness, it felt as though she was right.

 It was the most beautiful and catastrophic twenty seconds of my life.

Then entered Jean Luq in our lives. Having reported my stolen bike once at the police station last week, the tedious ritual of filing a complaint was still alive in my mind. It was only because Djamela didn’t want to go alone, especially at Ave de Bobozo police station, fearing that she might not get out of there quickly. It was Djamela that was next to me, but in reality Chantal had already taken over her, with a new pair of un naturally blue jeans and a blouse with a designer logo that looked like a blatant counterfeit. In truth she was trying to be one of them. She even had an air of superiority she had herself disguised in. Her back arched upwards and her chin high. Chantal looked more ruthless comapered to D’jamela, Although a sence of disappointment had set in her by the looks of her eye and tired face. She had lost most of her pre-conceived hopes a rags to riches life that Jean Luc was to give her. Shad wanted so much to be like the ghost kids those of Rue de Michel.  ‘ A notre belle parrie’.

Throngs of people filled police station with sighs of delayed justice. The smell of dusty benches worn out by the sliding buttocks of motionless police due process. The biodegradable oudor mixed with tiredness of the officers coming off the shift almost crippled the atmosphere.

Other appellants trying to state their cases.

“He didn’t it was stolen, he bought it off the internet legitimately” a mother pleading on behalf of his sixteen-year-old boy who was in possession of a stolen phone.

Another woman, who was wearing a tight black dress, whose mascara was being washed by fake tears, stressing a point to another officer in the background.

“Its not prostitution, its escort services, we even have cards with the company number” she was pleaded with wet alluring eyes to the officer.

Then came our turn. The policeman a young man that looked as though he hadn’t had much experience with a Qwerty keyboard. He was one handedly poking the typewriter, whilst listening to the row of people lined up. He attended them at the same rate as the typing the typing speed.

Djamela Looked at me, and motioned me to go forward.

“So why are you talking if she’s the one who’s reporting” he said.

The officer looked at the picture of Jean Luc holding D’jamela in his arms behind a G- Wagon Mercedes Benz.

“We deal with hard, evidence. Not AI or phatom ghosts” he said to D’jamela.

Having exhausted all the pleading to the officer and witnessing his blatant un arousal of the situation, she leaned towards him, but backed away a bit after flinching at the smell of his onion – whiskey breath.

“My menstrual cycle. There’s a latency of more than a month” she said.

The thought of someone like Jean Luc being jailed was a blessing in disguise. It made me think of how D’jamela would be free from his evil clutches.

A cold acidic taste entered my mouth. My blood pressure had already risen. The broken fan above didn’t make it easier a things began to heat up.

The officers became more relaxed and took in Jean Luc and Djamela to a room at the back of the station.
