# Content tells

What the text *says* that gives it away. These are the expensive ones to fix, because the fix
is usually a fact you have to go get.

## 1. Significance and legacy inflation

**Watch for:** stands as / serves as a testament / a reminder that / played a
crucial|pivotal|vital|key role / underscores the importance of / reflects a broader / marking
a turning point / setting the stage for / represents a shift / evolving landscape / indelible
mark / deeply rooted / focal point / symbolizing its enduring

The model inflates the subject by asserting that arbitrary aspects of it represent something
larger. It does this for anything — a species, a mall, a census figure, a town's etymology.
Sometimes it hedges first ("while modest in scale") and then does it anyway.

Sub-patterns:

- **Situating in "debates."** The subject "has participated in public discussions about" or
  "sits at the center of ongoing debates surrounding" — usually with no debate named.
- **Biology.** Over-emphasis on ecosystem role, conservation status, and "ongoing research and
  preservation efforts," even where the status is unlisted and no such efforts exist.
- **Places.** Every town is "strategically located," "steeped in history," and a "hub."
- **Mundane data.** Population and etymology sections that end by explaining what the number
  means for the region's identity.

**Fix:** delete. Significance that is real is already demonstrated by the facts. If the
paragraph would leave a hole, the hole wants a fact, not a claim.

## 2. Superficial analysis (the participial tail)

**Watch for:** a sentence ending with `, ` + an `-ing` verb: highlighting, underscoring,
emphasizing, ensuring, reflecting, symbolizing, contributing to, cultivating, fostering,
encompassing, enhancing, showcasing, solidifying, cementing, allowing, helping to, further
establishing, making it one of

This is the single highest-yield tell. The model finishes a factual clause, then bolts on an
opinion about what the fact means, using a comma and a present participle. Newer models with
web search will attach the opinion to a real named source that never said it.

> The bridge opened in 1957, connecting the two counties and fostering economic growth
> throughout the region.

Everything after "1957" is unsourced editorializing. Cut at the comma.

**Fix:** delete the clause. If the claim in it matters, it deserves its own sentence with
evidence.

## 3. Promotional tone

**Watch for:** boasts a / vibrant / rich / profound / renowned / groundbreaking / nestled /
in the heart of / diverse array / a commitment to / natural beauty / showcasing / exemplifies
/ state-of-the-art / seamless / world-class

Even when told to be neutral, the model drifts into travel-brochure or press-release register.
Watch especially for edits *claiming* to remove promotional tone that add it. Older models
were blatantly superlative; newer ones are subtler — warm rather than loud.

**Fix:** neutral verb, concrete noun. "Nestled in the heart of the valley" → "in the valley."

## 4. Vague attribution and inflated consensus

**Watch for:** experts argue / observers have noted / critics have cited / industry reports /
studies suggest / scholars note / it is widely regarded as / many consider / several sources /
has been described as / such as (before a suspiciously complete list)

Two failures at once: an opinion attributed to no one in particular, and one or two sources
inflated into a field. Also: lists presented as illustrative ("such as X, Y, and Z") when the
source lists exactly those three and implies no others.

**Fix:** name the person and cite them, or state the claim as fact if it is one, or cut it.
"Widely regarded as" is almost never salvageable.

## 5. Notability and coverage padding

**Watch for:** independent coverage / national media outlets / trade publications / profiled
in / written by a leading expert / maintains an active social media presence / has been
featured in

The model tries to prove a subject matters by listing the *kinds* of places that covered it,
rather than saying anything those places reported. Strongly associated with 2025-and-later
models. "Maintains an active social media presence" is close to a signature phrase.

**Fix:** replace metacoverage with content. Not "was profiled in several trade publications"
but what the profile said.

## 6. The "challenges and future" coda

**Watch for:** a closing section titled `Challenges`, `Challenges and Legacy`, `Future
Prospects`, `Future Outlook`, or `Awards and Recognition`; a paragraph opening `Despite its
[positive adjective], X faces several challenges...` and closing `Despite these challenges...`

A rigid outline habit: after the body, one paragraph of generic difficulties (funding,
infrastructure, awareness), then a hopeful turn about ongoing initiatives. Content-free in
both halves.

Note this is about the **formula**, not about mentioning difficulties. Real problems,
specifically described and sourced, are fine.

**Fix:** delete the section. Fold any real, sourced problem into the relevant body section.

## 7. Definitional leads

**Watch for:** a first sentence that treats a descriptive title as a proper noun or a term —
`"List of X" is a curated compilation of...`, `Catchment area refers to...`

The model defines the *title* instead of introducing the *subject*. `refers to` is the flag.

**Fix:** write about the thing, not the phrase. Or open somewhere other than a definition.
