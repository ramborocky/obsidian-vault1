Preface

A model is something you nod at. A question is something you can ask on a Tuesday.

The previous version of this work gave you five named mental models. Your two documents argued against that form, and they were right. Ferriss’s seventeen are not concepts — each is a question stamped to the moment it entered his life, told through what actually happened, with real numbers in it. So the models were retired and these were built instead. Nine come from your documents. Four came out of research. Four came out of your vault’s own record of the last six weeks, which turns out to be the most revealing source of the three.

Question 17 is the only one that has to be answered before tomorrow. The other sixteen are worthless without it.

What am I doing instead?

22 July 2026Your vault

The ACPA status question went onto the dashboard as blocker #1. It needed one phone call. Six weeks later it had become easier — the answer sits on your MEMS profile now, three fields, readable while logged in — and it still had not been done.

The instinct is to ask why. That question has never once produced an answer that changed behaviour, because it invites a story and you will write a flattering one. Ask the other one instead. In those six weeks, what did I do?

A nine-axis judging rubric. Six craft knowledge bases. An empirical campaign measuring real prize winners and correcting the rubric against them. Months of genuinely skilled work, all of it real.

Which is the point. Avoidance does not look like idleness. It looks like your best work. Relief arrives the instant you turn toward the safe task, every time, with zero delay — nothing you deliberately try to build will ever be reinforced that cleanly. So the substitute gets stronger and more impressive each round, and the alibi gets better.

The rubric cannot tell you that you can’t write. The MEMS profile can tell you that you’re further from qualified than you hoped. That is the whole selection criterion, and it operates below the level of decision.

The questionNot why haven’t I done it, but what did I do instead, and what verdict does that thing protect me from? The diagnostic that settles it: the blocker shrank from a call to a login and still didn’t move. If effort were the obstacle, halving it would have worked.

Who pays, out of what money, instead of what?

31 August 2026The Kippa post-mortem

Researching whether to build bookkeeping training for mama ntilie and small traders, I went looking for whoever had tried it. Kippa, in Nigeria, had.

$14M+raised, Target Global and others
500,000small businesses using it
0users who can now retrieve their own records

Half a million people used it. It still died. That finding has a precise shape: adoption is not demand. People will happily accept something useful with no intention of paying, their enthusiasm is real, and it is not a business.

Three parts, each answerable in one sentence. Who writes the cheque — a named role, not a category. Out of which budget line — money already allocated, because new budget is ten times harder than reallocated budget. Instead of what — what do they stop buying to buy you?

The practice passes: the partner at a four-person US firm, from a bookkeeping-outsourcing line running $400–800 a month, instead of the Manila provider billing $10–25 an hour. Three sentences, all specific, no scale required. The training idea has no answer to the third, no budget for the second, and a customer with a few thousand shillings of daily margin for the first.

The questionIf you cannot name all three, you have a project, not a business — however many people would use it free, and however right the underlying insight is.

What can’t be copied here?

2008, revised 2026Kevin Kelly · your document

Copies used to be hard, so we built a legal system around who may make them and creators lived by selling them. The internet made copies free. AI made competent variations free — words, images, code, advice, in seconds.

When copies are super abundant, they become worthless, and stuff which can’t be copied becomes scarce and valuable.

Kelly names eight generatives — qualities that must be generated in place, in time, in relationship, and cannot be cloned, faked, stored or banked: immediacy, personalization, interpretation, authenticity, accessibility, embodiment, patronage, findability. Trust is the archetype. You cannot download it.

Now apply it. The bookkeeping output is a copy. Reconciliations, schedules, a set of management accounts — AI produces competent versions in seconds and that price is heading to zero. A plan to sell the output has a clock on it.

GenerativeIn the practice
InterpretationWhat the numbers mean and what to do — the core sale
AuthenticityA signed, warranted, professionally liable set of accounts
ImmediacyClose on day five instead of day eighteen
PersonalizationFitted to their accounts and their board, iteratively
The questionYou are not selling bookkeeping. Bookkeeping is now the free copy. You are selling interpretation with a signature on it, delivered faster than anyone else can manage. That sentence sets the price and writes the offer.

What is my Red Hat?

Kelly, generative #3

An old joke that turned out not to be one: software, free; the software manual, $10,000. Red Hat has lived for twenty-five years selling support and consultation for code anyone can download for nothing.

Kelly’s second example is sharper. Sequencing your DNA will soon be free — pharmaceutical companies may eventually pay to do it. The interpretation of what it means will be expensive.

The free codeThe manual
Reconciliations, schedules, journalsWhich of your five customers is quietly unprofitable
A set of management accountsWhy margin fell three points and what to change
A filed returnWhether the business survives the loan it’s about to take

Nearly every accountant sells the left column and gives the right one away — the insight arrives as an aside in the last two minutes of the call, unbilled, because it took two minutes. Which is exactly backwards: those two minutes are the only part a machine cannot yet replace, and the eight hours are the part already collapsing to zero.

The questionWhat am I giving away in the last two minutes of the call — and what happens if I put it at the top of the invoice instead?

Who has to sign this, and can it be me?

Kelly #4Plus a finding that changes the sequence

Kelly calls it Authenticity — the artist’s stamp, the signature that makes one copy worth more than an identical one. In 2026 that stopped being a metaphor about art and became a funded category.

$1.15BBasis valuation, Series B led by Accel
$85MModus — and it buys accounting firms
$1.5BGeneral Catalyst’s fund for AI services roll-ups
Agents do the work. A certified accountant signs off.

So the scarce asset is not the ability to do the work. It is being allowed to stand behind it. Which reframes something sitting in your vault under the word admin: ACPA-PP is the only part of this business that cannot be competed away by anyone with the same tools and faster internet.

And then the sting. NBAA requires three years under a supervising mentor, with the logbook signed by mentor and employer. The two central moves — go independent for cash, get credentialed for the moat — may be in direct conflict, and nobody had checked. That doesn’t kill the plan; it changes the sequence, and all three resolutions turn on facts one email can settle.

The questionIn any market where machines do the production, find the act that legally requires a named human — then ask whether that human can be you, and what it costs to become them.

What would I have to believe for this to be a bad idea?

The inversion

When the mama ntilie idea arrived, my first instinct was to look for reasons it works. That instinct is worthless — enthusiasm supplies evidence for anything, and search engines are very good at finding support for a conclusion already reached.

Inverted, the question wrote its own research plan in four lines, and all four came back positive for disaster in a single afternoon: someone had already tried it at scale and failed; the training was already free from a telco, a donor and a bank; a funded competitor was three miles away with a lending licence; and the customer had a few thousand shillings of daily margin.

Not one of those would have surfaced from asking whether the idea was good, because each is a reason to stop, and a search for validation does not return reasons to stop.

The second use matters more: run it on the plan you have chosen. What would make the practice a disaster? The credential turns out to need an employer. Ask, and there it was.

The questionWhat would have to be true for this to fail badly — and which of those can I check this week for the price of an afternoon? The reason nobody does it is emotional: you have to want the answer more than you want the idea.

What did the last person who tried this find out?

Kippa and Ramani

Before market research, before a business plan, before a spreadsheet: find the corpse and read the autopsy. Almost every idea you have has been attempted by someone better funded. That is a gift — they paid for the experiment and the results are public.

Three searches, in order. The idea plus “shut down” or “layoffs” — failures get written up more honestly than successes. The idea plus “raises”, restricted to your region — Ramani.io turned out to be in Dar with $34M and a Bank of Tanzania lending licence. Who gives this away free, and who funds them — if a telco or donor is doing it for customer acquisition, there is no price above zero.

None of the three asks whether the idea is good. They ask what already happened. And the method points forward too: when you go looking for corpses and find a category being funded instead — Basis, Modus, a solo firm running 37 engagements with no staff — that is the strongest signal available.

The questionWho has already run my experiment, what did it cost them, and what did they learn that I can have for free? The corollary is uncomfortable: six weeks of craft apparatus, and not one hour spent asking whether anyone had tried the income plan before.

What am I not allowed to do?

31 August 2026The unknown-unknowns search

This question pays only if asked before you start, which is why almost nobody asks it. It produces no plan and no revenue — just a list of walls you would otherwise hit at speed in month nine. Asked once, it returned four things nobody here had considered.

The logbook needs a mentor. Self-employment may not count toward ACPA. US tax data offshore is a criminal statute — IRC §7216, up to a year’s imprisonment, standalone written consent required for disclosure outside the United States. AI on client data has to be named — “AI tools we may use from time to time” fails the 2026 consent bar; letters are expected to name the specific tools with data-processing terms. Professional services must VAT-register regardless of turnover in Tanzania, though exported services are zero-rated.

None is fatal, all are cheap to handle at the start, and the third is not even a cost. A supplier who arrives with §7216 already understood and a named-tools AI clause already written is a different category from those who have never heard of either. The compliance answer is the sales pitch.

The questionWhat are the rules of this game I would not know to look up? Stare at the asymmetry: this vault held a nine-axis rubric for judging a short story and nothing at all on whether its own income plan was legal, licensed or loggable.

Can I Shepardize this?

Martine RothblattYour document

Her youngest daughter was diagnosed with pulmonary arterial hypertension. No approved medicines, all patients dead within two to three years. Rothblatt was a lawyer running SiriusXM who hadn’t taken a life-science course since high school.

She read in the hospital library at night. Most of it she didn’t understand, so she looked words up, and when a paper was too hard she went backwards — college anatomy texts, then high-school ones — until something explained it, then climbed back up. And she used one technique from law school called Shepardizing: read every reference a paper cites, then every reference in those, until three or four levels down the citations start circling back on themselves. That is the point of diminishing returns, and it means the field is mapped.

She found a molecule that had failed its heart-failure trial but lowered pressure between lung and heart while leaving the rest of the body alone. She was told no, asked what it would take, built a company to become that, got fifteen executives to sign, and paid $25,000 plus 10%. It has since paid Glaxo Wellcome over a billion in royalties.

The questionWhere are the citations, and how far down before they circle back? Two weeks, not two years, to learn a client niche well enough to hold a conversation with a buyer.

What’s the least crowded channel?

Ferriss, December 2006Your document

Book finished, launch in April, no idea what to do. He asked a dozen bestselling authors what they’d spend on again and heard one word: blogs. Then the question that actually mattered — how is everyone currently trying to reach bloggers? Email first, phone second. Those were his strengths, and he deliberately didn’t use them. He went to CES, never walked in the front door, and parked in the blogger lounge. Better odds as one of five people in a room than one email out of five hundred.

Small US and UK firms that outsource are pitched constantly, by cold email, by Indian and Filipino providers. Email is the crowded channel — and it is crowded because it is easy.

Uncrowded, cheapest first: the groups where firm owners complain to each other about their current provider (answer questions for months, never pitch); their podcasts, which are permanently short of guests with something specific to say — and §7216 plus named-tools AI clauses is something specific; the offshore providers themselves, who have overflow; and one genuinely useful public artefact aimed at exactly that reader.

The questionHow is everyone else reaching this buyer — and what is the room with five people in it instead of the inbox with five hundred? Go as a participant, not a seller.

If I could only work two hours a week on this?

Ferriss, 2004Your document

He is explicit that the soft version fails. How can I not be a bottleneck? invites a list. The extreme version is the useful one: gun to your head, two hours a week, what survives?

He pairs it with the email that did most of the work — from now on, if it involves less than $100, decide it yourself and write down what you did. Then $500. Then $1,000. The reviews went weekly, monthly, quarterly, then never.

To get huge, good things done, you need to be okay with letting the small, bad things happen. And people’s IQs seem to double as soon as you give them responsibility and indicate that you trust them.

For a one-person practice with AI doing production, the answer is the whole operating design, in order of scarcity: sell — the asks, the only thing that makes a client; sign — review and stand behind it, the part that legally cannot be delegated; handle the exception — the transaction nobody has a rule for yet. Everything else belongs to the machine and a written procedure.

The questionWith two hours, what would I keep? Any week where production work displaces selling is a week the practice shrank while feeling busy.

Am I hunting antelope or field mice?

FerrissYour document

A lion can spend all day catching field mice and starve doing it — each mouse costs more energy than it returns. The trap is that mice are available. Always there, always catchable, and each one produces a small genuine hit of accomplishment. A day of mice feels full.

Field miceAntelope
Another craft base, another rubric axisOne paid client
Reorganising the vaultThe three MEMS fields
Reading a seventh book on the listThe NBAA mentor question
Tuning the offer wording againTen asks sent

Four clients at $350 a month covers the entire household-and-debt figure of $1,228. Four. There are perhaps a dozen genuinely antelope-shaped acts available to you this year. The left column is not fake work — that is what makes it dangerous.

And the sharper version is about tolerance for missing. A lion hunting antelope fails most days; mice never fail. A preference for mice is usually a preference for a guaranteed small win over a probable miss — the same circuit as question 1, in a productivity costume.

The questionAsked on Friday: which of the things I did this week could have produced a client, a fact, or a reader? If none, it was a week of mice, however full it felt.

Do I need to make it back the way I lost it?

Ferriss, and Lori GottliebYour document

Gottlieb left entertainment for medical school. Then, two years and two years of tuition into Stanford, she left to be a freelance journalist. Everyone told her to just finish, get the degree, tough out two more years. She had the loans already. She had paid.

She left anyway, and gave a specific reason: life has a 100% mortality rate. Why spend two more years on a path she knew she wasn’t taking, to justify the two already spent?

The mind refuses this, because writing off the past feels like admitting it was wasted — so it returns to the same table to win it back, and the losses compound. The version that applies here is not that the writing was wrong. It is which table it has to be won back at.

Years of craft are real skill and none of it is wasted. But the instinct is that the return must arrive as a prize, from a panel, in the form the years were spent in — and £15,000 across 7,806 entries is £1.92 of expected value per entry. The same craft pays at a different table: an accountant who writes clean English is rare and priceable, interpretation is a writing skill before it is a numerical one, and the weekly publish that builds a practice is prose.

The questionAm I trying to win this back at the table where I lost it — and is that the table with the money on it? Gottlieb’s own summary: she doesn’t feel she had four careers, but one, approached from different directions.

What has to die for this to live?

Steve YoungYour document

The day after he retired, he had been known worldwide for one thing and the next morning it was gone.

Everyone needs to treat it like a death — to mourn it and go through all the steps of mourning it and burying it, and actually have it as a place you can keep referring to, almost like a grave site. Because otherwise you carry it around and you never transition.

Staubach’s advice to him was one word: run. Run where? Just run away. The game will never leave you, but you need to leave it.

Read carefully, this is not about giving anything up. Young keeps referring to the grave site — the thing is buried and it is still there, and it can be visited. What dies is not the football. It is the primacy: the arrangement where one thing is the answer to who you are and everything else is a hobby.

Applied without softening: the identity to be buried is not writer. It is “writer, and one day the writing will pay, and until then the accounting is admin.” That arrangement has been running the allocation. Suleika Jaouad supplies the other half — moving on is a myth; you learn to move forward with it, integrated — and notes we have rituals for birth, marriage and death and none for the transitions between, so she invented her own.

The questionWhat arrangement has to be buried, properly and with some ceremony, for the new thing to have room? Nothing is given up. What ends is the ordering.

Is this making me sick, and who am I staying for?

Samin NosratYour document

She sold fresh pasta out of a friend’s empty commercial kitchen. It worked immediately, grew into an online marketplace, tens of thousands on the mailing list, press attention, other unemployed cooks selling alongside her. And it was destroying her. “All of a sudden I’m managing 9,000 business licenses and permits and health department and taxes and sales tax and vendors.” All she had wanted was to make pasta.

Two details matter. Why she stayed too long: other people’s income depended on it — the strongest glue there is, and not the same thing as the business being right. What got her out: therapy. Not a spreadsheet.

You cannot spreadsheet your way out of everything, nor onto everything.

That is a genuine correction to almost everything else in this booklet, which is arithmetic — expected values, price ladders, four clients at $350. The arithmetic is right and it is not sufficient. A plan that is optimal on paper and makes you ill has failed, and it will fail slowly enough that you will blame yourself rather than the plan.

Her resolution is the practical part: now that she knows what a bad arrangement feels like in the body, she knows what to ask for in advance, and she takes work that is temporary so the exit is built in before the entry.

The questionIs this making me sick — and if I’m staying because others depend on it, is that a reason or a rationalisation? Fifty clients at $150 is a different life from twenty at $400, and the arithmetic barely distinguishes them.

What would this look like if it were easy?

FerrissYour document

The mind’s default when a goal is important is to make the plan proportionally elaborate. The question interrupts that with an absurd premise: suppose this were easy.

Getting out of debt. Elaborate: build an app for informal traders, raise money, get a lending licence, scale. Easy: four clients at $350 a month. The gap between those two plans is not effort — it is a thousand times the risk.

Learning a niche. Elaborate: a course, a certification, six months. Easy: read three practitioners and everything they cite, three levels down, in two weeks. Getting the credential moving. Elaborate: a project plan across seven tasks. Easy: log in, read three fields, send one email. Pricing. Elaborate: market analysis. Easy: say $350 without flinching and see what happens.

Notice what the easy versions share. They are smaller, sooner, and more exposed — while the elaborate versions are bigger, later and safer. That is not a coincidence. Complexity is often avoidance with a project plan attached.

The questionIf this were easy, what would it be? Then ask what it costs to just do that version first. Not because easy is always right — because the elaborate version should have to win against it, and usually it can’t.

What’s the smallest exposure I can take today?

31 August 2026The only one due before tomorrow

Everything here converges on one finding: the obstacle was never ability, information, tooling or time. It is that the exposing act goes second, and the safe act — real, good, defensible work — goes first and absorbs the week.

Insight does not fix this. The clinical finding is unusually blunt: understanding why you avoid something has close to zero effect on whether you keep avoiding it. Only doing it, while still afraid, and letting the fear decay, works. Which means the sixteen questions before this are worthless on their own, and this booklet is the most sophisticated avoidance device yet built if it is read and nothing follows.

  1. Log into MEMS. Read three fields. Grade, registration date, years logged. Ten minutes, cannot be done wrong.
  2. Send NBAA one email. Does self-employed practice count toward the logbook, and can the mentor be someone other than my employer? It may reorder the entire plan.
  3. Write the offer in one sentence and send it to one person who might buy it. Not ten. One.

Each is small. Each returns something reality decides rather than something you decide — and that is the only property that matters. An act that cannot tell you you’re wrong has not exposed you to anything.

The question, every morningWhat is the smallest thing I can do today that puts a verdict outside my own control? Scored weekly: zero such acts is a week of hiding, whatever it produced. One is a working week. Three is a good one.
