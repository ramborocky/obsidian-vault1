# THE SECOND CURRICULUM
### Judgement · Strategy · Persuasion · Adaptation

Part of [[Operating Manual]]. Sixteen modules, one a week, four habits that outlast them.

> [!info] Why this exists
> School was optimised for retrieving content, alone, under timed conditions, graded on the answer and never on the confidence attached to it. That produces people who are good at exams. Every module below is built the other way: a short lesson, the reason it matters, and a drill with a real signal attached — because repetition against visible feedback is the only thing that reliably moves any of these.

---

## TRACK I · JUDGEMENT
*Aim: to say how sure you are, and be right about how sure you are.*

**I.1 · Put a number on it.** Convert beliefs to percentages with a resolution date; score them when they resolve.
*Why:* fifteen years of grading answers and never the confidence attached left you with no idea whether your certainty means anything. Memory keeps the hits and discards the misses. Calibration improves in weeks — but only once written down.
**Drill:** forecast log. Twenty predictions a month. At month end, take everything called 70% and count what fraction happened.

**I.2 · Take the outside view first.** Find the reference class before estimating from inside.
*Why:* your inside view is a story, and stories are built for coherence, not accuracy. The reference class is boring, unflattering and much more often right.
**Drill:** two lines at the top of every project file — the reference class, and its median outcome.

**I.3 · Write the kill criteria before you start.** Plus a pre-mortem: assume it failed, write how.
*Why:* sunk cost is not defeated by willpower afterwards. Once you've paid in months you become the worst available judge of your own project. Only the version of you who hasn't started can write the rule.
**Drill:** every commitment over a month gets one line — *I stop if, by [date], [observable thing] hasn't happened.*

**I.4 · You are a noisy instrument.** The same person judging the same case on different days disagrees with themselves.
*Why:* the instrument drifts with sleep, hunger and money-stress. This is most of why checklists beat expert intuition — the rule doesn't have a bad morning.
**Drill:** decide something important twice, a week apart, without looking at the first answer.

## TRACK II · STRATEGY
*Aim: to put effort where it changes the outcome, which is almost never where it feels productive.*

**II.1 · Find the constraint; ignore everything else.** One thing limits output; work elsewhere returns zero.
*Why:* effort flows to the parts that respond, and the constraint is by definition the part that doesn't. Most stalled projects are worked everywhere except the one place.
**Drill:** every Monday, one written line — *what is actually holding this up?* Unchanged three weeks running means avoidance.

**II.2 · Choose by payoff shape, not by odds.** Downside, upside, cost in hours, number of repeats.
*Why:* school rewarded a high hit rate on questions with known answers, which trains a reflex against exactly the bets worth taking. And price the "free" thing in hours — a no-fee entry eating 25 hours a week is expensive, and the zero is what hides it.
**Drill:** four lines before any commitment. If you can only run it once, it is not a bet, it is a bid.

**II.3 · Sort by reversibility, then set the speed.** Two-way doors fast at 70% information; one-way doors slowly.
*Why:* nearly everyone spends the deliberation budget backwards — agonising over the reversible, rushing the permanent.
**Drill:** label every open decision R or I. Clear the Rs before Friday.

**II.4 · Leverage, and the rare pair.** Output = judgement × leverage. The permissionless kinds — writing, code, media, systems — keep working after you stop.
*Why:* school implied reward scales with effort because inside school it did. A rare *combination* of two ordinary skills beats high rank in either, and numbers-plus-clean-English is a category with few occupants.
**Drill:** one durable asset a month. Test: does it still work in a month untouched?

## TRACK III · PERSUASION
*Aim: to get one named person to do one specific thing.*

**III.1 · Model the other mind before you open yours.** Steelman first.
*Why:* once you know something you cannot imagine not knowing it, so you answer questions nobody asked. Most failed persuasion is a failure of imagination, not of evidence. Contradiction hardens people; questions move them.
**Drill:** three unsent lines before any message that matters — what they believe, what they fear, what they get if they say yes.

**III.2 · Lead with the point.** Claim, then reasons, then evidence.
*Why:* readers grant you seconds; a buried point reads as no point. School taught the opposite because an examiner is paid to reach the end and nobody else is. A message you can't compress to one opening sentence is one you haven't finished thinking.
**Drill:** two weeks where every email opens with the ask. Watch reply times.

**III.3 · Concrete beats abstract, every time.** Not "improve efficiency" but "cut the invoice run from three days to four hours."
*Why:* abstraction is where trust dies, because it can't be checked. You already know this law from fiction — *objects carry emotion* — and it is the same law in a proposal. Almost nobody transfers it across.
**Drill:** mark every abstract noun in a draft; replace half with something you could photograph.

**III.4 · The ask, and the silence after it.** Explicit, specific, singular. Name the number, then stop talking.
*Why:* the highest-return communication act there is, and the one people structurally avoid — burying it in paragraph four, pre-conceding, filling the pause with a discount nobody asked for. Prices and loan terms are far more negotiable than they appear; volume is the only fix for inexperience.
**Drill:** one real ask per week, logged with the answer.

## TRACK IV · ADAPTATION
*Aim: to improve faster than conditions change, and still be standing when it compounds.*

**IV.1 · Engineer the feedback loop.** Fast, specific, honest — build it before the practice starts.
*Why:* the master module. Rate of improvement is set by loop length more than talent or hours. Fifteen years of school ran one loop per term and graded content only.
**Drill:** for each skill, write the signal and its latency. Slower than two weeks needs a proxy.

**IV.2 · Small bets, fast kills.** Sample widely when uncertain; commit hard when something works.
*Why:* you cannot reason your way to what a client or reader wants — that information exists only outside your head. The real cost of testing is lower than you estimate, because what you're pricing in is the embarrassment of a public no.
**Drill:** three cheap tests running at all times, each killable in a week.

**IV.3 · Survive first.** No single points of failure — income, health, relationships, backups.
*Why:* compounding requires staying in the game; a 50% loss needs a 100% gain to undo. Fragility is invisible until it isn't.
**Drill:** quarterly, list what breaks you if it vanishes. Build a second of the top item.

**IV.4 · Learn in public.** Publish while learning, same place every time.
*Why:* three jobs at once — the clarity an audience forces, a feedback loop, and distribution, which is the thing that decides whether work reaches anyone at all.
**Drill:** one public artefact a week. Consistency of place beats quality of piece for six months.

## The running order

Not track by track. Calibration and feedback loops come first because they are the instruments everything else is measured with, and the forecast log needs weeks of running before it says anything.

| Wk | Module | What you actually do |
| --: | --- | --- |
| 01 | I.1 | Open the forecast log. Twenty predictions before Sunday |
| 02 | IV.1 | Name the signal and latency for every skill you claim to be building |
| 03 | II.1 | Write the constraint. Do nothing else that week |
| 04 | III.2 | Every email opens with the ask. Track reply times |
| 05 | I.2 | Reference class and median for each live project |
| 06 | III.4 | First real ask. Then one a week from here, permanently |
| 07 | II.3 | Label every open decision R or I. Clear the Rs |
| 08 | IV.2 | Start three cheap tests. Score the log's first month |
| 09 | I.3 | Kill criteria, in writing, for everything running |
| 10 | III.1 | Three-line pre-write before every message that matters |
| 11 | II.2 | Price every commitment in hours per week, not money |

| 13 | I.4 | Decide one thing twice, a week apart. Compare |

| 15 | II.4 | Build one asset that keeps working when you stop |
| 16 | IV.4 | Publish the first piece. Same place every week after |
| 17 | — | Score four months of forecasts. **This is the exam, and you set it** |

| **The Monday line** | Weekly | Names the constraint. Unchanged 3 weeks = avoidance |

## Seven books, honestly labelled
Read one per track at most; reading more of these is the commonest way to avoid the drills.

- *Superforecasting*, Tetlock — **I.1, I.2.** The only book treating judgement as measurable and trainable. Start here.
- *Thinking in Bets*, Duke — **I.3.** Decision quality vs outcome quality. Short, repetitive, worth it for that distinction.
- *The Goal*, Goldratt — **II.1.** Constraints as a factory novel. Clumsy fiction; the idea transfers whole.
- *Made to Stick*, Heath & Heath — **III.3.** Over-anecdotal. Take the framework, skim the stories.
- *Style: Toward Clarity and Grace*, Williams — **III.2.** Rules you can apply mechanically to a written sentence. Unglamorous, unmatched.
- *Getting to Yes*, Fisher & Ury — **III.4.** Bloodless and correct. Voss is more fun and less reliable.
- *Peak*, Ericsson — **IV.1.** Skip the ten-thousand-hours arguments; keep the practice-loop design.

## How this gets failed
1. You read it all this week, feel sharpened, run no drill. Reading was the avoidance; it always is.
2. You keep the forecast log nine days and stop just before the first month resolves — exactly when it would have told you something.
3. You do every private module and skip the two with other people in them. The ask and the weekly publish carry the exposure, which is why they pay.
4. **You build an elaborate system for tracking the curriculum instead of running it.** It will look productive from outside and inside. → [[The Mentor Session — Seven Ideas]] §3.
5. You wait until things calm down. They don't.

## What the drills are actually for — added 31 August

The curriculum was written as general training. It is not general any more; it now serves one business ([[The Ten-Years-Ahead Operator]]), and four modules are load-bearing rather than educational:

| Module | In the abstract | In the practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **III.4** the weekly ask | one request to one person | **ten asks a week** to small US/UK firms who already outsource. This is Phase 1 |
| **I.1** the forecast log | calibration training | *"70% this prospect replies"* — your pipeline becomes the scoring set, free |
| **IV.1** feedback loops | signal and latency | **time one full month-end close built with Claude.** That number is the business model |
| **II.4** standing assets | one asset a month | the repeatable fixed-price package. Phase 2's gate is selling it three times unmodified |

Run those four against real client work and the other twelve become cheap. Run all sixteen as a study course and you have built [[The Mentor Session — Seven Ideas|apparatus]] again.
