# TEN THINGS I WAS BLIND TO
### Ordered by how much they change if taken seriously tomorrow

Part of [[Operating Manual]]. The compressed versions live in [[Five Models I Can't Unsee]].

---

### 1 · Sort tasks by uncertainty killed per minute, not by difficulty
Most people rank a list by size or dread. Rank instead by how much fog the task clears. A twenty-minute call that decides whether a path is six months or three years beats a week of planning built on the guess. It gets ignored because information-gathering produces no artefact and therefore doesn't feel like progress — but planning on an unresolved variable is decoration, not work.

Partner idea: most decisions are **two-way doors.** If you can walk it back cheaply, deciding fast is correct and deliberating is pure loss.

→ This is [[ACPA(T) NBAA To-Do]] in one paragraph.

### 2 · One constraint governs the system; everything else is decoration
At any moment one thing limits output — cash, hours, a credential, a person who hasn't replied. Improvement anywhere else returns **zero**, not "less". You will spend most of your life optimising non-constraints, because they are the parts that respond to effort and the constraint by definition doesn't.

The discipline is one weekly question: *what is actually holding this up?* — then refusing everything else until it moves.

### 3 · Productive procrastination is the most expensive habit there is, because it looks like virtue
Building the instrument instead of doing the thing. Organising notes instead of writing the page. Researching instead of pitching. It is avoidance that produces artefacts, so nobody — you least of all — can tell it from diligence, and it eats years.

The tell is emotional, not logical: the task you are doing is measurable and safe; the one you are avoiding is unmeasurable and exposes you to rejection. **Work going well but nothing leaving the building** is the diagnosis.

### 4 · Feedback-loop length sets how fast you improve — more than talent, more than hours
A domain that answers in a week makes you competent in a year. One that answers annually may never. Prizes and degrees deliver one noisy bit, twelve months late. Client pitches and published pieces deliver weekly.

Most people choose an arena for prestige and then wonder why they aren't improving. In a slow arena, **manufacture a fast loop inside it** — readers, a group, small public bets — or you are waiting, not practising.

### 5 · Price is set, not discovered
There is no correct rate the market knows and you must find. Rates are asserted; the market accepts or doesn't, and the acceptance range is far wider than you think. Two people of identical skill routinely earn 5× differently, and the difference is what number they said out loud without flinching.

**Narrowness licenses a high price from a beginner.** "Writer" competes with the world. "Financial reporting for small businesses, in clean English" competes with almost nobody.

### 6 · Distribution is a separate skill from craft, and it's the one that pays
Craft people believe quality is discovered and good work surfaces. It doesn't. Reach is its own discipline, roughly 10× more neglected than craft, which is precisely why your marginal hour returns most there. A mediocre thing with distribution beats an excellent thing without it, every time, and the excellent thing frequently is never seen.

An audience compounds; a finished manuscript does not.

### 7 · Your own liabilities are usually the highest guaranteed return available
Retiring a loan at 20% is a guaranteed, risk-free 20% return, better than nearly anything you'll be pitched. Money reasoning starts with the rate on what you owe, not what you might earn.

The half nobody does: **terms are negotiable far more often than borrowers ask.** Lenders restructure, extend and reprice, because a restructured loan pays better than a defaulted one. It costs one conversation. → [[Expenses]]

### 8 · A free option isn't free if it costs hours
Optionality is real — bounded downside, unbounded upside — but people misprice it by counting only cash. A no-fee entry that consumes 25 hours a week is one of the most expensive things you own, and the zero on the invoice is what stops you noticing.

Price every opportunity in **hours at their best alternative use.** Some tickets survive that test. The ones that don't are usually the ones you're attached to.

### 9 · Base rates beat stories, and your brain will not do this for you
You hear from the winner, never from the 7,805 who didn't place. Every field's public narrative is a survivorship exhibit. Prize money ÷ entrants gave £1.92 per entry in [[Three Tracks — An Honest Comparison]].

This is not "don't enter". Cheap positive-expectation long shots are fine (see 8). It means: know the number first, plan for the modal outcome, and never let a lottery be the only thing in the portfolio.

### 10 · Weak ties carry opportunity; strong ties already know what you know
Jobs, clients and introductions arrive overwhelmingly through people you know *slightly*, because your close circle shares your information and acquaintances reach pools you can't see. A cheap habit of staying faintly visible to many people outperforms almost any deliberate networking.

Deeper version: **being known for one specific thing is an asset that appreciates while you sleep.** Vague competence, however real, generates no inbound, because nobody can remember what to call you when the chance appears.

---

> [!warning] The two you are most blind to
> **#3 and #4.** They are the ones that dress up as diligence, and they are only visible when you look at output over months rather than days.
