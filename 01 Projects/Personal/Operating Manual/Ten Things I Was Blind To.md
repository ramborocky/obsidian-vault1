# TEN THINGS I WAS BLIND TO
### Rewritten after the research. Three of these are new and two were wrong.

Part of [[Operating Manual]] · prompt 1 of [[The Seven]]. Ordered by how much they change if taken seriously tomorrow.

---

### 1 · Sort tasks by uncertainty killed per minute, not by difficulty
Rank by how much fog a task clears, not by size or dread. **The live example is three fields on a MEMS profile** — grade, registration date, years logged — which decide whether your credential is six months away or three years, and which every plan in this folder is currently guessing at. Information-gathering produces no artefact and so doesn't feel like progress, but planning on an unresolved variable is decoration.

Partner idea: most decisions are **two-way doors.** If you can walk it back cheaply, deciding fast is correct and deliberating is pure loss.

### 2 · One constraint governs the system; everything else is decoration
Improvement anywhere but the bottleneck returns **zero**, not "less". Effort naturally flows to the parts that respond, and the constraint is by definition the part that doesn't. One weekly question: *what is actually holding this up?* — then nothing else until it moves.

Yours is monthly cash against two lenders. Not craft, not knowledge, not tooling.

### 3 · Productive procrastination is the most expensive habit there is, because it looks like virtue
Building the instrument instead of doing the thing. It is avoidance that produces artefacts, so nobody — you least of all — can tell it from diligence. The tell is emotional: the task you're doing is measurable and safe, the one you're avoiding exposes you to a verdict.

**And the new part: AI makes this nearly free.** A tool that can build apparatus at ten times the old speed is a tool that can hide you ten times faster. → [[The Mentor Session — Seven Ideas]] §3

### 4 · Feedback-loop length sets how fast you improve — more than talent, more than hours
A domain that answers weekly makes you competent in a year; one that answers annually may never. Prizes deliver one noisy bit, twelve months late. **Client work delivers a verdict every week, which is most of the argument for the practice over the prize** — not the money, the learning rate.

### 5 · Price is set, not discovered — and narrowness is what licenses the price
There is no correct rate the market knows. Two people of identical skill routinely earn 5× differently, and the difference is the number they said out loud without flinching.

Now with real figures: offshore bookkeepers bill **$10–25/hour**, AI-assisted practices charge **$150–400 per client per month**, and US small businesses budget **$400–800/month.** The same work, priced three ways. Which one you get is a decision, not a discovery. → [[Due Diligence — What the Market Says]]

### 6 · Users are not customers — name who writes the cheque or don't build
**The most expensive lesson available, and it cost someone else $14M.** Kippa reached roughly **500,000 African small businesses** with a bookkeeping app and still could not convert them into revenue; the app is now dark and users cannot retrieve their own records.

Adoption is not demand. Free usage is not a business. Before building anything, name the person who pays, the budget line it comes out of, and what they stop buying to buy you. If you cannot name all three, you have a project, not a company.

### 7 · Where the machine does the work, the value moves to the signature
This one is new in 2026 and it reframes your whole credential question. The funded model in accounting right now is explicitly *agents do the work, a certified accountant signs off* — Basis at a **$1.15bn** valuation, Modus at **$85M**, General Catalyst putting **$1.5bn** behind buying and AI-modernising services firms.

When execution gets cheap, the scarce thing is whoever is legally allowed to stand behind the output. **ACPA-PP is not a study decision any more. It is the moat.**

### 8 · Reach is a separate skill from craft, and it is the one that pays
Quality is not discovered; good work does not surface on its own. And the arithmetic of *where* you aim reach is brutal — YouTube CPM runs **$2.50 in Nigeria and $3.20 in Kenya against $32–36 in the US**, with finance content at $20–38. Same effort, ten to twenty times the return, depending only on who is watching.

The deeper version: opportunity arrives through people who know you *slightly*, and **being known for one specific thing is an asset that appreciates while you sleep.** Vague competence generates no inbound, because nobody can remember what to call you when the chance appears.

### 9 · Your own liabilities are the highest guaranteed return available
Retiring a loan at 17–20% is a guaranteed, risk-free 17–20% return, better than nearly anything you'll be offered. Money reasoning starts with the rate on what you owe.

And the half nobody does: **terms are negotiable far more often than borrowers ask.** Lenders restructure, because a restructured loan pays better than a defaulted one. It costs one conversation. → [[Expenses]]

### 10 · Base rates beat stories, and your brain will not do this for you
You hear from the winner, never from the 7,805 who didn't place. £15,000 across 7,806 entries is **£1.92 of expected value per entry.** Only 15–20% of Tanzania's SMEs get a bank loan. The numbers exist and are almost never looked up before committing.

This is not *don't enter.* Cheap positive-expectation long shots are fine. It means know the number first, plan for the modal outcome, and never let a lottery be the only thing in the portfolio.
