# 15 · Is this making me sick, and who am I staying for?

**Samin Nosrat, in your document.** She started selling fresh pasta out of a friend's empty commercial kitchen. It worked immediately. It grew into an online marketplace with a mailing list of tens of thousands, press attention, other unemployed cooks selling alongside her.

And it was destroying her. *"All of a sudden I'm managing 9,000 business licenses and permits and health department and taxes and sales tax and vendors... It was so much more than I ever wanted to do."* All she had wanted was to make pasta.

Two details matter.

**First, why she stayed too long:** other people's income depended on it. That is the strongest glue there is, and it is not the same thing as the business being right.

**Second, what actually got her out:** therapy. Not a spreadsheet. The thing that moved her was noticing she had wanted to be a writer since fifteen, had finally arranged the time and the office to do it, and *this thing was making that impossible.* She left, and went off to write blog posts for a yoga magazine for $25.

Ferriss's line in response is the one to keep: **you cannot spreadsheet your way out of everything, nor onto everything.**

That is a genuine correction to most of this folder. Almost everything here is arithmetic — expected values, price ladders, four clients at $350. The arithmetic is right and it is not sufficient. A plan that is optimal on paper and makes you ill has failed, and it will fail slowly enough that you will blame yourself rather than the plan.

Nosrat's own resolution is the practical part: now that she knows what a bad arrangement feels like in the body, she knows what to ask for in advance. She insists on creative control. She takes work that is *temporary* — one project, one article — so that the exit is built in before the entry.

> **The two questions:** is this making me sick — and if I'm staying because other people depend on it, is that a reason or a rationalisation?

Worth applying to the practice before it exists rather than after. Fifty small clients at $150 is a different life from twenty at $400, and the arithmetic barely distinguishes them.
