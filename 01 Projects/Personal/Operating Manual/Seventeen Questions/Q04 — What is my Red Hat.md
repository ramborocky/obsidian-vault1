# 4 · What is my Red Hat?

**Kelly's third generative, stated as an old joke that turned out not to be a joke:** software, free; the software manual, $10,000.

Red Hat has made a living for twenty-five years selling support, training and consultation for Linux — software that anybody can download for nothing. The code is free. Being told what it means, and being able to phone someone when it breaks, is not.

Kelly's second example is sharper. Getting a copy of your DNA will soon be free; pharmaceutical companies may eventually pay to sequence you. **The interpretation of what it means and what to do about it will be expensive.**

So the question for any offer: **which part of this is the free code, and which part is the manual?**

For the practice, the split is uncomfortable and clarifying:

| The free code | The manual |
| --- | --- |
| Reconciliations, schedules, journals | Which of your five customers is quietly unprofitable |
| A set of management accounts | Why margin fell three points and what to change |
| A filed return | Whether the business can survive the loan it is about to take |
| Data entry | What to say to the bank |

**Nearly every accountant sells the left column and gives the right one away** — the insight arrives as an aside at the end of a call, unbilled, because it took two minutes. Which is precisely backwards: the two minutes are the only part of the transaction that a machine cannot yet replace, and the eight hours are the part that is already collapsing to zero.

There is a local version of this too, and it is worth noticing. The mama ntilie training idea failed the payer test — but the reason banks require *"a proper recording system"* before lending is that they cannot interpret a shoebox. **The records are the free code. Whether this business is lendable is the manual**, and a bank will pay for that even when a trader won't.

> **The question:** what am I currently giving away in the last two minutes of the call, and what happens if I put it at the top of the invoice instead?
