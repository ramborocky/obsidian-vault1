# 13 · Do I need to make it back the way I lost it?

**Ferriss's question, and Lori Gottlieb's story in the same document is the cleanest illustration of it.**

Gottlieb left the entertainment business for medical school. Then, two years and two years of tuition into medical school — at Stanford — she left to become a freelance journalist. Everyone told her to just finish, get the degree, tough it out for two more years, then decide. She had the loans already. She had paid.

She left anyway, and said something specific about why: *life has a 100% mortality rate.* Why spend two more years on a path she knew she was not taking, in order to justify the two she had already spent?

That is the trap in one line. **Money and time already spent are gone, and the fact that they were spent on X is not a reason to spend more on X.** But the mind refuses this, because writing off the past feels like admitting it was wasted — so it goes back to the same table to win it back, and the losses compound.

The version that applies here is not about the writing being wrong. It is about **which table it has to be won back at.**

Years have gone into craft. Six knowledge bases, a nine-axis rubric, a story cycle planned, a published Kindle title. All of that is real skill and none of it is wasted. But the instinct is that the return on those years must arrive *as a prize*, from a panel, in the form the years were spent in. £15,000 across 7,806 entries is £1.92 of expected value per entry.

**The same craft pays at a different table.** An accountant who writes clean English is rare and priceable; interpretation is a writing skill before it is a numerical one; the weekly publish that builds a practice is prose. The skill converts. Only the venue changes.

> **The question:** am I trying to win this back at the table where I lost it — and is that table actually the one with the money on it?

Gottlieb's own summing-up is the reframe: she doesn't feel she had four careers. She had one, approached from different directions.
