# 12 · Am I hunting antelope or field mice?

**The image is a lion.** A lion can spend all day catching field mice and will starve doing it — each mouse costs more energy than it returns. The lion has to hunt antelope. Rarely, at real risk, for a payoff that actually feeds it.

The trap is that field mice are *available*. They are always there, they are catchable, and catching one produces a small genuine hit of accomplishment. A day of mice feels like a full day.

Sorted for you:

| Field mice | Antelope |
| --- | --- |
| Another craft base, another rubric axis | One paid client |
| Reorganising the vault | The three MEMS fields |
| Reading a seventh book on the list | The NBAA mentor question |
| Tuning the offer wording again | Ten asks sent |
| Buying the shoes | One published piece in the niche |

Look at the right column. **Four clients at $350 a month covers the entire household-and-debt figure of $1,228.** Four. Not four hundred. Every one of those four is an antelope, and there are perhaps a dozen genuinely antelope-shaped acts available to you this year.

The left column is not fake work. That is what makes it dangerous. Each item is real, useful, defensible, and would appear on any honest log of the week.

There is a sharper version of the question, which is about *tolerance for missing*. A lion that hunts antelope fails most days. Mice never fail. **So a preference for mice is usually a preference for a guaranteed small win over a probable miss** — which is the same avoidance circuit as everything else in this folder, wearing a productivity costume.

> **The question, asked on Friday:** which of the things I did this week could have produced a client, a fact, or a reader? If the answer is none, it was a week of mice, however full it felt.
