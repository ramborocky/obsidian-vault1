# 11 · If I could only work two hours a week on this, what would I do?

**Ferriss's forcing function**, and he is explicit that the softer version doesn't work. *"How can I not be a bottleneck in my own business?"* is a bad question — it invites a list. The extreme version is the useful one: gun to your head, two hours a week, what survives?

He pairs it with a second one that did most of the work. He had been the sole decision-maker on every edge case — a customs form, an unusual refund — dozens a week. The fix was an email: **from now on, if it involves less than $100, decide it yourself and write down what you did.** Then $500. Then $1,000. Then the reviews went weekly, monthly, quarterly, and eventually never.

Two things came out of it that transfer whole:

> To get huge, good things done, you need to be okay with letting the small, bad things happen. And people's IQs seem to double as soon as you give them responsibility and indicate that you trust them.

For a one-person practice with an AI doing production, the two-hour question has a precise answer, and it is the whole operating design:

- **Sell.** The asks. Nothing else generates a client.
- **Sign.** Review and stand behind the output. This is the part that legally cannot be delegated and is the entire moat.
- **Handle the exception.** The transaction nobody has a rule for yet.

Everything else — categorisation, reconciliation, drafting the commentary, chasing the missing receipt, building the pack — belongs to the machine and to a written procedure. And when it goes wrong at the $100 level, it is a note in a log, not an escalation.

The order matters too. **Sell, sign, exception** is the order of scarcity, and any week where production work displaces selling is a week the practice shrank while feeling busy.

> **The question:** with two hours, what would I keep? Then treat everything else as a candidate for a rule, a machine, or a person — and accept the small bad things that follow.
