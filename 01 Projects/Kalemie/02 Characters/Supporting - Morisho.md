# CHARACTER: Morisho

## Identity
- **Name:** Morisho
- **Role:** Supporting — dala dala conductor, wrongfully arrested
- **Age:** ~18
- **Occupation:** School dropout; conductor on the dala dala Djamela and Daudi ride

## Core Wound
Dropped out of school poor and stayed poor — every "soma shule usije ukawa konda kama mimi" (study hard so you don't end up wasted like me) he tells the schoolgirls is a wound he's naming out loud in someone else's direction because he can't fix it in his own.

## Lie They Believe
That small kindnesses — the two-thousand-shilling note for Anitha's school fees, the free coin for Djamela — balance the ledger of what men like him take from girls like her: attention, a joke, a hand too familiar on a door frame.

## Need
To be seen as more than a convenient arrest — as someone who, in his own limited and compromised way, was trying to protect the girls on his bus, not just extract from them.

## Want
To be liked by the girls he half-protects, half-preys on, without ever having to sit inside that contradiction long enough to name it.

## Contradiction
Genuinely protective and quietly predatory in the same breath — the coin he gives Djamela as she steps off could be a claim or could be tenderness, and the story never resolves which, because Morisho himself has never resolved it.

## Fear
Being exactly what the police decide he is the moment they need someone. Being unable to afford the envelope that would prove otherwise.

## Secret
He notices Jean Luc's SUV idling near the stop more than once and says nothing — not out of guilt, but because a conductor who talks about a rich man's car to the police is a conductor who stops having a route by next week.

## Speech
Street-fast Swahili and Lingala, transactional and warm at once — "mia tano mia tano," advice dressed as banter, banter dressed as advice.

## Habits
Keeps a stack of small notes arranged in the side of his drawer to hand to schoolgirls — school fees, coins, small currency of attention. Manages thirty passengers by instinct while still watching the door.

## Objects
The banknote stack, the dala dala door ledge he surfs like he's practicing something bigger than this route.

## Color
Faded HiAce-white, sun-bleached khaki.

## Smell
Diesel, sweat, the crowd.

## Religion
Not observant — his morality runs on street rules, not church ones.

## Dream
To own his own dala dala route someday instead of conducting someone else's.

## Shadow
He knows exactly what he is and doesn't pretend otherwise — the story's sharpest contrast with Jean Luc, who is the same shape of predator dressed as benefactor. Morisho never lies to himself about the transaction. That honesty doesn't make him safe. It just makes him legible.

## Arc
Genuine, crude, protective-and-predatory in the same breath — until he becomes the story's proof of the Controlling Idea's dark twin: *the system doesn't need the real predator caught. It needs someone caught.* He's visible, poor, and unable to afford an envelope. That's the entire trial. *Street-smart protector → convenient sacrifice.*

## Relationship Web
- **[[Protagonist - Daudi]]:** A minor authority figure Daudi half-trusts and half-warns Djamela against ("HIV," motioning at him) — Daudi's ambivalence about Morisho mirrors the reader's.
- **[[Supporting - Djamela]]:** Gives her a coin as she steps off the bus — read as habit, affection, or a small claim, deliberately unresolved.
- **[[Antagonist - Jean Luc]]:** Mirrors, not rivals. Both take from girls — Morisho takes small things (attention, presence), Jean Luc takes big things (identity, future). The difference the story insists on: Morisho knows what he is. Jean Luc doesn't.
