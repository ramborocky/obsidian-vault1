# CHARACTER: D'Juma

## Identity
- **Name:** D'Juma
- **Role:** Supporting — Djamela's father
- **Age:** ~45
- **Occupation:** Fruit-market trader by day (Ave de Tanganyique), traditional healer by evening

## Core Wound
Watched colonial and post-colonial extraction — the mines, the mineral wealth, Leopold's ghost still coagulating over Kalemie like the smoke off the birthday candle — empty the town of anything he could hand his daughter besides proverbs and a fruit stall. He has nothing built to leave her. So he built her sayings instead.

## Lie They Believe
That refusing the West — no cake, no Kim Kardashian, "that's a western thing" — protects Djamela from wanting it. It doesn't. It just makes the want a secret she keeps from him, which is worse than the want itself.

## Need
To say, once, plainly, without a parable standing in front of it, that he is afraid for her.

## Want
For Djamela to be proud of what they have — the stall, the watermelon, the lake — instead of measuring it against Rue Saint Michel and finding it wanting.

## Contradiction
A wise man whose wisdom is also a form of avoidance. Every proverb he offers Djamela is true and also a way of not looking directly at what's actually happening to her.

## Fear
That he already knows, in the way people know weather, what is going to happen to his daughter — and that naming it plainly would make it real before he's ready.

## Secret
He has seen Jean Luc's SUV outside places it shouldn't be, more than once, and said nothing to Djamela directly — hoping the lake speech would be warning enough without having to accuse anyone by name.

## Speech
Parable first, plain statement never — until the ending, when grief strips the parable away and he speaks a single unadorned sentence for the first time in the story.

## Habits
Skims pebbles while he talks, like Daudi. Hands out fruit before he hands out advice. Attends "traditional African healing customers" in the evenings — half medicine, half listening.

## Objects
Watermelon (refused cake), the fruit stall, pebbles skimmed on the lake surface.

## Color
Deep green (watermelon rind), lake-grey.

## Smell
Overripe fruit, woodsmoke, the mineral smell of lake water on his hands.

## Religion
A blend of Catholic parish attendance and older lake-cosmology he never quite names as separate from it.

## Dream
That Djamela inherits the stall proudly, on her own terms, rather than fleeing it in shame.

## Shadow
His refusals (no cake, no western things) are control dressed as principle — he polices her wanting instead of ever addressing why she wants it, which is real deprivation, not vanity.

## Arc
Speaks entirely in proverbs and refusals for the whole story — a way of loving that never risks the words "I'm scared for you." After Djamela's death, he says one plain sentence to Daudi at the lakeshore, unparabled: "I was wrong. The lake doesn't put things back in order. It keeps them. There's a difference." That correction is the first ungoverned thing he says in the entire story — and it's what finally lets Daudi understand the lake speech was never comfort. It was D'Juma rehearsing, for years, how to survive losing her, long before he had to. *Parable as armor → one plain sentence as grief.*

## Relationship Web
- **[[Protagonist - Daudi]]:** The son-shaped listener he never had — Daudi absorbs the lake speech the way D'Juma needs someone to, since Djamela never fully does.
- **[[Supporting - Djamela]]:** Loves her completely and communicates almost none of it directly; the birthday watermelon is the clearest translation he has of "I love you and I have so little to give it in."
- **[[Antagonist - Jean Luc]]:** Never confronts him directly — sees him only from a distance, a car he half-recognizes outside places it shouldn't be. His silence here mirrors Daudi's own inaction; the story's Controlling Idea implicates the father too, not just the boy.
