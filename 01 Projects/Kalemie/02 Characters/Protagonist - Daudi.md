# CHARACTER: Daudi

## Identity
- **Name:** Daudi
- **Role:** Protagonist
- **Age:** 15
- **Occupation:** School Student

## Core Wound
His mother died giving birth to him. He has built an entire imagined mother out of that absence — patient, soft-spoken, his — and no living woman can compete with someone who was never real enough to disappoint him. His father remarried, but a stepmother arriving after the fantasy was already built never had a chance; Daudi doesn't resent her exactly, he just doesn't let her in far enough to matter.

## Lie They Believe
That if he finally says the words to Djamela — the confession he's rehearsed and never delivered — she will love him back, completely, and the hole his mother left will close. He has mistaken Djamela for the mother he never had, which is why losing her to Jean Luc doesn't just feel like heartbreak. It feels like the first loss happening again.

## Need
Food, an education he can actually finish, and the thing no one has given him yet: a mother's unconditional love. Underneath both — self-belief that doesn't depend on being chosen by someone else first.

## Want
Djamela, unconditionally. Justice for anyone he watches being wronged. Men like Jean Luc — who prey on naive girls — put away, permanently.

## Contradiction
He wants to be the one who protects naive girls from predatory men, but he can't protect the one girl who matters most to him, and some nights that failure curdles into something closer to entitlement — as if loving her hard enough should have been sufficient to keep her out of Jean Luc's car.

## Fear
That Djamela's love was never real. That his father dies next and the last inherited version of "family" goes with him. That he's not strong enough — not against the older boys who bully him, and not against a man like Jean Luc, who doesn't even have to raise his voice to win.

## Secret
He knows. He's known for a while that Jean Luc is grooming Djamela and that she's letting it happen — going along with the renaming, the car, the promises. He doesn't say anything, not to protect her, but to protect the story he tells himself: that she is still his, that this is a phase, that it hasn't actually happened yet if he doesn't name it. Separately, and unrelated to Djamela: he steals. Small things left unattended in the yards of the rich — a tool, a radio, once a bicycle wheel — always with the same explanation ready: *I found it.*

## Speech
Mostly Swahili, some Lingala, short sentences he trusts to land clearly the first time. Around adults — especially men like Jean Luc — he reaches for something more clinical, more "scientific," a register he's picked up from the detective novels he reads. It's armor. If he sounds like he knows things, he's harder to dismiss as just a boy.

## Habits
Wandering alone into the bush to see what's there. Seeking out older men for whatever knowledge they'll hand over. Taking apart and rebuilding things other people have thrown away — a gutted computer, a broken chair — less out of need for the object and more because *fixable* is a category he badly wants to believe in.

## Objects
The lake, where his placenta was returned to the water at birth, in the old way — which makes the lake, in some half-superstitious part of him, the closest thing he has to a mother's body. His books, Conan Doyle chief among them — Holmes as a model for a kind of attention that turns powerlessness into method. Broken machines he's rebuilding, one part scavenged at a time.

## Color
Khaki shorts, a red tracksuit bottom, the red of the earth under it. His own skin, which he is aware of the way boys become aware of skin only once someone has made it a target. A hatred, mostly wordless, for the colonisers and the expatriates who still move through Kalemie like they own the ground under it. A private, embarrassing love for black cars with a shine to them — which he'd never admit shares a shape with the thing he hates about Jean Luc.

## Smell
Body odor, unwashed socks, the smell of a boy nobody is doing his laundry for with any care. It's a smell he's self-conscious about around Djamela and never mentions.

## Religion
Catholic by upbringing, scientific by inclination — he doesn't experience these as in conflict, mostly because he hasn't been forced to yet.

## Dream
A companion who loves him without condition, without him having to earn it first. An end to being, fundamentally, alone in a house with people who share his blood but not his particular grief.

## Shadow
He envies Jean Luc more than he'd ever say — not the exploitation, but the ease, the car, the way rooms rearrange themselves around a man like that. Somewhere underneath the moral outrage is a boy who has quietly decided his own smallness — physical, social, sexual — is the real reason Djamela is drifting toward someone else. That's the shadow: a 15-year-old telling himself the story is about justice when part of it is about inadequacy.

## Arc
Naive schoolboy who believes love is a transaction he can win by being good enough → young man who understands the world isn't fair and rewards no one for deserving things. The lesson isn't that he becomes hard. It's that he learns the difference between winning and getting through — and chooses, deliberately, to keep acting anyway, even once he knows acting won't be rewarded.

## Relationship Web
- **[[Antagonist - Jean Luc]]:** The man he hates and, in the part of himself he doesn't examine, wants to be. Daudi watches Jean Luc the way a detective watches a suspect — but detectives are supposed to act on what they find, and Daudi doesn't, not until it's too late.
- **[[Supporting - Djamela]]:** The love he's mistaken for a mother. He knows what's happening to her before he'll say it out loud, even to himself — his silence is the story's clearest evidence for the Controlling Idea: inaction is complicity.
- **[[Supporting - D'Juma]]:** The father-shaped listener Daudi absorbs the lake speech for, since D'Juma's own daughter never fully receives it. Daudi trusts him the way he can't quite trust his own father.
- **[[Supporting - Morisho]]:** A minor authority Daudi half-trusts and half-warns Djamela against — his ambivalence about Morisho ("HIV," he mutters, motioning at him) mirrors the reader's own uncertainty about which man on this bus is actually dangerous.
