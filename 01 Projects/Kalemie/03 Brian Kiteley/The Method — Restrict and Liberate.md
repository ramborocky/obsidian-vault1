# KITELEY — RESTRICT AND LIBERATE

> The sonnet is a shape to hang content on, not the fabric, thread, or clasps with which to work.

## The Central Claim

Constraints do not limit invention. They **provoke** it — precisely because they are restrictive and liberating at the same time. Kiteley's exercises are written like sonnet instructions: an arbitrary rule, a word count, and no guidance about content.

The word counts are part of the constraint, not administration. Most exercises run 300–800 words. His instruction is to draft longer and then edit down to the limit — and the rule underneath it:

> Any time you can cut a piece of prose by 20 percent, you should cut it by 20 percent.

He learned the principle by accident: in his twenties he wrote for an hour before work, and the best writing was always in the last fifteen minutes, when he knew he had to stop. **The pressure of having to end freed him.**

## On Not Writing A Story

The most counterintuitive instruction in the book, and the one most likely to help a stalled project:

> Keep doing these exercises around an idea **without trying to write a story.** Hold off bringing the elements together as long as possible.

The etymology carries the argument. *Exercise* comes from a Latin farming term meaning **to drive out of an enclosure**. Let the constraints rule the creation rather than any idea of what a story should be.

His workshop method: ten exercises in four weeks, all cohering around one set of characters, one place, a short span of time — the components of a short story — assembled into a story only afterwards, by the group, sometimes by welding two unrelated exercises together.

## Four Supporting Ideas

- **Each sentence educates the next sentence** (Gass). Kiteley reads far more than he writes, and rereads constantly — "incantatory rereading," an attempt to find the magic. Barthes: a first reading shows what we already know; rereading teaches what we don't.
- **Out-of-body work.** You can't solve a problem with the same mind that created it. The exercises let you become editor, outsider, finder of lost threads.
- **Recognizability is talent.** A talented writer is one whose prose you recognise. And the painter James Weeks's warning: *the thing most teachers tell you to get rid of is the thing you most need to hang onto.*
- **Keep everything.** Organise and name the fragments so you can find them years later. A classmate who burned all her old writing horrified him.

## The Success Test

Reread an exercise days or weeks later, when the prose has gone unfamiliar. If you feel the living, breathing complexity of a whole story kicking at the outer walls of the fragment, and you wonder *what next?* — it succeeded.

## For Kalemie

**The 20 percent rule is the single most immediately useful sentence in this book for this project.**

At 3,941 words, a 20% cut on the master draft recovers **788 words** — nearly double what the ending needs, and more than the entire remaining expansion budget in [[Kalemie — Standing Diagnosis]]. Kiteley's claim is not that 20% is a target but that it is always *available*. Applied to [[2026-07-26 — Chantal (Djamela POV, Draft 2 — Barrett Pass)|Chantal Draft 2]] at 3,399, it would give 2,719 — still inside the prize's 2,000-word floor with room to spare.

**On "not writing a story," a genuine warning.** This instruction is medicine for a writer with no material. Kalemie has three drafts, a beat sheet, five other craft bases and no ending. Told to hold off assembling elements, this project would happily comply forever.

**So invert it.** Use the exercises exclusively as [[Prescriptions for the Live Draft|prescriptions against named faults]], and treat each one as owing a specific paragraph to a specific scene. Kiteley's own last exercise points the same way — *make up your own exercise in the context of a story you are struggling with*, using the characters and setting of the actual manuscript.

**Where the sonnet principle has already worked here, unrecognised.** The Commonwealth's 2,000–5,000 gate is exactly a sonnet constraint, and it has produced the most useful thinking on this project — the word budget, the cut-list, the decision to end Chantal at the act rather than the reflection. [[The Balance Test — Length as a Function of Idea|Barrett's balance test]] is the same idea a century earlier. **The ceiling is not an obstacle to the story. It is the only reason several of its best decisions got made.**

**On recognizability, one specific application.** Weeks's line — what a teacher tells you to cut is what you most need to keep — is worth holding against every diagnosis in this vault, including mine. The things repeatedly flagged in Kalemie are its long sentences, its unglossed local language, its physics vocabulary and its refusal to explain. **Those are also the four things nobody else's story has.** Cut the faults; do not cut the signature.

## Questions for AI

1. Cut the master draft by exactly 20% without losing an event. Report what went and whether anything real was lost.
2. Do the same to Chantal Draft 2. Compare which draft survives the cut better — that is a real quality signal.
3. Which of Kalemie's repeatedly criticised features are faults, and which are the signature Weeks warns about? Separate the lists and defend both.
4. Am I using exercises to defer assembly? Check the dates: what has actually entered the draft in the last week?
5. Apply the success test to the last three things I wrote. Do any of them have a whole story kicking at the walls?
