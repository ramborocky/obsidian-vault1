# KITELEY — POINT OF VIEW EXERCISES

> The first-person pronoun is provoked by others; it bursts out when you need to differentiate yourself from the surrounding world of other egos.

## The Framing

Kiteley discourages beginners from writing in multiple points of view, and gives a reason worth keeping: **omniscience and multiple perspectives rob you of the writer's most important tool, which is suspense.** If the reader knows what everyone thinks, there is nothing left to withhold.

He also notes that we rarely use *I* in our own everyday thoughts. It appears under pressure, when we need to separate ourselves from other people. That observation is the engine of the first exercise.

## The Four That Matter Here

**1. The Reluctant I** — write a first-person story using *I*, *me* or *my* **only twice**, while keeping the narrator important to the narrative. The point is a narrator less interested in himself than in what he is observing. Critical constraint: the reader must know within the first few lines that this is first person — no surprises forty words in. *600 words.*

This exercise generated Kiteley's own first novel. He noticed his grandfather told stories with remarkably few first-person pronouns, applied the rule, and the book started sounding like him.

**8. Third to First** — rewrite a section originally in third person into first, or vice versa. Then **halve the pronoun count**: fifteen *she*s become eight *I*s. *500 words at first.* His note: this looks arbitrary and is more interesting than it looks, because it lets you see the narrative in neutral terms and find where the reader needs to stand.

**11. The Cheerful Spectator** — a narrator intimate with the story but essentially unimportant to its outcome. The gain is leisure: room to explore the kinks in the plot and the gaps in the observer's knowledge. *800 words.*

**4. The Unstable Self** — alternate between *I* and *he/she* for a character under stress. *500 words.*

## The Idea Worth Stealing Regardless

From the same section, Broyard's analyst on what he missed from his patients: **incongruity** — "the gorgeous incongruities that people produce under stress," the abrupt leap from the mundane to the sublime. Kiteley's most common criticism of contemporary fiction is that it does not reflect these wrenches of perspective. *Look for the musical sound of abrupt self-transcendence.*

## For Kalemie

**Both drafts are first person and neither has settled the question [[Methods of Narration — The Author Must Vanish|Barrett raised]].** Kiteley supplies the drills that answer it by experiment rather than argument.

**The Reluctant I is aimed straight at Daudi.** He is a narrator who catalogues, watches, and does not act — which is a fault when it is passivity and a technique when it is deliberate self-effacement. The exercise forces the distinction. Two pronouns across 600 words, applied to the opening, would push the prose toward the objects and away from the observer: exactly the register the clue-list already achieves and the rest of the draft loses.

There is a real prize here. *"In our neighbourhood they called me the founder of dead things"* uses *me* once and is the best line in the story. The draft's own instinct is already Reluctant-I; it just has not been made a rule.

**Third to First is the cheap test for the POV question.** Rather than arguing about whether Kalemie should be first or third — [[Methods of Narration — The Author Must Vanish|Barrett's four objections]] versus modern practice — take one scene, convert it, halve the pronouns, and read both. 500 words settles what a month of theory will not.

**The Cheerful Spectator is worth a serious look, because it may be what Kalemie actually is.** Daudi is intimate with the story and, by the ending's own logic, unimportant to its outcome — he cannot save Djamela, cannot convict Jean Luc, cannot free Morisho. The draft treats his powerlessness as a flaw to be fixed. Kiteley treats that exact position as a *form*, with a payoff: leisure to explore the kinks and the gaps in what the observer knows.

**That reframing may dissolve the passive-protagonist problem rather than solving it.** Every base in this vault has diagnosed Daudi as too passive. It is worth one 800-word experiment to test the opposite hypothesis — that he is a spectator narrator in a story that has been trying to make him a hero.

**On incongruity, one specific gap.** Kalemie's characters are consistent to a fault: Daudi observes, Djamela wants, Jean Luc glides, D'juma parables. Nobody leaps. The nearest thing to a wrench of perspective is Djamela's *"See? Not everything falls apart"* — a girl who has just kissed someone reaching, in one line, for cosmology. That is the register Kiteley means, and it happens once.

## Questions for AI

1. Rewrite the opening as The Reluctant I — two first-person pronouns in 600 words. Report what the constraint forced into the prose.
2. Convert Scene 7 to third person and halve the pronouns. Show both versions and say where the reader ends up standing in each.
3. Test the Cheerful Spectator hypothesis: read Daudi as a spectator narrator rather than a failed protagonist. Which scenes suddenly work?
4. Find every wrench of perspective in the draft — abrupt leaps from mundane to sublime. If there are fewer than three, that is the finding.
5. Count first-person pronouns per scene in both drafts. Where the count spikes, is the narrator under pressure, or just present?
