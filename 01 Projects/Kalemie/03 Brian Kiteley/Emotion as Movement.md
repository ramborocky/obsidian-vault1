# KITELEY — EMOTION AS MOVEMENT

> *Emotion* comes from the Latin *emovere*: to move out of.

## The Etymological Argument

The word contains the idea of movement, and Kiteley builds a craft principle on it:

> Characters are convincing and plausible **only by the way they move from one state of mind to another**, from one crisis to another.

Not by having feelings. By changing between them. A character in a fixed emotional state, however intensely rendered, is not doing what fiction does — which is why "she was devastated" fails and a woman who is funny at her sister's bedside and then destroyed months later by handwriting succeeds.

He pairs this with Dennett's account of consciousness as **producing future** — the brain extracting anticipations to stay one step ahead of disaster. *We read fiction to see how characters improvise their lives moment by moment.*

## The Exercises Worth Having

**80. True Feeling** — describe intensely and exhaustively an exchange of true feelings between two characters, in simple straightforward language. One fairly obvious, uncomplicated emotion, exposed. **Give the character a secret — and reveal it neither to the reader nor to the other character.** *500 words.* The Meryl Streep method: she gives every character a secret nobody on set knows.

**66. Wants** — two characters want the same thing; if one gets it the other cannot. *500 words.* Kiteley's line: *the best stories have the simplest problems at their heart.*

**48. Consciousness Slowed** — slow a sequence of events down dramatically, using repetition or near-repetition to show perceived reality changing. *700 words.*

**86. Teacher** — one character teaches another something **that changes the teacher**. *500 words.*

**92. Witness Protection** — a character improvises a new identity in an entirely new situation. Not about the new situation; about **how the character adjusts her mind to it**. *400 words.*

**100. Style** — describe one simple event five radically different ways, same narration type throughout. *350 words.* Adapted from Gardner, and paired with Gass's five-rung psychic distance ladder, from *It was the winter of the year 1953* down to *Snow. Under your collar…*

## For Kalemie

**The movement principle explains a fault none of the other bases has named: Kalemie's characters do not change state within scenes.**

Daudi observes throughout. Djamela wants throughout. Jean Luc glides throughout. Each is consistent, well-drawn, and static — and static is the thing Kiteley says makes characters implausible. The one genuine state-change in the master draft is Jean Luc's flicker when the older officer says *minor*, and the draft treats it as a plot beat rather than as what it is: **the only moment anyone in the story moves out of one state into another.**

**Witness Protection is Djamela's scene and it is not written.** She answers to *Chantal* and the draft moves on within a paragraph. Kiteley's framing — *not about the new situation but about how the character adjusts her mind to it* — is precisely what the draft's own callout is asking when it wonders whether the renaming "goes by too fast." 400 words on the adjustment, not the event.

**True Feeling is the missing beat at the police station.** The pregnancy disclosure produces no interior movement in Daudi — the sawdust-doll fault [[Character by Salient Detail|Barrett names]] and the question [[Q and A — Delete the Questions|the callout has been asking since July]]. Kiteley adds the constraint that makes it writable: **give him a secret and reveal it to nobody.** His [[Protagonist - Daudi|character file]] already supplies one — he has known about Jean Luc for months and said nothing. Write the exchange with that pressing underneath and unspoken, and the beat writes itself.

**Wants is the cleanest test of the story's engine.** Two characters want the same thing and only one can have it. In Kalemie, what Daudi wants is Djamela; what Djamela wants is out. Those are not the same thing, which means the story does not currently have the structure Kiteley describes — and it may explain why the middle feels like observation rather than pressure. The version where they *do* collide is worth 500 words of testing.

**Teacher is D'juma's scene, and it is the one the Scenario already half-designed.** One character teaches another something *that changes the teacher*. D'juma delivers the lake cosmology and is unchanged by it; [[Scenario — Arcs, Beats & Scenes|the Scenario's ending]] has him return, unparabled, to correct it — *the lake doesn't put things back in order, it keeps them.* **That is exercise 86 exactly**, and it is the strongest thing in the drafted ending.

**Psychic distance is the underused instrument.** Gass's ladder maps onto the two drafts precisely: Kalemie's opening sits at rung 5 (*Snow. Under your collar…*), the neighbourhood paragraph at rung 1 (*It was the winter of the year 1953*). The story moves between rungs without deciding to, which is a large part of what reads as unevenness. **Exercise 100 on a single paragraph, five ways, would make the ladder visible** — and it is Gardner's exercise, so it cross-reads with [[Psychic Distance]] directly.

## Questions for AI

1. For each scene, name the state the POV character starts in and the state they end in. Flag every scene where they are the same.
2. Write exercise 92 for Djamela's renaming — 400 words on the adjustment, not the event.
3. Write exercise 80 for the pregnancy disclosure, with Daudi's secret pressing underneath and never surfacing.
4. Where on Gass's five-rung ladder does each scene sit? Chart it, and find the unintended jumps.
5. Do Daudi and Djamela ever want the same thing? If not, what would the story become if they did?
