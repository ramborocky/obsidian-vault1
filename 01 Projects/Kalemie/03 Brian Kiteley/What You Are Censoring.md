# KITELEY — WHAT YOU ARE CENSORING

> My teacher Donald Barthelme said writers all had some major thing they were censoring. Most of us scoffed at this, after class.

## The Story

Kiteley's class scoffed. Then Barthelme told him privately what his own censored subject was: his father, an intimidating architect. He described a dream — a room stacked with Donald's own books, at the height of his fame, in which his father picked one up, walked over, hit him on the forehead with it and said, *why don't you get a real job, Don?*

Kiteley's conclusion is the useful part. Not that the dream was sad, but that **the block was real and specific and had to be attacked with every tool available.** The exercises exist partly to find what you are avoiding.

## Why Exercises Get At It

The censored thing is protected by exactly the faculty you use to write deliberately. So the method is to bypass deliberation:

- **Out-of-body work.** You can't solve a problem with the same mind that created it. Become the editor, the outsider, the finder of lost threads — someone other than the person who did the censoring.
- **Speed and volume.** [[Outrunning the Critic|Exercise 190]] works by making you write faster than you can supervise.
- **Arbitrary constraint.** A restriction gives the censoring mind something else to worry about.

## The Two Direct Exercises

**108. Censorship** — write about what you are avoiding.

**130. Ignoble Subjects** — write a fragment on a subject you find ignored or avoided in contemporary fiction, for reasons of taste or decorum. Kiteley suggests listing such subjects first, then choosing one, or letting it choose you. *500 words.*

He is careful here in a way worth noting: his teaching example is Todd Solondz's *Happiness*, a film about a father who assaults his son's classmate, in which **nothing of the act is shown** and the disturbing scene is a conversation afterwards. His students laughed easily at Tarantino's blood and asked to be excused from Solondz. His point is that graphic violence had become comfortable while the genuinely difficult subject had not — and that the difficult one was handled by *withholding the event entirely*.

That is the model. The ignoble subject is approached by refusing to depict it.

## For Kalemie

**Scene 6 is the censored thing, and this is the note that says so plainly.**

[[Kalemie — Standing Diagnosis]] reached it by measurement: the prose spikes into its least controlled register (*"the bioluminescent stream of pure everything"*) at exactly the moment its meaning is least determined. [[The ELIZA Effect and the Vestments of Plausibility]] reached it by mechanism: the frame is not rigid, so the reader cannot furnish the gap. Gardner's [[Three Faults of Soul — Sentimentality, Frigidity, Mannerism|frigidity]] names it as the writer declining to look at the thing the story is about.

**Kiteley adds the only thing those three lack, which is what to do on Tuesday morning.**

Run **108. Censorship** on the scene directly — not the scene rewritten, but a fragment about what the story will not look at. Then run **130. Ignoble Subjects**. The list you make before choosing is itself the diagnostic; if Scene 6 is not on it, the list is lying.

**And the Solondz model resolves the craft question the diagnosis left open.** The choice is not between depicting and evading. It is between *depicting* and *deliberately withholding a determined event* — which is what [[2026-07-26 — Chantal (Djamela POV, Draft 2 — Barrett Pass)|Chantal]] already does successfully twice: *"I am not going to describe the hotel,"* and *"Nothing happened to my body in that room."* Both are rigid frames with empty centres. Both work. Scene 6 in the master draft does neither.

**The second censored thing may be larger.** Every version of this story routes the girl's interior life through a boy who survives her. The Scenario's own open question asks whether Djamela's fragment should be pulled out into its own story. Two full drafts now exist and Kalemie still has not let her speak inside it. That is not a craft oversight — it has been noticed, named in the Structural Notes, and deferred three times. Kiteley's framing is worth sitting with: **what gets deferred repeatedly is usually not difficult, it is protected.**

**A third candidate, offered without insistence.** Daudi's [[Protagonist - Daudi|character file]] states flatly that he knows what is happening to Djamela and says nothing to protect the story he tells himself — and that he steals, and explains it with *I found it*. The draft dramatises neither. The Controlling Idea is *inaction is complicity*, and the one scene that would prove it — Daudi choosing silence, onscreen, knowing — has been on the Dashboard as a High Priority item since 22 July and remains unwritten. **Every craft base in this vault has diagnosed the passive protagonist. None of them asked why that particular scene is the one that never gets written.**
