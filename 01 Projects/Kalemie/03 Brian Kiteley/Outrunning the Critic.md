# KITELEY — OUTRUNNING THE CRITIC (EXERCISE 190)

> In my classes, this has been the most fruitful exercise of the whole book.

## The Exercise

Write **one hundred short sentences** about a character in your fiction. Do not lift pen from paper — or fingers from keyboard — for all one hundred. Then go back **exactly twenty-four hours later** and revise.

Three constraints that do the work:

- The sentences **need not connect** or follow logically.
- **Do not start sentences with the character's name or a pronoun.** This is the rule that forces new material — without it you write a hundred variations of *she was.*
- A hundred is the minimum stretch; two hundred or five hundred would be better.

*800 words.* You may substitute a central concept, or a key room or space, for a character.

## Why It Works

Kiteley's own account: students take to heart the idea of writing without the editorial voice interfering, and **it breaks writers out of their narrative habits better than anything else in the book.**

The mechanism is pressure substitution. Producing a hundred sentences is unnerving, and after a certain point *the pressure of creating character details overcomes the pressure to tell a story.* You stop narrating and start noticing. His students applauded the dramatic change in style it produced in their classmates — which is the real signal: it changes the prose, not just the notes.

The twenty-four-hour rule matters. Same-day revision is still the writing mind. A day later it is a reader's.

## For Kalemie

**Three characters need this, and one of them needs it urgently.**

**Djamela, first.** She is the deuteragonist of a story told entirely from outside her, and the gap has been named in the Structural Notes, in [[Character by Salient Detail|Barrett's terms]], and in every diagnosis since July. [[2026-07-26 — Chantal (Djamela POV, Draft 2 — Barrett Pass)|Chantal]] fixed it by giving her a whole story — which works, and which does nothing for Kalemie itself. A hundred sentences about her, none beginning with her name or a pronoun, is the cheapest available route to the material Kalemie is missing.

The no-pronoun rule is doing something specific here. Her existing description is *tall and beautiful just like her mother* — the catalogue failure. Forbidding *she* forces the sentences into objects, actions, spaces, other people's reactions: *The nails came off on Sunday nights, in the dark, over a newspaper.* That is the register the story needs and cannot reach by trying.

**D'juma and Morisho, next.** Both are still blank character templates. Both were given full arcs in [[Scenario — Arcs, Beats & Scenes|the Scenario]] — D'juma's parable-as-armour, Morisho's small kindnesses that balance nothing — and neither has entered the draft. A hundred sentences each, and the Dashboard's empty dataview tables fill themselves as a by-product.

**The substitution option is the interesting one.** Kiteley permits swapping the character for a **key room or space**. Kalemie has two that are load-bearing and thinly rendered:

- **Daudi's room** — the buckets, the mattress, the wall of nailed things, the loose board under the third bucket. It is where Scene 6 happens and where the ending happens, and it has never been described at length.
- **The lake** — the story's governing metaphor, currently carried almost entirely by D'juma explaining it. A hundred sentences about the lake, none starting with *it* or *the lake*, would give the metaphor a body instead of a speech, and would let [[The Beginning — Three Paragraphs at Most|the explanation be cut]] without losing anything.

**One honest note on doing this with me.** The exercise works by outrunning the critic — and a model has no critic to outrun. I can produce a hundred sentences instantly and they will regress toward the plausible, which is [[The Funhouse Mirror — AI Output as Cliché Detector|exactly the cliché]] the exercise exists to escape. **Do this one by hand.** Use me afterwards, at the twenty-four-hour mark, to mark which sentences are yours and which are anyone's.

## Questions for AI

1. I have written 100 sentences about Djamela. Mark every one you could have generated without reading the draft — those are the ones to cut.
2. Of what remains, which five contain a whole scene inside them?
3. Check the no-pronoun rule: flag any sentence that starts with a name or pronoun and rewrite it.
4. Run the same audit on 100 sentences about the lake. Has the metaphor acquired a body, or is it still D'juma's speech in disguise?
5. Twenty-four hours later: which sentences surprise me? Those are the ones the exercise was for.
