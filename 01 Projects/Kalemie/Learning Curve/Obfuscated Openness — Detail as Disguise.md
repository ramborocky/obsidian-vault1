# OBFUSCATED OPENNESS — DETAIL AS DISGUISE

> Source: Lynda Clark, *All the Small Things* (*Short Fiction in Theory and Practice* 12:1)

## The Problem It Solves

You want to write something true — a real loss, a real place, a real person — and publish it. Two objections arrive immediately, and Clark takes both seriously:

- **The privacy objection.** Some critics hold that private grief should stay private rather than invite a voyeuristic readership.
- **The overshare objection.** Your own. The fear that the writing is therapy performed in public, and that readers will be able to see which parts are yours.

The usual escapes are both bad. Fictionalise heavily and the truth drains out. Publish it raw and you have written a document rather than a story.

## Clark's Solution

In her story *Total Transparency* — about a woman slowly turning invisible, written after her sister's death — she used **such a proliferation of small specific details that even people who knew the real events could not tell which were remembered and which were invented.**

This is the technique, and it inverts the usual intuition. The instinct when protecting something true is to include *fewer* real details. Clark's method is to include *more* details of every kind, so that the real ones are camouflaged among the invented ones. Volume is the disguise.

Three supporting moves:

- **A framing device with multiple valid readings.** The woman's literal invisibility can be read as grief, as denial, as the social erasure of the ill. No reading is authorised, so no reading is confessional.
- **Withheld identity.** Her narrator has no name and no gender; nor does the partner. Iser's point again — the reader shades in the outlines, and outlines are harder to trace back to a real person.
- **Deliberate double meanings.** A line about the condition not hurting reads simultaneously as denial and as literal reassurance. The title itself carries both the vanishing and the irony of a story that is anything but transparent.

## Why It Works Beyond Privacy

The reason to know this technique is not primarily protective. It is that **it produces better prose than either alternative.**

A story loaded with specific unexplained detail has texture, and texture is what makes a fictional world feel prior to the reader's arrival. The camouflage requirement forces you to invent detail at the same density as the details you remember — and invented detail written to that standard is indistinguishable from remembered detail, which is the definition of a convincing fictional world.

The privacy is a by-product. The texture is the point.

## For Kalemie

Kalemie is set in a real port on Lake Tanganyika, uses real street names — Ave de Industrielle, Rue Saint Michel, Ave Kabanyo Mute — a real parish, real schools, real transport, and writes about the sexual exploitation of a child by a man claiming diplomatic status. Whatever its relationship to lived experience, it is operating in exactly the territory this technique was built for.

**The draft is already doing this well.** The density of specific unexplained detail is one of its genuine strengths: *mia tano mia tano*; the 16-seater Toyota Hiace; ugali dagaa; PK bubble gum; the fob shaped like a cartoon lion; the fingernails polished every Friday and removed every Monday; the vaccination scar near the elbow rather than the upper arm. None of these is explained. All of them imply a world that existed before the reader arrived. **This is the story's most professional quality and it is not currently listed as a strength anywhere in the vault.**

Three places the technique can go further:

**1. The clue list is too efficient.** As noted in [[All the Small Things — Grief Through the Minor Object]], six of the seven items in Daudi's opening catalogue are evidentiary. Clark's method requires *noise* — details of equal specificity that lead nowhere. A list where every item is load-bearing reads as constructed; a list where two items are simply true reads as observed. This single change improves camouflage, characterisation and [[The Judging Rubric — Nine Axes|axis 3]] simultaneously.

**2. The framing device is available and unused.** Kalemie has a governing metaphor — the lake, which swallows things and keeps secrets — with exactly the multi-readability Clark used invisibility for. But the draft currently *authorises* a reading: D'juma explains what the lake means, and Daudi confirms he is beginning to understand. That closes the frame. Cutting the explanation (as [[Kalemie — Standing Diagnosis|the diagnosis already requires]] for structural reasons) also restores the framing device to full ambiguity. **The same deletion buys two things.**

**3. Double meanings are underused given how available they are.** The story's central irony is already structural — D'juma refuses a birthday cake as a Western imposition, and his daughter wishes to become Kim Kardashian. That is a double meaning at the level of scene. At sentence level the draft rarely does it. *Not everything falls apart* is the clearest instance and it works. Djamela's *"See?"* after the twenty seconds is the other — and its ambiguity is currently the good kind, because the frame around it is what needs fixing, not the line.

**One caution specific to this material.** Clark's technique disguises *which details are real*. It does not resolve **what the story is asking the reader to witness** — and for Scene 6, where the characters are thirteen, those are different questions. Camouflage applied to an unresolved scene produces evasion, not restraint. Resolve Scene 6 first (see [[The ELIZA Effect and the Vestments of Plausibility]]), then camouflage. Order matters here.

## Questions for AI

1. Identify every specific unexplained detail in the draft. Which are load-bearing and which are pure texture? If the ratio is above 50% load-bearing, the world will read as constructed.
2. Invent five details of Kalemie life at the same specificity as *"fingernails polished every Friday and removed every Monday"* that serve no plot function whatsoever.
3. After D'juma's explanation of the lake is cut, list every reading of the lake metaphor that becomes available again.
4. Find three places where a line could carry a second meaning without any added words — only a change of phrasing.
5. Check that no detail in the draft is doing camouflage work for Scene 6 while Scene 6 remains unresolved.
