# THE ELIZA EFFECT AND THE VESTMENTS OF PLAUSIBILITY

> Source: Lynda Clark, *Towards "Creativity Amplification"*, drawing on Joseph Weizenbaum (1966) and Wolfgang Iser (1974)

## The Original Observation

Joseph Weizenbaum built ELIZA at MIT in the 1960s — a program that reflected fragments of the user's own sentences back at them, framed as a psychotherapist. He found it genuinely difficult to convince some users they were talking to a machine.

His explanation is the phrase worth keeping: the human speaker, he wrote, contributes much to clothe the program's responses in **"vestments of plausibility."** The program supplies almost nothing. The listener supplies the coherence, the intention, the personality — and then experiences their own contribution as something they received.

Clark tests this on herself throughout the paper. She reads a personality into a chatbot, and finds on later inspection that the exchange she remembered as a continuous conversation was a series of unconnected keyword matches. Sharp's actors find a love triangle in a script that has none.

## The Turn Clark Makes

Then she turns the observation on fiction, and this is the sentence that makes the paper worth reading. Readers, she notes, *want* to be impressed — and if you leave them the room to have that experience, they generally will, whether or not the prose has strictly earned it.

This is uncomfortable and it is true. It is also the strongest available argument for restraint, and it converges exactly with two things already in this vault:

- **Iser**, whom Clark cites: the literary work comes into existence at the meeting of text and reader. The reader shades in the outlines. The text that leaves no outlines to shade has no reader participation and therefore, in Iser's sense, is not fully a work.
- **Gardner's** [[The Vivid and Continuous Fictional Dream|fictional dream]]: the dream is constructed in the reader's head, not on the page. Explanation is what wakes them, because it does the constructing for them and leaves them nothing to do.

## The Principle

**The reader is not a receiver. The reader is a co-author who will work for free, and will credit you for their own labour.**

Two operational consequences:

1. **Under-supply on purpose.** A withheld detail recruits the reader; a supplied one dismisses them. The dread in a scene is manufactured almost entirely by what the text declines to say.
2. **Do not confuse this with vagueness.** The reader can only furnish a space that has been clearly *bounded*. Weizenbaum's users filled in a psychotherapist because ELIZA was framed as one — the frame was rigid and the content empty. Vagueness gives the reader no frame and no content, and produces nothing. **Precise frame, empty centre.**

That distinction is the whole difference between restraint and evasion, and it is the test [[Kalemie — Standing Diagnosis|the diagnosis applies to Scene 6]].

## For Kalemie

### The story already does this once, superbly

The opening withholds Djamela's name. A boy will not meet Daudi's eyes; a crowd stands with its back to something; the shrug is described as the shrug of someone who knows but will not be first to say a name. **The frame is rigid — a specific person is dead and everyone present knows who — and the centre is empty.** The reader names her, and the naming is theirs.

That is precise frame, empty centre, executed exactly. Nothing needs to be added.

The thin envelope is the same technique at the level of an object. Nobody says what happened in the back room. Djamela's posture and the envelope's reduced thickness are the entire frame, and the reader supplies the transaction. The draft's own callout asks whether this needs "one more sentence of Daudi processing what he's seeing." **It does not.** Adding that sentence would take the reader's work away and hand it back to them as information. This note exists partly to answer that question: no.

### Where the technique fails in the draft

**Scene 6.** The frame is not rigid. What is empty is not a bounded space the reader can furnish — it is the basic question of what physically occurred between two thirteen-year-olds, and the prose becomes least controlled precisely where the meaning is least determined. Compare with the opening: there, the reader knows exactly what kind of fact is missing (a name) and can supply it. Here the reader does not know what kind of fact is missing.

**This is the distinction between restraint and evasion, and Scene 6 is currently on the wrong side of it.** The fix is not to explain more. It is to make the frame rigid — establish precisely what kind of thing happened — and then leave the centre as empty as the envelope scene does.

**Scene 1 and Scene 4.** The reverse failure: too much supplied. D'juma's lake speech is explicated across three paragraphs, and Djamela states her thesis about conquering like the Europeans directly. Both hand the reader a finished interpretation. There is nothing to shade in, so there is no participation, so there is no plausibility to clothe. The draft's own instinct — asking whether Scene 4's irony is "too neat" — is detecting exactly this.

### The uncomfortable corollary

Clark's point cuts both ways, and it is worth stating plainly: **a reader predisposed to be impressed will be impressed by the story's weakest lines too.** *"The bioluminescent stream of pure everything"* will read as profound to a generous reader.

A Commonwealth judge on story 140 of 200 is not a generous reader. They have spent their goodwill. The vestments of plausibility are woven by readers who have energy to spare, and the whole design of [[The First-Page Cut|first-round judging]] is to produce readers who do not. Write for the reader who is not helping you, and the generous reader will still be delighted.
