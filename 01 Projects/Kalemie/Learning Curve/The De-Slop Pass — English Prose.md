# THE DE-SLOP PASS — ENGLISH PROSE

> **A revision protocol, not a rubric.** Nothing here is scored out of anything. It produces edits or it produces nothing.

> [!important] Not applied to any current draft — built for future projects
> **Chantal, Found Things, Beat of My Hurt and the master draft have NOT been run through this**, by instruction. This is tooling for work that comes after. Running it on a draft already in submission format would only invite a rewrite nobody asked for.
>
> The one already-documented Chantal fault it would catch — the over-composure named in [[The Apprenticeship — Learning By Hand]] — is recorded there and needs no second opinion.

## Provenance

Adapted from the `story-deslop` skill in **oh-story-claudecode** (worldwonderer, MIT), a Chinese web-novel toolkit. **Its rules transfer; its word lists do not** — those match Chinese constructions and will not fire on English.

Its core belief, translated, is worth having in the vault verbatim:

> **AI-ness is not grammatical error. It is over-smoothness, over-symmetry, over-explanation.**

That was arrived at independently, in another language, from commercial genre fiction — and it is the same fault [[The Apprenticeship — Learning By Hand|the apprenticeship note]] names in literary prose. Two traditions, one diagnosis. That makes it worth trusting.

## The Four Laws

**1 · Change the fewest words for the biggest effect.**
Fix one word rather than a sentence. Cut one sentence rather than rewrite a paragraph. Leave untouched anything that is not faulty. **Always preserve:** names, places, numbers, dialogue content, proper nouns.

**2 · Deletion caps, by severity.**

| Severity | Maximum cut |
| --- | --: |
| Light | 15% |
| Moderate | 25% |
| Heavy | 35% |

Past the cap you are no longer de-slopping, you are rewriting. **Never delete a whole paragraph** — work sentence by sentence. Before cutting anything, confirm it carries no plant, hook, callback, character fact or causal anchor.

**3 · When unsure, flag — do not cut.**
Mark it `[needs review]` and move on. Uncertain material stays on the page. This single rule prevents the pass from eating a draft.

**4 · Change how it is said, never what is said.**
Plot, character, direction and timeline are out of scope. If the prose reveals a logic problem, that is a different pass on a different day.

> [!danger] What this pass will never do
> **It does not chase detector scores.** No claiming a percentage, no padding, no deliberate typos, no scrambling punctuation to defeat a checker. Those tactics damage the prose and they are dishonest, and a story that needs them has a bigger problem than its adverbs.
>
> This pass improves how a sentence reads. That is all it promises.

## The Patterns

**These are my observations, not an empirical corpus study**, and they will age as models change. Treat the table as a starting hypothesis you revise, not scripture.

### Tier 1 — structural, and the ones that actually matter

| Tell | What it looks like | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Every paragraph lands** | Each one ends on a completed, resolved beat | Let one in six end flat, mid-thought, or on a fact |
| **The summarising last sentence** | The paragraph shows something, then a final sentence explains what it showed | Cover the last sentence of each paragraph. If it reads better without, it goes |
| **The named emotion** | Grief is shown, then labelled — *she felt a quiet grief* | Delete the label. The showing was the work |
| **Tricolon everywhere** | Three-item lists as the default rhythm — *the noise, the heat, the smell of it* | Count them. More than one per 500 words is a metronome. Make some two, some four |
| **The antithesis snap** | *It wasn't X. It was Y.* | **See the note below — this one is conditional** |
| **Metronomic contrast** | Long sentence, short punchy sentence, on a schedule | Vary the interval, not the lengths |
| **No dead detail** | Every object is load-bearing; nothing is just there | Add details that solve nothing. See [[All the Small Things — Grief Through the Minor Object]] |

> [!success] On the antithesis — the pattern is not banned, the *repetition* is
> *It was not shame. It was arithmetic.* is the best line in Chantal and it should never be touched. One antithesis at a peak is rhetoric. Three across a story is a machine tic, and the reader feels it as a mannerism without being able to name it.
>
> **The rule is a budget, not a ban: one per story, spent at the highest point.** Draft 3's craft audit records "two closed antitheses opened" — that instinct was already correct and this table is only formalising it.

### Tier 2 — lexical, cheap to catch

Search for these. Most are deletable outright.

`a mixture of` · `a kind of` · `something like` · `not unlike` · `somewhere between` · `there was something [adj] about` · `a flicker of` · `a ghost of a` · `a hint of` · `the weight of` · `in that moment` · `couldn't help but` · `as if to say` · `a testament to` · `the air was thick with` · `little did` · `palpable` · `visceral` · `liminal` · `ineffable` · `utterly` · `practically` · `truly`

**The `a kind of` family is the biggest single win.** It is hedging — the writer approximating instead of choosing. Delete the hedge and commit to the noun.

### Tier 3 — punctuation and rhythm

- **Em-dash density.** More than one per 300 words reads as machine cadence. Convert to full stops or commas.
- **Ellipsis for hesitation.** Use an action, a short sentence, or a line break instead.
- **Fragments on a schedule.** Fragments are fine. Fragments arriving at regular intervals are not.
- **Semicolons in close narration.** Usually a sign the prose is thinking in written rather than spoken register.

## Running It

Forty minutes, printed, in this order. **Do not open all three tiers at once** — one pass per tier or you will fix nothing well.

| | Pass | Method |
| :--: | --- | --- |
| **1** | **Read aloud, pen down** | Pencil-dot only: where you ran out of breath, stumbled, or got bored. This finds more than tiers 2 and 3 combined |
| **2** | **Tier 1 — structural** | Cover the last sentence of every paragraph. Count your tricolons and antitheses |
| **3** | **Tier 2 — lexical** | Search each term. Delete or commit |
| **4** | **Tier 3 — punctuation** | Count em-dashes and ellipses per 300 words |
| **5** | **Cap check** | Measure what you cut against the severity cap. Over it? Restore the least certain cuts |

### The three counts, done by hand

```
Words: ____
Em-dashes: ____        target: < 1 per 300 words
Tricolons: ____        target: < 1 per 500 words
Antitheses: ____       target: 1 per story, at the peak
Paragraphs ending on a landed beat: ____ / ____
     target: not all of them
[needs review] flags left open: ____
```

> [!warning] The self-defeating risk — read before using this twice
> [[The Funhouse Mirror — AI Output as Cliché Detector|The Funhouse Mirror]] says whatever the machine produces by default is the cliché. **A banned-list has the same trap running backwards:** apply it hard enough and every writer using it converges on the same de-slopped voice, which becomes the next cliché.
>
> **Use it as a detector, never as a style.** It tells you where to look. What goes in the gap is yours, and if the replacement sounds like the replacement anyone else would have made, you have moved the problem rather than solved it.
