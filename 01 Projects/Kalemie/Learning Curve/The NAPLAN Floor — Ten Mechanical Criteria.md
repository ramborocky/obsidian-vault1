# THE NAPLAN FLOOR — TEN MECHANICAL CRITERIA

> "All statements in the Category descriptor column must be evidenced for the script to be allocated that score."
> — NAPLAN Narrative Writing Marking Guide, *Quick Reference Companion*, QCAA 2023

## What This Is, And What It Is Not

The ACARA/QCAA **NAPLAN Narrative Writing Marking Guide** is the instrument used to score narrative writing by Australian schoolchildren in Years 3, 5, 7 and 9 — the same ten criteria at every year level. Source: *Quick Reference Companion*, Queensland Curriculum & Assessment Authority, January 2022, released under CC BY 4.0.

**It is not a prize rubric and must not be used as one.** A competent adult literary writer hits its ceiling on most criteria automatically; it was built to separate a nine-year-old from a thirteen-year-old, not a longlist from a shortlist. Used as a benchmark it will flatter this project every single time.

**Its value is the opposite of the Nine Axes.** [[The Judging Rubric — Nine Axes|The Nine Axes]] is entirely literary judgement — opening authority, the gap, controlling idea, something to say — and contains **not one mechanical criterion**. NAPLAN is more than half mechanics. Five of its ten criteria describe faults the Nine Axes cannot see, cannot score, and will happily award 40/45 while ignoring:

| NAPLAN criterion | Covered by the Nine Axes? |
| --- | --- |
| 1 Audience | ≈ axis 1 + 9, coarser |
| 2 Text structure | ≈ axis 4 + 6 + 8, coarser |
| 3 Ideas | ≈ axis 7 + 9, coarser |
| 4 Character and setting | ≈ axis 3, coarser |
| **5 Vocabulary** | partially — axis 2 |
| **6 Cohesion** | **no axis** |
| **7 Paragraphing** | **no axis** |
| **8 Sentence structure** | **no axis** |
| **9 Punctuation** | **no axis** |
| **10 Spelling** | **no axis** |

Use it as the **hygiene pass** — a floor, run last, when the literary work is done and the question is whether anything embarrassing is still on the page. Not as a measure of whether the story is good.

## The Ten Criteria

Zero counts as a score in every range, so Audience 0–6 has seven possible values.

| # | Criterion | Range | Skill focus | Top score requires |
| --- | --- | --: | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Audience** | 0–6 | Capacity to orient, engage and affect the reader | Precise and *sustained* language choices and narrative devices, plus understanding of the reader's anticipated values and expectations |
| 2 | **Text structure** | 0–4 | Orientation, complication and resolution organised effectively | Effective plot devices beyond simple structure; unexpected forms permitted |
| 3 | **Ideas** | 0–5 | Creation, selection and crafting of ideas | Ideas skilfully used to explore a theme with real substance |
| 4 | **Character and setting** | 0–4 | Portrayal of character; sense of place, time, atmosphere | Sustained characterisation *with introspection*; setting never unclear at any point |
| 5 | **Vocabulary** | 0–5 | Range and precision of language choices | Word choice is a major reason the script is engaging; natural, articulate, matched to genre |
| 6 | **Cohesion** | 0–4 | Control of threads across the whole text — referring words, substitution, word association, connectives | Devices used *deliberately* across an extended text; continuity of ideas — the reader is told what they need to know **at the right moment** |
| 7 | **Paragraphing** | 0–2 | Segmenting text to help the reader negotiate the narrative | All paragraphs correct; no block that should be split, no break that should be joined |
| 8 | **Sentence structure** | 0–6 | Grammatically correct, structurally sound, meaningful | Precise meaning, consistently effective, slips only — no errors |
| 9 | **Punctuation** | 0–5 | Correct and appropriate punctuation | **All** applicable punctuation correct, very occasional omission only |
| 10 | **Spelling** | 0–6 | Accuracy and difficulty of words used | Correct simple, common and difficult words plus at least two challenging words; slips only |

**Maximum: 47.** Criteria 5–10 total 28 of those 47 — which is why this instrument is a floor and not a ranking.

Three structural rules worth stealing regardless of the rest:

- **Every statement in a descriptor must be evidenced.** No partial credit for nearly. This is stricter than the Nine Axes, which lets a generous reader average.
- **Off-genre caps you.** A script of high quality in the wrong form cannot score above 2 in Audience and takes 0 in Text structure and Character and setting. The prize equivalent is the word ceiling: 5,000 words is a genre boundary, not a suggestion.
- **Resolution, operationally defined.** *"A general sense of an ending that leaves the reader adequately satisfied. High quality scripts may include a phase following the climax in which the character/s reflect or move on."* This is the clearest definition of a working ending anywhere in this vault — clearer than [[Climax and Conclusion — The Story in Six Words|Barrett's]], because it names the post-climax phase as a feature rather than a fault.

## Overrule It On Three Things

The same treatment [[Short Story Writing by Charles Raymond Barrett|Barrett]] gets, and for the same reason — a source can be useful and wrong at once.

**1. Punctuation.** The guide states outright that it *"strongly emphasises punctuation that is conventionally correct, even at the higher score levels"* and explicitly declines to allow the scope for personal style it concedes exists in skilled writing. This is the one criterion with no room for a literary decision, and it is the criterion Chantal deliberately breaks. Overruled — but see the finding below, because the flag is still worth having.

**2. Standard Australian English.** Criterion 8 requires it, with an exception only for *"slang or other incorrect speech intentionally depicted"* — i.e. dialect is tolerated as characterisation, never as narration. Kalemie's Swahili-French-Lingala code-switching *is* the narration. This is Barrett's contempt for dialect wearing a 2023 lanyard. Overruled.

**3. The whole ceiling.** Vocabulary tops out at "natural and articulate"; Ideas at "explore a theme". These are entry conditions for a literary submission, not achievements. Never read a NAPLAN top score as a strong result — read it as *no fault found*.

## For Kalemie

**Read this correctly.** Near-ceiling was the predicted result and it means almost nothing about the story's chances. The instrument has done its job if it found faults, not if it produced a number. It found three.

### Finding A — the unquoted dialogue

Chantal carries no quotation marks on any dialogue, and no question marks on questions inside it: *Do you understand entropy, I said.* / *What is Kim Kardashian, my father said.* The style is consistent across the whole draft, which is the difference between a decision and a mistake.

NAPLAN caps this. Criterion 9 is the single place the guide refuses stylistic licence, and this draft walks straight into it. **The right response is to overrule the rubric, not the draft** — but the flag is still worth holding, because a Commonwealth first reader is not bound by NAPLAN and unquoted dialogue is a live reader-risk with anyone. The relevant question is not *is it correct* but *is it load-bearing*: it should be doing something the quoted version could not. Here it is — a girl narrating speech she has already decided how to remember, with no typographic boundary between what was said and what she is telling you. Keep it. Know why.

### Finding B — *shemeji*

> He gave me a coin off my fare once and called me shemeji. That is the whole of what he did.

Every other unglossed term recovers from context: *dagaa*, *dala dala*, *boda boda*, *khanga*, *mia tano*, *délestage*, *Mama alisema*. ***Shemeji* does not.** A reader without Swahili gets "he called me a word" — and this is the line carrying why Morisho is the one who will be taken. The whole ending turns on a familiarity the reader cannot feel.

This is precisely NAPLAN's cohesion clause — *the reader is told what they need to know at the right moment* — and it is a fault the Nine Axes has no axis to catch. It does not need a translation, which would kill it. It needs the sentence around it to do one word's more work.

### Finding C — the French proper nouns

*Ave de Tanganyique* and *Ave de Industrielle* are both irregular as French: the second in particular would conventionally be *Avenue de l'Industrie* or *Avenue Industrielle*, and *de Industrielle* has neither elision nor a noun. *Jean Luc* is normally hyphenated.

I have not been able to verify Kalemie's actual street naming, so this may be deliberate local usage rather than an error — but it sits in a francophone story that a francophone judge may well read. **Worth checking against a real source before submission, not fixing on my say-so.** NAPLAN's spelling criterion would never catch it, since proper nouns are exempt; that it surfaced here at all is incidental.

**What this instrument did and did not do.** It opened an eleven-point gap between the master draft and everything that came after it, and then went almost completely flat across the three finished pieces — one point of spread on 47. That is the correct behaviour for a floor check and the proof that it is not a ranking. Do not use these totals to choose a submission. Use [[The Judging Rubric — Nine Axes|the Nine Axes]] for that.

It discriminated in exactly one place among the finished three, and that place is a criterion the Nine Axes cannot see.

### Found Things — the untranslated inciting incident

Cohesion **3/4**, the only sub-ceiling score in the three finished drafts. Criterion 6 at score 4 requires *continuity of ideas — the reader is told what they need to know at the right moment.*

> Wamemchukua, he said, and tipped his chin at the police truck parked crooked on the verge. Polisi wamemchukua Morisho asubuhi. Wanasema anahusika na kifo cha msichana.

Three unglossed Swahili sentences carrying the **entire inciting incident** — that a girl is dead and Morisho has been taken for it. Everything unglossed elsewhere in this draft is a single recoverable noun (*dagaa*, *khanga*, *mama ntilie*) or is glossed by the sentence after it, as the boda boda calls are. This is not that. A reader without Swahili gets a police truck and a shrug, and then waits until *They buried her on a Thursday* — roughly two-thirds of the way in — for confirmation that anyone has died at all.

The opening's tension is built on a fact the reader does not have. Compare [[The Judging Rubric — Nine Axes|the Nine Axes]], which would score this passage highly on axis 1 and has no mechanism to notice the problem at all. **Same class of fault as Chantal's *shemeji*, an order of magnitude larger.**

Not an argument for translating it. An argument for Daudi's next paragraph carrying, in his own register, the one fact the Swahili is holding.

### The Beat of My Hurt — two things to look at

Ceiling everywhere except punctuation, and the same unquoted-dialogue reason. Two flags, neither of which changed a score:

**The currency.** *Fifteen thousand for the house, fifteen for the walk*, then *the man paid it at the door in dollars*, then *the second fifteen thousand, dollars again*. Thirty thousand Congolese francs is roughly ten to fifteen US dollars — a modest fee, which is the entire moral weight of the transaction: a family buys the version of how their son died for very little. A reader who takes "fifteen thousand… in dollars" at face value reads a fortune and gets the scene exactly backwards. This is the one place I hesitated over the cohesion score. It is cheap to fix and expensive to leave.

**The first sentence.** *There are eleven beats for the dead and me, I know nine.* The *and me* leaves the clause without a clean parse, and it is the first thing anyone reads. It may be deliberate oral cadence — the rest of the draft is controlled enough that I am not assuming error — but it is worth a conscious decision rather than an inherited one. Everything else in criterion 8 is clean.

### The master draft — where the eleven points went

34/47, and the drop is concentrated in exactly the criteria the Nine Axes has no axis for. Its 24/45 there was driven by structure; its 34/47 here is driven by mechanics. **Two different verdicts, both correct, on two different faults.**

- **Text structure 2/4.** The heaviest single loss. Criterion 2 names resolution as a required component of a 0–4 scale, so a missing ending costs two points of four rather than one axis of nine. Proportionally this is a much harsher reading of the missing ending than [[The Judging Rubric — Nine Axes|the Nine Axes]] gives it — and the more honest one.
- **Vocabulary 3/5.** Score 5 requires prose *free of signs of misunderstanding the nuance of mature expressions.* *Squirmish* is not a word. *Désvu* is not a word. *Biodegradable odour*, *the bioluminescent stream of pure everything*, *a new signature vibe*, *trying to sound like my everyday vocabulary* — the register lurch [[Kalemie — Standing Diagnosis|the Standing Diagnosis]] already names, scored.
- **Sentence structure 4/6.** Errors rather than slips, by the guide's own distinction. *The thoughts of holding things together was the subject.* *Djamela took her mother's figure, she was tall and beautiful just like her mother.* Several fragments with no main clause (*Listening to his broken lines of French and street Congolese Lingala.*).
- **Cohesion 3/4.** The name is spelled **D'jamela and Djamela throughout, often in the same paragraph**. Under criterion 6 that is referring-word inaccuracy, and it is pervasive rather than occasional. Tense also lurches: *The down part of town is where we resided.*
- **Ideas 4/5.** One checkable error of fact: *like Leopold never wanting to leave Brazzaville.* Brazzaville was the French Congo capital, named for Brazza. Leopold's was Léopoldville. The image points at the wrong empire. Worth knowing if any of this material is ever recovered from the archive for the cycle.
- **Punctuation 3/5** — and this is the finding worth keeping. The master draft uses conventional quotation marks throughout and still scores 3, the same as the three drafts that use none. **One is a style decision and the other is comma splices.** A rubric that emphasises conventional correctness cannot tell them apart, which is the clearest possible demonstration of why criterion 9 gets overruled and not obeyed.

## Where This Sits In The Workflow

Run it **once, last**, after [[The Five Passes]] and never before. Its criteria 5–10 are the final pass of the five under another name, and running a mechanics check on prose that may still be cut is exactly the error [[The Five Passes]] exists to prevent — *never polish a sentence in a scene you might cut.*

The number goes in [[Craft Log]] beside the Nine Axes number, and the two are not comparable and should never be summed.

## Questions for AI

1. Score the current draft on all ten criteria without reading the scores above, then report only the criteria where you score below ceiling. If you score everything at ceiling, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a fault — a floor check that always finds something is not a floor check.
2. Finding B: rewrite the *shemeji* sentence so a reader with no Swahili feels the familiarity without being told the meaning. Three versions, none longer than the original.
3. Finding A: quote the four dialogue exchanges that would be *most* damaged by adding conventional quotation marks, and the two that would be least. If any exchange would be unharmed, the style is not load-bearing everywhere and that is worth knowing.
4. Apply criterion 6's continuity test line by line: find every point where the reader is told something *later* than they needed it. *Shemeji* is one. List the others.
5. Criterion 6 was the only discriminator among the three finished drafts, and it caught the same fault twice at different sizes — *shemeji* in Chantal, the Swahili block in Found Things. Run criterion 6 alone across all three and report every unglossed term ranked by how much plot it is carrying. That ranking is the actual deliverable of this rubric.
6. [[The Beat of My Hurt (Draft 4 — Free)|Beat of My Hurt]] ties Chantal at 45 on mechanics while sitting outside the Kalemie cycle entirely. Score it on [[The Judging Rubric — Nine Axes|the Nine Axes]] and report whether the mechanical tie survives contact with the literary instrument — see [[CHECKLIST — Getting The Beat to Chantal's Level]].
