# ALL THE SMALL THINGS — GRIEF THROUGH THE MINOR OBJECT

> Source: Lynda Clark, *All the Small Things: Depicting the Randomisation of Grief in (Digital) Short Fiction* (*Short Fiction in Theory and Practice* 12:1) — [InGAME, University of Dundee](https://github.com/IngameDundee/CreativityAmplification)

**The most directly applicable note in this knowledge base. Kalemie is a grief story, and this is a paper about how grief actually enters a text.**

## The Origin

Clark's sister died of cancer in 2019. Her account of it is the argument: at the hospital bed and at the funeral she found she could not grieve — she was struck instead by how funny her sister still was, and afterwards assumed the funeral had discharged the obligation.

Months later she found a birthday card her sister had sent her. The handwriting undid her completely.

She names the absurdity directly — being levelled by a tiny loss rather than the enormous obvious one — and then makes the craft observation that matters: this is not an anomaly. It is how grief typically arrives. **The small thing carries what the large thing cannot**, because the large loss is too big to be perceived at once, while the small object is exactly the right size to be held.

## The Principle

- **Grief is non-linear, random, and unranked.** There is no hierarchy in which a funeral outranks a birthday card. A story that ranks them is lying about the experience.
- **The invisible attachment surfaces only through loss.** You do not know the handwriting matters until it is the last of it. The object is unremarkable until the moment it is the only remaining evidence.
- **Therefore the object must be planted while it is still unremarkable.** Its meaning is created retroactively, by absence. If it arrives already freighted, the effect is impossible — the reader has been told what to feel about a thing that has not yet lost anything.

This last point is the technique, and it is a **plant-and-detonate structure**: introduce the object with no emotional weight at all, let it recur in ordinary contexts, then let the loss make it unbearable without a single word of commentary.

## The Second Principle — Refuse The Single Correct Grief

Clark's worry after publishing her first grief story was that by framing loss through one couple's experience, she had implied a right way to grieve. Her response was structural: in her interactive piece *Uncle*, the bereaved man's activities are drawn at random from a pool — sorting snacks into constituent parts, walking repeatedly through a cloud of perfume — with every option equally probable. Because the system makes no ranking, no behaviour is marked as the healthy one.

The transferable version for non-interactive fiction: **show more than one grief in the same story, and decline to arbitrate between them.** The instant a narrative marks one mourner as grieving correctly, it becomes prescriptive, and prescriptive grief reads as false to anyone who has done it differently.

## For Kalemie

### The motherboard is already the birthday card

[[Dashboard|The Dashboard]]'s Critical Fix #2 — *"Make motherboard mean something — It belonged to Djamela. Daudi doesn't know yet"* — is exactly this structure, arrived at independently. Clark's paper is the theoretical justification for it, and it sharpens the execution in three specific ways:

**It must stay unremarkable on first appearance.** In the current opening the motherboard appears in a list — *the motherboard, the hubcap, the antelope skull* — as one of three found objects on Daudi's wall. This is close to perfect and should not be improved. It is unweighted, ordinary, and grouped with objects that carry no charge. Do not let a single adjective attach to it. The whole detonation depends on the plant being genuinely inert.

**Daudi not knowing is the mechanism, not a plot device.** The Dashboard flags this almost as an afterthought. It is the entire point: Clark did not know the birthday card mattered until she found it. Daudi's ignorance is what makes the object honest.

**The ending already uses this shape — check it does not use it twice.** The planned ending is *Daudi finds the envelope, nails it to the wall, smiles*. That is a minor-object detonation. So is the motherboard. Two in one story is one too many unless they are deliberately the same gesture — in which case nailing the envelope beside the motherboard is the strongest available ending, because the wall becomes the place where Daudi keeps the evidence of things that happened before he arrived. See [[The Resonant Close]] on elements demanding return.

### The vaccination scar problem

The opening's list of clues — the fingernails, the vaccination scar, the cologne, the lion key fob, the `#blessed` selfie — is Daudi managing grief by cataloguing, which is a genuinely fine piece of characterisation.

But every one of those objects is **evidentiary**. Each is a clue pointing at what happened to Djamela. None of them is a small thing that simply *hurts*. Clark's distinction is exactly here: the birthday card told her nothing about her sister's death. It was not evidence. It was only unbearable.

The strongest item on the list is the one that breaks the pattern — Djamela writing *Daudi* in the dust inside her locker and erasing it before she left. That is not a clue. It is a small thing that hurts. It is the last item, and the draft is right to give it the final position.

**The revision instruction:** the story currently has one non-evidentiary object and six clues. It needs two or three more of the first kind — details that solve nothing. A judge reading a list of six clues sees a detective story. A judge reading a list where one item solves nothing sees a boy who is not actually investigating, and that is [[The Judging Rubric — Nine Axes|axis 3]] moving from 4 to 5.

### Two griefs, unranked

D'juma has lost a daughter. Daudi has lost the girl he loved. Djamela's mother appears in the birthday scene and then effectively vanishes from the draft.

Clark's warning applies directly: the story currently routes all grief through Daudi and risks presenting his stunned cataloguing as *the* response. One or two lines showing D'juma or the mother grieving in an entirely different, non-obvious register — without Daudi understanding or approving of it — would cost almost nothing in words and remove the prescriptiveness entirely. Given [[Kalemie — Standing Diagnosis|the 5,000-word ceiling]], "almost nothing in words" matters: this is a 40-word fix.

## Questions for AI

1. List every object in the draft. Mark each as **evidentiary** (points at what happened) or **non-evidentiary** (only hurts). Report the ratio.
2. Propose three non-evidentiary small things from Djamela's ordinary life with Daudi — details that solve nothing and would only become unbearable after her death. Each must be plantable in under 15 words.
3. Check the motherboard's first appearance for any word that pre-weights it. Strip anything that tells the reader it matters.
4. Draft the ending with the envelope nailed beside the motherboard. Does the doubling strengthen the close or dilute it?
5. Write 40 words showing D'juma or Djamela's mother grieving in a register Daudi does not understand and does not endorse. Do not let Daudi interpret it.
