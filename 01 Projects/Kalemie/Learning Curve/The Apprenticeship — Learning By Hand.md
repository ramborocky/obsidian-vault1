# THE APPRENTICESHIP — LEARNING BY HAND

> Six craft bases. Four rubrics. One story with a finished draft and no submission.
> **The bottleneck is not knowledge. Stop reading about craft and start moving your hand.**

## The Honest Diagnosis

This vault can tell you that Chantal loses points on prose control, that the late reframe belongs at ~85%, that Baldwin plants at 20% and detonates at 64%, and that panels forgive architecture and do not forgive voice.

**None of that is a skill. All of it is information.** You can hold every one of those facts and still not be able to produce the sentence, because sentences come from the ear and the hand, and this vault has been feeding the head.

> [!warning] The rule for the next month
> **Close five of the six craft bases.** Open one: [[Prescriptions for the Live Draft|Kiteley]] — the only one that prescribes instead of diagnosing.
>
> **Add no new rubric, no new instrument, no new comparison board.** Four is already two too many. If a fifth suggests itself, that is the addiction talking.

## The Four Methods — in order of how much they teach per hour

### 1 · Copywork — the highest-yield thing on this page

**Type out, by hand, one page of a story you admire.** Not read it. *Type it.* Baldwin's final section. Uwazuruike's opening. One page, twenty minutes.

Franklin taught himself prose this way. It works for a reason nobody has improved on: **you cannot copy a sentence without your hand learning where the writer put the comma, why the short sentence lands after the long one, and how long they were willing to wait.** Reading skips all of that. Your eye is fast and forgiving. Your hand is slow and cannot skip.

**Do it three times a week. Twenty minutes. That is the whole prescription.**

After each one, write **one line only**: *what did my hand notice that my eye did not?*

### 2 · Reading aloud — the cheapest instrument you own

Your own prose, out loud, on paper, standing up.

It catches, for free and instantly: every register lurch, every unglossed word, every sentence that runs out of breath, every place two sentences should be one. It is the only tool in this vault that needs no notes, no score and no rubric — **and it is the one you have never once run on Chantal.**

**Non-negotiable rule: pen down.** If you stop to fix, you stop hearing. Mark the spot with a pencil dot and keep going. Fix afterwards.

### 3 · One technique at a time

You currently hold thirty techniques loosely. **Hold one tightly for a week.**

Week's structure: name the technique · find it in three published stories · write 300 words using only it · read the 300 words aloud · log one line in [[Craft Log]].

The first four weeks, in order — these are chosen from what your own drafts are actually missing:

| Week | Technique | Why this one |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **The unfinished thought** | Every paragraph in Chantal is a completed thought. Real speech is not |
| 2 | **The wrong detail** | A narrator who misremembers once becomes trustworthy everywhere else |
| 3 | **The plant left alone** | Baldwin waits 44% of a story. Your longest wait is 16% |
| 4 | **The scene that is only dialogue** | No interiority allowed. Forces the speech to carry everything |

### 4 · English by mouth, not by grammar

If the confidence gap is English rather than craft — and I think it is — **grammar study will not close it and reading aloud will.**

Twenty minutes daily, out loud, from a writer whose English you want in your mouth. Baldwin. The Uwazuruike sheet has the Nigerian English you already write well. **Fluency is a motor skill.** You do not study it, you rehearse it.

## Chantal — The Humanising Pass

You said it needs to be read and humanised. Both are one pass, and here is what is actually wrong.

> [!important] The fault, stated plainly
> **Djamela is too good at narrating.** Three thousand five hundred words and every single paragraph is a finished thought that lands. Nothing is fumbled, nothing is repeated, nothing is got wrong, no sentence loses its way.
>
> That is why it scores 40/45 on execution and **3/5 on the slide** in [[08 Three Gates — Field Comparison]]. And it is the same fault from both directions: a thirteen-year-old who has just been sold in a back room does not have this much composure for three thousand words, and prose where nothing ever fumbles is what readers now recognise as machine-made.
>
> **The story does not need warmth added. It needs its control to break once, in the right place.**

### Fix 1 · Let the counting fail — the single highest-value change

The worst moment of her life is the back room. Two sentences later she produces a perfect aphorism:

> I came out of that room bent over. […] **It was not shame. It was arithmetic.**

That line is the best in the story and it should stay. **But it arrives too fast.** Nobody has that ready two sentences after the worst thing that has happened to them. It reads as a narrator who has already recovered, which tells the reader she is fine.

**The fix, and it is one paragraph:** she counts everything — so let her count the *wrong* thing. Between coming out bent over and the aphorism, give her one short paragraph where the arithmetic runs on something useless. The slats in the bench. The cracks in the chair. Her own steps to the door. Something with no meaning at all, counted precisely, because the real sum is unbearable and the machine will not switch off.

**Then** the aphorism lands — because the reader has watched her fail to do the one thing she can always do, and she has climbed back up to it.

That single paragraph is the humanising pass. Everything below is smaller.

### Fix 2 · Let her get one thing wrong, late

She already does this once, perfectly, in the third paragraph — *I counted twice, and the first time I got thirty-three.* **The technique is in the story, used once, at the very start.**

Use it once more, after 70%. One detail she gives two versions of, or half-remembers, and does not correct. Real memory is not precise, and a narrator who admits one gap is trusted everywhere else.

### Fix 3 · Two of the unglossed words

Already identified and still open: ***shemeji*** in [[The NAPLAN Floor — Ten Mechanical Criteria|the floor check]]. It carries why Morisho gets taken and a reader without Swahili cannot feel it. Do not translate it — let the sentence around it do one word more work.

### Fix 4 · The read-aloud itself

Print it. Stand up. Read the whole thing out loud, once, pen down, pencil dots only.

**Mark three things and nothing else:**

1. Where you ran out of breath
2. Where you stumbled on your own sentence
3. Where you got bored of your own voice

Then stop. Those three lists are the entire revision. No rubric, no gates, no scoring — you have four instruments already and none of them can hear.

## What Not To Do

- **Do not build a fifth rubric.** Including one that scores this note.
- **Do not re-score Chantal this week.** It has been scored on four instruments in one day. Another number tells you nothing new.
- **Do not add words to fix warmth.** Chantal is 3,569 words with 1,400 of headroom, and the humanising fix above is one paragraph. Warmth is not volume.
- **Do not read a sixth craft book.** [[03 Brian Kiteley|The Kiteley base]] carries the warning already: *this book can become a way of not finishing.* So can all of them.

## The Two Daily Practices

### The Impromptu — 15 minutes, every day

**One page of a new story. Any story. Started and abandoned in fifteen minutes.**

Rules, and they are the whole point:

- **No planning.** Open the file and write the first line before you know the second
- **Never finished.** One page, then stop, even mid-sentence
- **Never revised.** Ever
- **Never promoted.** [[Collection Tracker|The Collection Tracker rule]] holds — **only one story is in active Drafting at a time, and that is Chantal.** These are seeds, not projects

**Why it works:** the reason Chantal took four drafts to find its voice is that every sentence was written under the weight of a prize. Fifteen minutes on something that will never be submitted is the only place you can write badly on purpose, and writing badly on purpose is how the hand gets fast.

**Where they go:** one file, dated entries, filed against [[Collection Tracker]] as trunk material for the cycle. Some will be nothing. One or two will be the next story, and you will not be able to tell which at the time.

**Measured by:** pages written per week. Nothing else. Not quality — **quality is not the metric here and judging them defeats the exercise.**

### The Read — 20 minutes, every day

**One published short story, read properly, start to finish.** Not an extract, not a craft book, not a story sheet. The story.

You have read enormously *about* short stories this month and comparatively few of them. Two are measured on [[02 Comparison Board|the board]] out of a target of ten.

### The Reconstruction — Saturdays, from the week's reading

Pick the strongest story you read that week. **Close it. Then rebuild its skeleton from memory:**

1. Where does it plant the thing it later detonates? At roughly what percentage?
2. What does it never explain?
3. What is the last image, and which earlier image does it drag behind it?
4. Where would you have cut it, and what would that have broken?

**Then open it and check how wrong you were.** The gap between your reconstruction and the real thing is the lesson — it is exactly where your instincts differ from a writer who placed.

> [!success] This is the campaign's stalled pipeline, disguised as practice
> [[02 Comparison Board]] targets **ten stories and has two.** One reconstruction a week is eight weeks to a complete board — and each one produces a story sheet as a by-product rather than as a project.
> **The learning practice and the campaign are the same work.** Do not run them as two things.

## The Week

**Daily, every day:** 🖊 The Impromptu (15 min) · 📖 The Read (20 min)

**Plus one rotating twenty-minute slot:**

| Day | Twenty minutes |
| --- | --- |
| Mon | Copywork — Baldwin |
| Tue | Technique of the week — 300 words |
| Wed | Read aloud — your own |
| Thu | Copywork — a different writer |
| Fri | Technique of the week — 300 words |
| **Sat** | **The Reconstruction** — rebuild the week's story, then check |
| Sun | One line in [[Craft Log]]. Nothing else |

**About 55 minutes a day.** That is the ceiling, not the target.

> [!important] If a day collapses, protect the Impromptu
> Fifteen minutes of your own bad sentences beats an hour of anybody else's good ones. Copywork can be skipped. Reading can be skipped. **The hand must move every day**, because it is the only thing on this page that is a muscle.
