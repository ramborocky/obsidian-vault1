## 60-second version — if you read nothing else

**Four genuinely brilliant moments. Three big losses.**

**Brilliant:** the towel he doesn't take · the invitation card, found not confessed · the mother's grief · the whole *packing-up-as-plot* structure.

**The losses:**

1. **It gives away its own argument in sentence four** — and that sentence is also the title. Nothing left to discover.
2. **The narrator beats his lover half to death at 83%, and the story never mentions it again.** The ending then asks you to find that man adorable.
3. **13 mechanical faults** — including a wrong word in the *first paragraph*, and `Justin Beiber`.

**22/45.** Published by a good magazine, won nothing — which is exactly why it's useful. It shows you where the bar sits.

# § I — the doorway

> [!success] ✅ 1 · THE FIRST LINE IS DOING REAL WORK
> **Cue:** *"Here he is…"*
> Present tense, second person, and you're in the doorway before you've been told anything.
> **Why:** no scene-setting, no weather, no waking up. The story starts *at* the event.
> **Steal:** open at the moment, not at the approach to it.

> [!bug] ❌ 2 · AND THEN IT GIVES THE WHOLE GAME AWAY
> **Cue:** *"…the only way he can genuinely love you."*
> That's the story's entire argument. It's sentence **four**. It's also the **title**.
> **Why it hurts:** a story makes its case by showing you things until *you* conclude it. Announce the conclusion and the next 2,100 words have nothing to prove.
> **And it isn't even true** — the ending disproves it, so the story finishes holding no position at all. **This one choice is why it scores 1/5 on controlling idea.**
> **Fix:** delete the sentence. You lose nothing.

> [!failure] 🔧 3 · A WRONG WORD, IN THE FIRST PARAGRAPH
> **Cue:** *"dry of saliva"*
> Saliva is the thing dryness is the *absence* of. You can be dry, or dry of moisture. Not dry of the wet thing.
> **Why it matters more than it looks:** it's at **2.7%**. A prize reader meets an error before they meet the second character.
> **The most expensive fault in the piece** — not the worst, but the worst-placed.

> [!warning] ⚠️ 4 · PAIN BY INVENTORY
> **Cue:** pain from the belly → crown, temples, kneecaps, between the toes.
> Four body parts listed. Reads as thorough, not as painful.
> **Why:** a reader can only feel one place at a time. A list invites skimming; one exact sensation forces feeling.
> **Rule:** one precise ache beats a tour of the body.

> [!success] ✅ 5 · THE BEST PSYCHOLOGICAL DEVICE IN THE STORY
> **Cue:** what you *intend* to say vs. what your lips actually say. (Twice — the nonchalant shrug, then *pick up your things and leave*.)
> He plans a cool answer. His mouth betrays him.
> **Why it's good:** it shows a man split in half without the word "conflicted." The gap between intention and speech **is** the character.
> **The problem:** used twice, then abandoned for the whole rest of the story.

> [!success] ✅ 6 · THE ONE LINE OF REAL INTERIORITY
> **Cue:** *"who the fuck are you when your feelings take charge?"*
> Register drops from lyrical to blunt, and it lands.
> **Why it works:** the swearing is earned — he's losing control, so the sentence loses control.
> **The contrast is the lesson.** Everything above it is *reported* feeling — *you don't want to shiver, you don't want your hands to tremble.* **That's a list of feelings, not a feeling.** This line is the real thing, and it's nearly the only one.

> [!note] 🎯 7 · NO QUOTATION MARKS — ON PURPOSE
> Every line of speech in this story is unquoted. It never slips.
> **Why that's a decision, not a mistake:** it holds across 2,165 words. Mistakes aren't consistent.
> **Directly relevant to you:** Chantal does the same, and it was flagged as a reader risk. Here's a published writer doing it in a real magazine. **The flag can come down.**

# § II — the wallpapers and the CD rack

> [!tip] 💡 8 · THE ENGINE — THE BEST IDEA IN THE STORY
> **Cue:** he peels the wallpapers, then empties the rack.
> **The structure:** object removed → flashback to what that object meant → repeat.
> **The relationship is dismantled in reverse order of how it was built, on the page, in front of you.** The packing *is* the plot.
> **Steal this outright.** An inventory run backwards is a complete engine, needs no exposition, costs nothing. → worth an [[x-factor]] experiment at full length.
> **What he does wrong:** drops it at § V and never picks it up again.

> [!warning] ⚠️ 9 · SEVEN ARTISTS, NO MEMORY
> **Cue:** the CD list — Backstreet Boys through Sam Smith. Then a second list of film genres, then a third of TV.
> **Why it's weak:** a list is research. A memory is *one* record and what happened when it played.
> **Test:** one CD with a story beats seven with none.

> [!failure] 🔧 10 · TWO FAULTS, SIXTY WORDS APART
> **Cue:** *"porcelain walls"* (12.9%) and *"Justin Beiber"* (13.9%).
> Porcelain is a toilet, a cup, a tile — not a wall. He probably means plaster.
> **And Bieber is misspelled** — inside a list whose whole purpose is to prove this character knows the music.

> [!success] ✅ 11 · THE SMELL SYSTEM — A GENUINELY ORIGINAL INVENTION
> **Cue:** people have distinct smells *if only we listened harder*. He's balsamy. Your mother smells of cherries — **down the phone, from miles away.**
> **Why the cherries line is superb:** it's impossible, and that's the point. It tells you this narrator's love is synaesthetic and a little unhinged, without saying so.
> **This should have been the spine of the story.** Hold that — see notes 21, 38 and the verdict.

> [!bug] ❌ 12 · AND HE KILLS IT WITH A JOKE, EIGHT LINES LATER
> **Cue:** the sister smells of excrement because she's just been dumped.
> Only joke in the paragraph, and it's a fart gag.
> **Why it's fatal:** it tells the reader **the device isn't serious.** Once you signal that, they stop trusting the next smell — and the next two smells are the best moments in the story.
> **Fix:** cut the sister. The system survives.

> [!failure] 🔧 13 · *"Wrestle Mania"*
> One word. Minor alone; it's number four of thirteen.

> [!warning] ⚠️ 14 · DON'T NAME THE TECHNIQUE
> **Cue:** *"unfurling these memories in a vignette"*
> The narrator tells you he's having a montage, while having a montage.
> **Why it leaks:** that's the writer's word, not the character's. Nobody thinks *I am now experiencing a vignette.*
> **Rule:** if a sentence describes the machinery of the story, it belongs to the author — and the reader feels the author standing there.

# § III — the rug

> [!warning] ⚠️ 15 · THE VOICE JUMPS LEVELS
> **Cue:** *"hitherto bleak and silhouetted"* — in an intimate, present-tense, second-person story, three lines from a very physical sex scene.
> **"Hitherto" is a legal-document word.**
> 🎯 **Craft word — register:** the *level* a voice is pitched at. Formal, plain, crude, lyrical. Good writing varies it on purpose.
> **Here it isn't on purpose** — it's a writer reaching for an impressive word. The rubric's definition of a **1** for prose control is literally *"register lurches."* This is why it scores 2.

> [!success] ✅ 16 · THE BEST-BUILT PARAGRAPH ENDING IN THE STORY
> **Cue:** he sings in the kitchen; you sit up, put your head between your knees, and cry.
> **No reason given.** The section just ends.
> **Why it's excellent:** you've been shown a perfect afternoon, then the narrator weeps and won't say why. **The reader does the work** — and a reader who assembles a meaning believes it far harder than one who's handed it.
> **This is the exact opposite of note 2, in the same story, forty lines apart.** He knows how to withhold. He just didn't do it where it counted.

> [!failure] 🔧 17 · *"he winched and grimaced"*
> **Winced.** A winch is a machine for pulling things.

# § IV — the wardrobe, and the towel

> [!success] ✅ 18 · ABSENCE, MEASURED IN HARDWARE
> **Cue:** what's left takes up **twelve hangers** — then: *did I buy more hangers because of him, or were they always there?*
> **Why it works:** grief counted in coat hangers. The question is unanswerable and he doesn't answer it.
> **Your own house rule working** — *objects carry emotion* — for the second time.

> [!failure] 🔧 19 · TWO IN ONE SENTENCE
> **Cue:** *"timberland boots"* and *"Roulette belts"*.
> **Timberland** needs its capital — especially between Gucci and Adidas, which got theirs.
> **"Roulette belts" isn't a thing.** Probably a half-heard brand. In a paragraph built entirely from brand names, getting one wrong undoes all of them.

> [!warning] ⚠️ 20 · THE NARRATOR'S GENDER LANDS HERE — BY ACCIDENT
> **Cue:** *"look guyish for once, babes!"* — at **43%**.
> Until this line many readers have been reading a woman. This is where it clicks that both characters are men.
> **The problem:** a late reveal has to be *aimed*. This one just happens, inside a joke, in a clause about jeans.
> Placed deliberately it's the story's hinge. Left to chance, half your readers spend 43% quietly rebuilding the scene.

> [!success] ✅✅ 21 · THE TOWEL — THE BEST MOVE IN THE STORY
> **Cue:** he takes the soap dish, the slippers, the boxers. **He leaves the towel.**
> Then three questions, none answered: forgotten? left because it smells of him? does he know you'll press it to your face?
> **Why it beats everything else here:** the story never says *he still loves me* or *I won't survive this.* It puts a towel on a rail and lets you do the arithmetic.
> **The single best object measured anywhere in your campaign — better than the 2026 winner's.** It's also the smell system finally paying out. → [[All the Small Things — Grief Through the Minor Object]]

> [!success] ✅ 22 · AN OBJECT THAT KEEPS ACCUSING
> **Cue:** the invitation card, still in the dining room, under *"dust and guilt and sad stories."*
> He doesn't take it either. It's been there for weeks and stays there for the rest of the story.
> **Why it works:** a second object carrying a feeling — and the one thing in the flat neither man will touch.
> **Small wobble:** three items where two would hit harder. *Dust and guilt* is the better sentence.

# § V — the invitation card

> [!warning] ⚠️ 23 · THE SONG DOES THE FEELING FOR HIM
> **Cue:** the Backstreet Boys lyric quoted from the kitchen.
> The lyric is about unconditional love; the scene is a betrayal. Neat irony — **but the song is supplying it, not the story.**
> **Rule:** borrowed emotion is the cheapest kind. Delete the lyric; if the scene goes flat, it wasn't carrying anything.

> [!warning] ⚠️ 24 · SEVEN GUESSES IN A ROW
> **Cue:** *was it the long face, or the twisted lips, or the gait, or the gaze, or the hug, or the kiss, or the voice?*
> Seven possibilities stacked, then two more rhetorical questions after them.
> **Why it drains the moment:** a narrator who lists seven signs has **noticed nothing.** One observed detail — the fork probing the rice like something's buried under it, which is the very next sentence and is **excellent** — does more than the whole list.
> **Fix:** cut all seven. Keep the fork.

> [!success] ✅ 25 · DISCOVERY, NOT CONFESSION — THE BEST STRUCTURAL CHOICE HERE
> **Cue:** he goes to the bathroom; you go into his bag; you find the card.
> **He never tells you.** You dig it out while he hides in another room.
> **Why it beats a confession scene:** the reader finds out in the same second the narrator does — no gap, no warning, no braced posture. And it fixes the boyfriend permanently: *he left it where it would be found, because he couldn't say it.*
> **Steal this.** Bad news discovered always beats bad news announced.

> [!failure] 🔧 26 · *"Can I head up the restroom"*
> No preposition works — *head to*, or just *use*. The sentence doesn't parse.
> **Where:** 53.9% — the last thing the boyfriend says before the story's biggest reveal.

# § VI — the sink

> [!success] ✅ 27 · A REAL SCENE TURN
> **Cue:** you intended to throw him out. You hugged him from behind instead.
> 🎯 **Craft word — a "turn":** a scene turns when it ends somewhere different from where it was heading. Starts angry, ends angry = no turn, and probably not a scene.
> **This one turns properly**, and it turns on him crying — the first crack in him in five months.

> [!failure] 🔧 28 · *"the five months you'd been together"* — REMEMBER THIS NUMBER
> Five months, at **58.2%**, in the story's own words.
> **It breaks at 97.4%.** See note 44.
> **Also:** the wallpapers in § II were *old, dog-eared, soaked in the flat's staleness.* Wallpaper doesn't do that in five months.

> [!warning] ⚠️ 29 · DIALOGUE WITH NO BODIES IN THE ROOM
> **Cue:** the run of one-line exchanges — *Babes? / I'm listening. / Then? / I don't know.*
> Nine exchanges, almost no gesture, no room around them.
> **What it buys:** speed. The acceleration into *you owe me a goddam explanation* works.
> **What it costs:** the scene has no place. Two voices in a white room. **You get one of these, not both** — and this is the moment the story could most afford to slow down.

# § VII — his story

> [!success] ✅ 30 · ONE IMAGE, WHOLE CONDITION
> **Cue:** walking the streets on tiptoe, afraid the earth would break if he pressed too hard.
> **Best single image in the story:** it turns an abstract thing — being closeted in a family that's closing in — into a physical way of walking.
> **And note the compression.** The whole apparatus around it (friends, father, uncle, sisters and their children) is three lines of reported questions. He isn't dramatising it, and he's right not to. **This is what economy looks like.**

> [!note] 🎯 31 · WATCH WHAT JUST HAPPENED TO THE NARRATOR
> This whole section is **the boyfriend's story, reported.** The "you" has effectively left the room.
> **And the prose immediately gets better.** Sharper, less decorated, no lurching.
> **That's a verdict on the second person.** Held until the end — see the verdict.

> [!success] ✅✅ 32 · THE MOTHER — THE BEST WRITING IN THE STORY
> **Cue:** *her husband*, she says — as if the man weren't also his father.
> Then: the small secrets the two of them kept, passed with eyes and hands, laughed over because everyone else was jealous.
> **Why it's extraordinary:** a mother accuses her son of killing his father, and the story answers her not with an argument but with a memory of the same man being tender to the same boy. **Love and violence coming out of the same place** — and the word "grief" never appears.
> **This is the level the rest of the story needed.** It's 100 words long.

> [!bug] ❌ 33 · AND THE MOST IMPORTANT SENTENCE IS UNREADABLE
> **Cue:** *He went to see him… He told him to marry the girl, and then he breathed his last.*
> **Four "he"s. Three different men.** Who told whom? Who died? You have to stop and reconstruct it.
> **Where:** 73.3% — the father's death, the event the entire backstory exists to deliver.
> 🎯 **This is a cohesion fault** — the reader isn't given what they need at the moment they need it. Same family as Chantal's *shemeji* and Found Things' untranslated Swahili — **but bigger, because those hid information and this one breaks the sentence itself.**

> [!warning] ⚠️ 34 · THE BEST-SEEN PERSON IN THE STORY GETS ONE PARAGRAPH
> **Cue:** the fiancée — educated, her eyes done in a careful, overworked perfection — **and she can feel him flinch when she takes his hand.**
> *She knows.* In one clause. Sharpest observation in 2,165 words.
> **And she's never mentioned again.** The story ends with the two men leaving town and spends no word on the woman whose life the plot just decided.
> **The lesson isn't "give her more."** Cutting her entirely is defensible. **But raising someone to that level of seeing and then dropping them is worse than either** — you've shown the reader you can see her, then asked them to stop.
> **Test for Chantal:** is there anyone observed that precisely who then vanishes?

> [!failure] 🔧 35 · *"a heir"*
> **An heir.** Silent H — and it's in the one line carrying the family's whole demand.

# § VIII — the kitchen floor

> [!bug] ❌❌ 36 · THIS IS WHERE THE STORY BREAKS
> **At 83%:** he throws a mug. Then takes the man's head in both hands and beats it against a wall until blood runs warm in his palms. Then **steps back to look** — and with the man already sobbing and not moving, unscrews a fluorescent tube and breaks it across his face.
> **That's attempted murder.** Not a slap, not a moment of passion.
> **The story never refers to it again.** Not the victim, who comes back smiling. Not the narrator, whose head we are inside for every word of this story.
> **17% of the story remains, and it is spent being charming.**

> [!bug] ❌ 37 · AND THE ONE LINE OF EXPLANATION IS THE CHEAPEST AVAILABLE
> **Cue:** *he wanted you to kill him, you thought.*
> **The narrator supplies his victim's consent** — and the story never examines it, questions it, or returns to it.
> **Why this is the fatal move, not the violence:** fiction can go anywhere and show any act. What it cannot do is **not notice.** A story that depicts something ugly is doing its job; a story that hasn't registered what it depicted has lost control of its own material.
> 🎯 **This is what "sentimentality" actually means in craft** — not "too emotional." It means **emotion the story hasn't paid for.** The warm ending is drawn against an account emptied here.

> [!bug] ❌ 38 · THE TENDERNESS THAT ISN'T
> **Cue:** you wake wrapped in his blanket — and he's made tea and left bread.
> The balsam callback lands beautifully as an image, third and final use of the smell system.
> **But look what it's attached to.** He did that *after* you put a fluorescent tube in his face: cleaned up, covered you, made breakfast, left.
> **The story presents this as tenderness.** It shows no sign of knowing it's also the most recognisable pattern in domestic abuse there is. **That's the difference between brave and careless** — nothing here suggests he chose it. → [[The Underground Rubric — Risk, Afterlife and the Room]] is the note for the version that works: **risk taken knowingly.**

# § IX — two weeks

> [!success] ✅ 39 · A SHORT SECTION THAT KNOWS ITS JOB
> **Cue:** the text arrives; you stop reading partway; you throw the phone down and go out for beer.
> Forty words, two weeks passed.
> **The unread message is the whole characterisation** — he can't finish a sentence about himself, and you can't finish reading it.
> **Steal:** the interrupted message. Cheap, fast, does two people at once.

# § X — the keys

> [!warning] ⚠️ 40 · THE THEME, SAID OUT LOUD
> **Cue:** *"I want to do me henceforth, not my father anymore"* / *"That's why I can be easily manipulated—"*
> He announces his own character arc, in dialogue, in the final scene.
> **Why it's weak:** he's just given back the car. **The car already said it.** The line explains an action the reader has understood — the definition of a redundant sentence.
> **Also *henceforth*** — same fault as *hitherto* in note 15. A writer who reaches for a formal word whenever a scene turns emotional.

> [!success] ✅ 41 · THE REVERSAL — GENUINELY GOOD, THE STORY'S BEST AXIS
> **Cue:** *because we're leaving together.* Keys on your lap. A flat in Benin, rented, waiting.
> He wasn't packing to leave you. He was packing to take you with him. **The title's promise inverted in one line.**
> **This is the 4/5 on "the gap"** — its highest mark.

> [!bug] ❌ 42 · BUT IT'S A TWIST, NOT A GAP — AND THAT DIFFERENCE IS THE BALLGAME
> 🎯 **A twist** changes the facts. You go *oh!* and read on.
> 🎯 **A gap** changes the *meaning* of everything you've already read. You go back to page one and find it was all there, waiting.
> **Nothing planted earlier makes this readable in hindsight.** No hint of apartment-hunting, no call to Kessy, no evasion that reads differently second time. He was crying at a sink over a wedding he'd agreed to.
> **The fix costs one sentence, anywhere in § V–IX** — one detail the reader mis-files at the time and re-files at the end. That sentence is the difference between 4 and 5, and between a surprise and a story.

> [!failure] 🔧 43 · THE TIMELINE BREAKS — IN THE STORY'S BIGGEST SENTENCE
> **Cue:** *"all these years"* — at **97.4%**. Against **five months** at 58.2% (note 28).
> **Why it's expensive:** it's not in a description, it's in the line carrying the whole emotional payload — everything he'd longed to hear.
> **The reader is doing arithmetic at the exact moment they should be feeling something.**

> [!failure] 🔧 44 · *"shock-surprise"* — at 99.3%
> Not a word, and not a coinage that earns itself. Second-to-last spoken line.
> **13 faults, and the last lands 25 words from the end.** The hygiene pass was never run.

> [!bug] ❌ 45 · THE LAST LINE — AND IT DECIDES EVERYTHING
> The closing line asks you to find this man delightful.
> **Count the words back to the fluorescent tube: ninety.**
> Ninety words ago one of these men beat the other's head against a wall and broke glass across his face. Nobody has mentioned it. The victim came back smiling with keys. Now the story wants a warm ending.
> **That is not ambiguity. Ambiguity is a choice the writer makes and controls. This is forgetting.**

# 🏁 THE VERDICT

> [!question] Why the ending happened — and this is the useful part
> Brittle Paper filed the story under **"Love story."** The ending is the beat that tag requires.
> So the close is **motivated by the genre, not by the story** — it satisfies the category the piece was published in and abandons the material the piece actually built.
> **That's the failure mode of writing toward a known destination, and it's the one you're most exposed to.** A prize has an expected register too: serious, resonant, the closing image that lands. **Writing the ending you imagine a Commonwealth panel wants is the identical error** to writing the ending a love-story tag wants. → [[The Funhouse Mirror — AI Output as Cliché Detector]]

## The second person earns nothing

Two reasons exist to write *you* instead of *I*:

- **Dissociation** — the self can't own what happened. There is **one** line of this (note 6), never developed.
- **Accusation** — pointing at the reader. There is **none**, in a story whose narrator commits a violent crime and is never once pointed at.

**Swap every *you* for *I* and this story is unchanged.** A device that costs the reader effort and returns nothing should be cut. And remember note 31: **the prose measurably improves in the one section where the "you" isn't there.**

## If you take three things

**1 · Never write your own thesis down.** Note 2 is the entire distance between 1/5 and 5/5 on controlling idea. Yours is *inaction is complicity.* **That sentence, and every paraphrase of it, must never appear in Chantal.**

**2 · The heaviest thing in your story has to be metabolised by your narrator, on the page.** Not resolved — *registered.* Chantal already does this (*it was not shame, it was arithmetic*), which is exactly why you're told to end on the four ships and add nothing.

**3 · Run the mechanical pass even though you'll score near-perfect.** Halim teaches English and has three competitive placements. He published `Beiber` and a wrong word in his first paragraph, through an editor, and it's still there five years on. **Typos aren't a skill signal — they're an attention signal, and they survive skill, prizes and editors.**

## Two things to steal

- 💡 **The backwards inventory** (note 8) — object removed → scene that explains it. A whole engine for a story about an ending. Test it at full length in [[x-factor]], not near the manuscript.
- 💡 **Discovery, not confession** (note 25) — bad news dug out of a bag beats bad news announced, every time.

## And the one thing to beat

**The towel** (note 21). Better than anything in the 2026 winner. Everything else in this story is fixable by deletion; the towel is the only thing in it you couldn't have written by removing something.
