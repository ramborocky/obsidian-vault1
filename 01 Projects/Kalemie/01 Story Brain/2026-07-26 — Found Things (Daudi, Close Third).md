# FOUND THINGS

> [!NOTE] What this draft is and which craft rules built it
> The third-person-close experiment. Not a replacement for [[KALEMIE-Draft_with_AI_Questions|the master draft]] or [[2026-07-26 — Chantal (Djamela POV, Draft 2 — Barrett Pass)|Chantal]] — a third instrument, built to test the POV question the vault has deferred three times.
>
> **Why close third for this material.** [[Methods of Narration — The Author Must Vanish|Barrett's four objections to first person]] are all live on Daudi: he survives, so there is no anxiety for him; he reports knowledge he cannot have; the narrator gets between the story and the reader. But the real argument is positive. Close third can show the reader **more than the character will admit**, which makes Daudi's refusal to know visible on the page while he is still telling himself he does not know. The Controlling Idea is *inaction is complicity*; this POV is the only one that can dramatise it rather than state it.
>
> **Rules applied:** single opening under three paragraphs, one predominant emotion, mystery demoted to a frame so the story runs one action, climax proper in six words, ending within a paragraph of it, all destinies settled including Morisho ([[Short Story Writing by Charles Raymond Barrett|Barrett]]). A circle — begins and ends at the same stop — with the police station written as an iceberg, Daudi given one genuinely unexpected act, every scene carrying a state change, and no closed aphorisms ([[The 3 AM Epiphany by Brian Kiteley|Kiteley]], exercises 188, 186, 193, 80, and [[Writing That Learns, Not Teaches]]). Ages fixed at Daudi 15, Djamela 13; Scene 6 determined and withheld; motherboard planted unweighted ([[Learning Curve]]).
>
> **Title** per [[Titles Good and Bad|Barrett's five tests]] — apt, specific, attractive, new, short, with the aptness suspended until the last page.
>
> **One coordination note:** this draft leaves the motherboard's origin unexplained, so [[2026-07-26 — Chantal (Djamela POV, Draft 2 — Barrett Pass)|Chantal]] keeps that reveal. All three drafts remain shippable; only one can be submitted.

The dala dala was not there.

Daudi stood at the stop with his bag on his shoulder and worked out what else was missing. Morisho's voice, first. The boda bodas were there but idling, arranged wrong, facing the lake instead of the road. A crowd stood at the shoulder with their backs to him, which meant whatever they were looking at was low to the ground.

He asked a boy he half knew where Morisho was.

The boy did not lift his head. Wamemchukua, he said, and tipped his chin at the police truck parked crooked on the verge. Polisi wamemchukua Morisho asubuhi. Wanasema anahusika na kifo cha msichana.

Something went out from under Daudi's feet — the same small private collapse as the day he found the antelope skull in the bush and understood, holding it, that it had been a head.

Msichana gani, he said.

The boy shrugged. It was the shrug of somebody who knows and has decided not to be the first one to say it out loud, and Daudi recognised it because he had used it himself.

He did not ask again. He turned and walked back the way he had come, past the fish stalls and the shuttered pharmacy, and he did not run, and he noticed that he did not run.

---

In the neighbourhood they called him the founder of dead things.

His room was one room, at the back, with a window the size of two hands and a floor that sloped toward the door. Along the wall above the mattress he had nailed the things he had brought home: a hubcap from a Land Cruiser, the antelope skull, a bicycle wheel with eleven spokes, a green motherboard with little gold roads on it going nowhere. His stepmother called it rubbish and had stopped saying so about two years ago.

He had a story ready for anything on that wall. I found it. He had used the story often enough that it came out smooth, and it was true about two-thirds of the time.

He sat down on the mattress without taking his bag off.

The physics book was where he had left it, open at entropy, and he looked at the page for a while without reading it. Order requires energy. Disorder is what happens when nobody is doing anything. He had liked that when he first read it because it sounded like a law about the universe and not about a person.

Then he got up and took the antelope skull off its nail and put it on the floor, and sat back down, and could not have said why.

---

She had turned thirteen in March. Her father had bought a watermelon.

Not a cake. A watermelon with one candle pushed into the top of it, standing crooked, because a watermelon is not built to hold a candle.

Baba, she said. You could have got a cake. Even a cheap one.

Watermelons are healthier, D'juma said. More natural. More African. Cakes are a western thing.

Her mother said watermelons were sweet, like her, in the voice she used when she was helping him be right.

Daudi watched D'juma's hands while he said it. They were a fruit seller's hands, split at the thumbs, and they were busy the whole time with the candle, setting it and resetting it, and they did not stop until it stood.

Make a wish, D'juma said. Blow.

Djamela shut her eyes. To be Kim Kardashian, amen.

What is Kim Kardashian, said D'juma.

A social media influencer, her mother said, quickly, the way you move a knife off a table.

The smoke went up and sat under the iron roof.

Afterwards Daudi and Djamela sat on the porch with the plate between them and looked at the lake and the four rusted ships in it. She said her father thought she wanted things.

He waited. Waiting was a thing he was good at and had not yet understood was a decision.

I do want things, she said. I want to control them. She said the rest of it looking at the ships and not at him, and what she said was that they could beat the Europeans without weapons or degrees, only followers, and Daudi heard how young it sounded and also how exactly she had worked it out from what was in front of her, and he said nothing, because agreeing would have been a lie and disagreeing would have made her stop talking.

The watermelon had been sitting out. It tasted like it was preserving itself in sugar.

---

The car stopped for her in April, at the dagaa stop, in front of everybody.

The boda boda men had already started — mambo mrembo, mototo wa mujini — the way they did, calling it a compliment and meaning a notice. Daudi had his mouth open to say the thing he always said, which was nothing.

Then the horn. Not theirs. And the window came down with a swish and not a clink, and the air changed from fried dagaa to something out of Dubai.

Je m'appelle Jean Luc. Tu es d'où, ma belle.

Rue Saint Michel, Djamela said.

Daudi turned his head to look at her, because it was a lie and she had not paused before it. She was standing very straight. The straightness was new, and he watched her decide to keep it.

Mama alisema — he started.

We shouldn't talk to strangers, said Jean Luc, finishing it for him, and smiled.

The man said he was a director. Consul général de Belgique. His French had a gap in it where the grammar should have been, and Lingala came in to fill the gap, and Daudi heard the gap. He filed it with the other things. He had a place for things like that and it was not a place he went back to.

A ten thousand note went under a piece of paper with a number on it.

Je m'appelle, said Jean Luc.

Chantal, said Djamela.

She said it fast, the way you say a word you have been carrying around, and Daudi understood two things at once and only allowed himself the first. The first was that she had chosen a name. The second he put in the place with the French.

Le petit garçon, Jean Luc said, and the window went up.

Djamela did not look back at him. He waited for it the whole way across the road, and she did not, and he took that and put it with the rest.

---

It rained hard in April, the way it does, and she came to his room because it was nearer than hers.

She stood in the doorway with the water coming off her shoulders and he moved the physics papers off the mattress without being asked. The roof was letting through in three places and the buckets were where he had put them, which was the only thing in the room he was ever confident about.

Do you understand entropy, she said.

She had heard him explain it eleven times. He explained it again, because being asked was worth something.

It is like holding onto something, she said. And then it gets too heavy.

My father says the lake is the opposite. He says it puts things back in order.

It is hydrogen and oxygen, Daudi said. It has no intelligence.

He heard how it came out and could not get it back.

They sat. The rain filled the room so completely there was nothing else in it. Her knee was against the edge of his and he did not move his leg, and the not-moving took his whole attention, and he was aware — distantly, the way you are aware of a conversation you are not having — that she had put it there.

Then what do you want to remember, Mr Scientist, she said.

He had a sentence ready. He had several. He sat with his hands on his knees and did not say any of them, and the reason was not that he could not think of one. It was that saying one would settle the question, and while the question was open she was still, in some way he could not have defended, his.

So she leaned over and kissed him. It lasted about twenty seconds. She counted; he heard her counting, under her breath, and did not understand until much later that this was the most like herself she had ever been in front of him.

She pulled back. See, she said. Not everything falls apart.

He did not say entropy always wins, though he thought it, and the thinking was the closest he came all year to saying something true.

---

After that there were the months when he did not look.

He would not have put it that way. What he would have said, if anyone had asked, was that she got busy.

Her nails were done on Fridays now and gone again by Monday morning, and he noticed this every week for eleven weeks and did not once ask who paid.

She smelled of a perfume that was not sold in Mihogoni. It got into the weave of a khanga and stayed there, and he knew the smell before he knew the name of it, and he did not ask the name.

There was a key fob in her left pocket shaped like a cartoon lion, plastic, the kind you would give a child, and a white card on the ring with it. He saw the card twice. Cards like that opened the lakeside hotel. He had watched a man use one from the road once, out of interest in the mechanism.

There was a photograph on her phone with forty-one likes on it and a hand at the edge of the frame with a gold ring on it.

Any of these on its own was a fact. He was good at facts. What he did with them was what he did with the gap in Jean Luc's French, which was to put them somewhere and not go back, and the somewhere had a name he would not have used, and the name was later.

His stepmother asked him one evening whether Djamela was all right and he said she was fine and went to his room and lay on the mattress and looked at the wall of things he had found.

---

In August she asked him to come with her to Ave de Bobozo. She did not want to go alone and he said it was because of his bicycle, which had been stolen once, so he knew the forms.

The station smelled of dust and men. There was a woman in a black dress explaining that it was escort services and not the other thing, they had cards with a company number, and the officer she was explaining it to said more like your pimp's number, and everybody laughed except the woman and Djamela.

The one who took them was young and typed with one finger, hunting each letter as though it owed him money. He looked up at Djamela's face and made a sound in his throat.

Daudi said she was reporting a hit and run.

So why are you talking, the officer said, and winked at him, one man to another, over the top of her head.

Daudi did not answer the wink. He also did not step back, and afterwards he would think about the fact that he had not stepped back.

She held out her phone with the photograph on it. The officer said they dealt in hard evidence, not AI, not phantom ghosts. She leaned in close to him and pulled away again from the smell, and then she said the thing about her cycle and the latency, and the room changed like a door opening somewhere else in the building.

Daudi's ears went. He was aware of the fan not working and of his own hands and of the older officer coming over and beginning to recite — the penal code, a teenage minor, thirty years, twelve lashes — in the voice the priest used at St Augustine for the parts he had said ten thousand times.

He was aware that he was not surprised.

That was the part. Not the pregnancy. That he was standing in a police station hearing it for the first time and his body had not moved at all, and somewhere underneath the not-moving was a small mean relief, because now it was said out loud by somebody else and it had never been his to say.

He did not look at her. She was two feet away and he did not look at her, and there was nothing preventing it.

Jean Luc arrived in twenty minutes.

Daudi did the arithmetic on that while the man was still crossing the room. Twenty minutes meant the hotel or the road above it. Twenty minutes meant near. Twenty minutes meant he had been near for a while.

The man came in with his shoulders back and an envelope under his jacket and the look of somebody whose phone has finally rung. When the older officer said the word minor, something crossed his face and was gone, and Daudi caught it, and it was the only thing that happened all afternoon that he let himself keep.

They took her and Jean Luc into the room at the back.

Daudi sat on the bench outside it for eleven minutes. The bench had been worn smooth in two places. He put his thumb in one of the places and left it there.

When they came out the officers were looking at each other the way men do when a thing has gone well, and Djamela was carrying the envelope, and it had been fat going in.

---

They buried her on a Thursday. Daudi did not go to the burial. He went to the lakeshore instead and stood where the ships were and did not throw anything into the water, which he had half meant to do.

D'juma found him there in the evening. He did not say anything about the lake.

I was wrong, D'juma said. It doesn't put things back in order. It keeps them.

Then he stood next to Daudi for a while and left, and it was the only time Daudi ever heard him say a thing without putting a story in front of it.

---

He went into her room on the Saturday, when her mother was at the market.

He did not know what he was looking for and that was not true. Under the mattress where the physics notes used to go there was a plastic lion, cartoon, the kind you would give a child, with a white card on the ring.

He sat down on the floor of her room and held it, and this time he did not put it anywhere.

He had seen it in April.

That was the thing. Not that it existed. He had seen it in her pocket in April and he had known what the card was for because he had watched a man use one from the road, out of interest in the mechanism, and he had taken that fact and put it in the place called later, and later had arrived and she was in the ground.

He sat on the floor for a long time. Then he put the fob in his pocket and went home and took the motherboard down off the wall to make room, and stood holding it, and could not remember where he had got it.

---

On the Monday he walked to Ave de Bobozo and gave the fob to the young officer with one finger.

He said what it was and where it had been and which floor it opened. He said the name Jean Luc twice, clearly, and spelled it. He said Morisho had been on the dala dala every morning of his life and had never once been to the third floor of the lakeside hotel.

The officer turned the plastic lion over. He looked at the card. He put both of them in the drawer on his right, and he wrote something down that was two words long, and he said they would look into it.

Daudi stood there.

The officer looked up and asked whether there was anything else, in the voice you use to a boy, and Daudi said no. Then he went outside and stood in the road for a minute in the sun.

He did not feel better and he had not expected to. What he had done was small and had cost him nothing anybody could see, and it had come nine weeks after he could have done it, and it would not open the cell they were keeping Morisho in.

He had done it anyway.

That was new, and he would have to see what it was for.

---

The dala dala was at the stop the next morning, in its place, at the time it was always there.

There was a boy hanging off the side of it, sixteen maybe, with a stack of notes folded into his hand the way Morisho folded them, shouting mia tano mia tano at the road, and getting thirty people into a vehicle built for sixteen.

Daudi stood on the verge with his bag on his shoulder.

Then he paid his fare and got on, and rode it to school, and looked out of the window the whole way at the lake, which kept everything, and gave up nothing, and did not care one way or the other.

>
> **The one genuine win:** the short-sentence ratio is 52%, against 60% in Chantal D2 and a Barrett-flagged 63% in D1. The bumpety-bump fix that failed in the Chantal revision succeeded here — because close third naturally carries longer declaratives for interiority, where first-person Djamela's voice pulled toward clipped fragments. That is a POV effect, not a discipline effect, and worth knowing.
>
> **The one genuine risk:** at 2,942 words this is the shortest draft and nearest the prize's 2,000-word floor. It has the most headroom of the three and, unlike the others, may actually need some of it — see weakness 1 below.

### What close third bought that neither first-person draft could

**The reader sees Daudi refuse to know while he is refusing.** *"He filed it with the other things. He had a place for things like that and it was not a place he went back to."* In first person that sentence is either a confession — which makes him self-aware and destroys the point — or it cannot be written at all. Close third lets the narration name the machinery while the character stays inside it. This is the Controlling Idea dramatised rather than stated, and it is the strongest argument for the POV.

**The revelation is relocated.** The story's worst moment is no longer the pregnancy or the envelope; it is *"He had seen it in April."* The reader has been carrying that fob since the inventory section and Daudi has not. That is [[Inevitable but Unforeseen — Suspense and Surprise|Barrett's progressive and culminative hint]] working as designed — invisible on first pass, inevitable on rereading.

**The relief is the true climax.** *"a small mean relief, because now it was said out loud by somebody else and it had never been his to say"* — Kiteley's [[Emotion as Movement|True Feeling with a secret]], and a thing a first-person Daudi would never admit to.

### Craft rules applied, by source

- **Barrett** — one opening, three paragraphs; mystery demoted to a frame so the story runs one action; Morisho settled; ending 90 words past the climax; title passing all five tests with aptness suspended to the final page.
- **Kiteley** — a circle (188): opens and closes at the same stop, with the same words shouted by a different boy. The police station written as an iceberg (186): the back room is never entered. Daudi given one unexpected act (193) — he goes to the station. D'juma changed by what he teaches (86). Every scene carries a state change.
- **Learning Curve** — ages fixed; Scene 6 determined and withheld; the motherboard planted unweighted and never explained; no interpretation supplied where the reader can do the work.
- **McKee / Cleland / Gardner** — every scene turns; the Gap widened at the BMW (he expects her to look back) and the station (he expects to be surprised); physics vocabulary kept as Daudi's only metaphor register; local language unitalicised; perception verbs minimised.

### Where it is weakest

1. **The ending risks being too quiet.** [[The Resonant Close|Gardner's underdone failure]] is live: the new conductor shouting Morisho's words is the whole argument, and a tired judge may read past it. Test one added sentence.
2. **Djamela is still seen from outside** — the fault close third does not fix. Only [[2026-07-26 — Chantal (Djamela POV, Draft 2 — Barrett Pass)|Chantal]] fixes that.
3. **Two aphorisms survive**, both in the final section. The weakest is *"That was new, and he would have to see what it was for."*
4. **Sentence rhythm unmeasured at time of writing** — run the count before trusting any claim about it.

Three complete drafts now exist of one story: first-person Daudi (unfinished), first-person Djamela, close-third Daudi. **Only one can be submitted.** The honest comparison is on [[The Judging Rubric — Nine Axes|the nine axes]], scored cold, a week from now — not today, and not by me alone.
