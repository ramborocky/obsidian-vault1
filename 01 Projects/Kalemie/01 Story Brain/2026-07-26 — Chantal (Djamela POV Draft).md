# CHANTAL

> [!NOTE] What this draft is
> A full-length companion draft told entirely by Djamela, written in the register of the interiority fragment in [[Scenario — Arcs, Beats & Scenes]] — close, embodied, arithmetic instead of physics. It stands alone as a submittable story and also explains, from her side, every found object Daudi catalogues in the opening of [[KALEMIE-Draft_with_AI_Questions|the master draft]]: the motherboard, the lion fob, the polished fingernails, the name written in the dust.
>

> — **~4,050 words**, inside the Commonwealth 2,000–5,000 gate with room to breathe.

Nobody tells you the exact day your body stops being only yours. Mine picked a Tuesday.

I was behind the school gate counting on my fingers, the way you count coins when you already know they are not enough. Alicia was talking about somebody's hairdo. I was doing arithmetic nobody had taught me on purpose.

Thirty-four days. I counted twice. The first time I got thirty-three, and I wanted the smaller number to be the true one, so I counted again and it got bigger. That is not how counting is supposed to work.

The bell went. Alicia took my arm the way she does, and I let her, and we walked in, and I sat down at a desk with a wobble in it and opened a book about the digestive system of a cow.

I am telling you the Tuesday first because everybody always wants to start with the man. The man comes later. The man is not the beginning of anything except his own story.

---

We live in Mihogoni, the down part of town, where the road stops being a road and becomes a suggestion. My father sells fruit on Ave de Tanganyique in the mornings and lays his hands on people in the afternoons, and both of these are jobs, and neither of them is the kind you say out loud at school.

At school there is a girl called Béatrice whose father works for the mining company. She has a school bag with a zip that works on both sides. Once, in Primary Six, she told the whole row that our house smelled of fish, and everybody laughed, and I laughed too, because that is the fastest way to stop being the thing people are laughing at.

Our house does smell of fish. That is not the part that stayed with me. The part that stayed with me is that I laughed.

There are children in this town we call the ghost kids. They go to Monseigneur André Kaseba, and they wear checkered skirts with ties and blazers, and they sit in the back of their parents' cars looking at nothing. When our dala dala stops next to one of their cars at the lights on Ave de Industrielle, we look in and they do not look out. Not because they are proud. Because we are not there.

Morisho, who hangs off the side of the dala dala and shouts mia tano mia tano until his voice goes, told us once that those children are ghosts to us. We will never see them or talk to them.

He said it like a warning. I heard it like a price list.

---

I turned thirteen in March. My father bought a watermelon.

Not a cake. A watermelon, with one candle pushed into the top of it, standing up crooked because a watermelon is not built to hold a candle, which anybody could have told him.

Baba, I said, you could have got a cake. Even a cheap one.

Watermelons are healthier, he said. More natural. More African. Cakes are a western thing.

My mother said watermelons are sweet, like you, Djamela, and she said it in the voice she uses when she is helping him be right about something.

I want to be fair to my father here, because I have not always been. He is not a stupid man. He is a man who was handed a town that had already been emptied out twice before he got to it, and who has nothing to give me except proverbs and fruit, and who has decided that if he refuses the whole world outside Kalemie hard enough, I will stop wanting it.

It did not work like that. It only made the wanting private.

Make a wish, he said. Blow.

I closed my eyes and said: to be Kim Kardashian, amen.

What is Kim Kardashian, my father said.

A social media influencer, my mother said, fast, the way you move a knife off a table.

I blew the candle out. The smoke went up and sat under the iron roof and did not leave.

Later we sat on the porch and looked at the lake with the rusted ships in it. My father was inside. I said to Daudi, he thinks I want things.

Daudi said nothing, which is what Daudi does.

I do want things, I said. I want to control things. I want to conquer, like the Europeans did, except we do not need weapons or degrees now. We only need followers.

I was thirteen years and one hour old and I believed this completely. I want that written down honestly. Nobody made me believe it. I worked it out myself, from the evidence available to me, which was a town where the people with the cars had taken things and the people without the cars had been taken from.

I had the mathematics right. I had only got the sign wrong.

---

Daudi is the boy from two houses down, and he is a year and a half older than me, and he has a wall in his room where he nails things he has found. A hubcap. The skull of an antelope. A bicycle wheel that I am fairly sure he did not find.

He calls it a collection. Everybody else calls it rubbish.

His mother died having him, which is a thing that gets said about him rather than by him, and he has built a whole woman out of that — patient, soft, never once disappointed in him, because a woman who never existed never has to be. I know this about him the way you know the shape of a room in the dark. He has never said one word of it to me.

When we were small I gave him a motherboard.

I found it behind the internet café that used to be by the market, in a heap of things that had stopped working. It was green, with little gold roads on it going nowhere. I thought it was the most beautiful broken thing I had ever seen and I gave it to him because he was the only person I knew who would understand that a thing could be beautiful after it had stopped being useful.

He nailed it to the wall between the skull and the hubcap. He has never once mentioned where he got it.

I do not think he remembers it was me. He thinks he found it. Daudi thinks he found everything.

---

The car was a BMW, black, with windows you could not see into.

We had got off the dala dala at the dagaa stop, Daudi and me, and the boda boda men were doing what boda boda men do, which is call you beautiful in a way that is not a compliment but a notice, a way of saying we have all agreed you are available for comment now.

Then the horn. Different from theirs. And the window came down with a sound I had never heard a window make — not the clink of a handle going round, but a swish, like a word being said quietly on purpose.

Je m'appelle Jean Luc, he said. Tu es d'où, ma belle.

I want to tell you what I did next, and I want to tell it exactly, because everybody who has heard this story since has decided they already know.

I said: Rue Saint Michel.

I lied. Immediately, easily, without deciding to. Rue Saint Michel is where the ghost children live and I have never set foot on it, and it came out of my mouth like it had been sitting behind my teeth for years waiting.

Daudi started saying the thing about strangers. Mama alisema —

We shouldn't talk to strangers, said Jean Luc, finishing it for him. And he smiled.

I have thought about that smile more than I have thought about anything else in my life. He knew what he was. He said it out loud, in a joke, in front of both of us, and he was so certain that saying it would not cost him anything that he was right.

He told us he was a director. Consul général de Belgique. He said it in French with Lingala underneath, and his French was not as good as he thought, and I noticed that, and I decided not to have noticed it.

That is the part I would like somebody to write down somewhere. Not that I was fooled. That I did the deciding.

He wrote a phone number on a piece of paper and put a ten thousand note under it, and my eyes went wide, and Daudi saw them go wide and has never once asked me why. It was not the money. Ten thousand is a school skirt. It was the car, and the window, and the fact that a man with a car like that had stopped it, in the road, in front of the boda boda men, for me.

Je m'appelle, he said, asking.

And I said: Chantal.

Nobody had asked me for a name before. They had only ever used the one I came with.

He gave me a name the way you give somebody a gift, and I took it the way you take a gift, and it was not a gift. It was a collar with a nice buckle. He renamed me before he had paid for anything at all, and every single thing that happened after that was just the account settling.

Daudi stood beside me the whole time. He did not say one word after mama alisema. I did not look at him. I have been asked since whether I knew he was hurt and the answer is yes, of course, I could feel it coming off him like heat off a wall, and I got into that feeling and warmed my hands on it, because a girl who has just been chosen wants somebody standing there to see her be chosen.

I was thirteen. I am not asking to be forgiven for that. I am asking for it to be counted correctly.

---

It rained in April, hard, the way it does, and I ran to Daudi's house because it was closer than mine.

His room is one room. Baked brick, a window the size of two hands, a foam mattress on the floor with a grey sheet on it, and buckets set out under the places where the roof has given up. He had physics papers spread over the mattress and he moved them for me without being asked.

Do you understand entropy, I said.

I knew what it was. I had heard him say it eleven times. I asked because I wanted him to be the person who knew something, for once, in front of me.

It is a natural state, he said. Order needs energy. Disorder is what happens when you stop.

It is like holding something, I said, and then it gets too heavy and you have to let go.

My father says the lake is the opposite, I said. He says it puts things back in order. He says it remembers everything in its right place.

Your father is a wise man, Daudi said.

It is hydrogen and oxygen, he said, a minute later. It has no intelligence.

We sat there. The rain came through the roof and hit the buckets and the sound filled the whole room up so there was nothing else in it.

My knee was touching the edge of his knee. I had put it there.

Then what do you want to remember, Mr Scientist, I said. Encrypted data? Binary codes? Or a colourful moment with no meaning in it?

He did not answer. He did not move. I watched a boy who wanted something more than he has ever wanted anything hold completely still because moving might have cost him the version of me he had in his head.

So I kissed him.

It lasted about twenty seconds. I counted, because I count everything, because counting is the only thing I have ever been good at without being taught.

Then I pulled back and I said, see? Not everything falls apart.

He looked at me like I had shown him the back door of the universe.

Here is the true thing, and it is not a kind one. That kiss cost me nothing. Nothing at all. It was the cheapest thing I have ever given anybody, and he has been carrying it around ever since like it was the down payment on a house.

I let him carry it. I knew what he thought it was and I let him think it, and I have decided that this is the worst thing I have done to another person — worse than the lying, worse than Rue Saint Michel — because it was free for me and it was not free for him, and I let the difference stand.

It is the only thing I ever gave Daudi that did not cost me anything. It is also the only thing I ever gave him that he actually wanted.

---

I am not going to describe the hotel.

I will tell you what I have instead, which is the inventory, because that is how I have always understood the world and I am not going to pretend otherwise now.

A key fob shaped like a lion, cartoon, plastic, the kind you would give a child. It opens a door on the third floor. I kept it in my left pocket where the seam has gone and I never once put it in my right.

A perfume. Shalis by Remy Marquis. Not mine. It gets into the weave of a khanga and it does not come out with soap, only with time, and I learned to leave certain clothes at the bottom of the basket for four days before my mother did the wash.

Fingernails, done every Friday, taken off again every Monday before school, because at school there are rules and at the hotel there are different rules, and I lived between the two of them like a person crossing a road that never stops.

A photograph on my phone. Me, and at the edge of the frame a hand on my shoulder with a gold ring on it. The ring is not real gold. He wears it like a crown. I tagged it blessed and forty-one people liked it and not one of them asked me a single question.

Two vaccinations, low on the arm, near the elbow, not where they usually put them.

That is the inventory. Anybody who wanted to could have read it. It was all on the outside of me for months.

Nobody read it. My mother did the wash. My father sold fruit. The school marked me absent and then marked me present. Alicia told me she wished her father could afford my nails.

I want to be precise, because I have earned the right to be precise about this. It is not that nobody saw. It is that seeing would have obliged them to do something, and not seeing was free, so they chose the free one. Everybody in that town is very good at arithmetic. I learned it somewhere.

---

We went to Ave de Bobozo about the car.

I say the car. There had been a car, and it had come off the road at the corner near the market and it had not stopped, and my leg had a mark on it going yellow at the edges. That was true. I want you to know one of the things I said that year was true.

Daudi came because I did not want to go alone. He said it was because of his bicycle, which had been stolen once, so he knew the forms.

The station smelled of dust and men. There was a woman in a black dress explaining to an officer that it was not prostitution, it was escort services, they had cards with a company number on them. He said more like your pimp's number. Everybody laughed except her and me.

The officer who took us was young and typing with one finger, hunting each letter down like it owed him something. He looked up at my face and made a sound in his throat.

Mhh. What do we have here.

Daudi said, she is reporting a hit and run.

So why are you talking, the officer said. You sound like you know more than she does. And he winked at Daudi, one man to another, over the top of me.

He asked me where and what type of car and who was driving, and he was typing at the speed of a man who has decided in advance how much this is worth.

So I held out my phone. I showed him the photograph, the one with the hand and the ring, because I had worked out on the way there that a hit and run was worth nothing but a man like that was worth something.

We deal with hard evidence, he said. Not AI. Not phantom ghosts.

And I leaned towards him, and I got close enough to smell onion and whiskey on him, and I backed off, and then I said it.

My menstrual cycle. There is a latency of more than a month.

The air in that room changed like a door had opened somewhere.

The older one came over. Big gut on him, the kind you get from a lifetime of small envelopes and large drinks. He started reciting. The penal code. A teenage minor. Rape. Thirty years, and twelve lashes on top. He said it the way the priest at St Augustine says the parts he has said ten thousand times.

A buccal swab will suffice as conclusive evidence, he said.

Shame on the predator, he said, for luring young girls.

And the young one, still typing, said quietly: where is his number. We will call him. We will summon him now.

Yes, he said. We will finish the case. If you understand me.

I did understand him. I understood him perfectly. I have understood men like him since I was eleven years old and my auntie's neighbour let his hand sit too long on the back of my chair while a room full of adults decided all together not to see it, the way you decide not to see a crack in a wall you are only renting.

I gave him the phone. I dialled the number myself.

Jean Luc took twenty minutes.

Twenty minutes. Later I did that arithmetic too. Twenty minutes means he was already in town, already near, already somewhere he could get to us from — which means he had been near for a while, which means the whole time I had been walking around thinking I was choosing things, I had been the thing being kept an eye on.

He came in with his shoulders back and a fat envelope under his jacket, and he did not look surprised, and he did not look afraid. He looked like a man whose phone had finally rung.

I saw one thing. One. When the older officer said the word minor, something went across Jean Luc's face and was gone — a flicker, like a bulb deciding whether to hold. He had planned for envelopes. He had not planned for consequences. Then it closed over and he smiled and it was as if it had never been there at all.

They took us to the room at the back.

I am not going to describe that room either, and it is not for the reason you think. Nothing happened to my body in that room. What happened in that room is that three men worked out what I was worth, out loud, in front of me, in two languages, using the word compensation, and then agreed on it.

I sat on a plastic chair with a crack in it and I did the arithmetic along with them. I could not help it. I have never been able to help it. And what I got, sitting there, was that the number they were saying was smaller than the number I had been carrying in my head for eight months about what all of this was going to be worth in the end.

I came out of that room bent over. Daudi saw me come out and I know exactly what he thought, because he is fifteen and good and he thinks shame is the only thing that can bend a person.

It was not shame. It was arithmetic. It was coming up short, the way I always come up short, the way Mihogoni always comes up short against Rue Saint Michel no matter how straight you hold your back getting out of the car.

They gave me the envelope on the way out. It had been fat going in. It was thin coming out, and nobody said anything about that, and nobody had to.

---

School on the Monday. I took the nails off on the Sunday night, same as always.

I went to my locker at the end of the day when the corridor was empty, and there was a good coat of dust on the inside of the door, and I wrote his name in it with one finger.

Daudi.

I looked at it for a while. It is a short name. It does not take long to write and it does not take long to look at.

Then I wiped it off with the side of my hand, because a name in dust is evidence, and everything I have learned this year I have learned about what happens to people when there is evidence lying around with their name on it. Morisho gets picked up because he is poor and visible and cannot afford an envelope. Daudi is poor and visible. Daudi collects things. Daudi would keep it.

So I took it away from him. It is the only thing I have ever done for Daudi that cost me something, and he will never know I did it, which I have decided is what makes it count.

On the way home I stopped at his house. He was not there. His father was, and his father does not look at me, which suited me.

I went into that room with the buckets in it and I took the motherboard off the wall.

I want to be clear that this was not sentiment. I needed a place to put something and I needed it to be a place nobody would ever look, and there is one board in that room, under the third bucket, that comes up if you know it comes up. I have known since I was nine.

I put the fob under the board. The lion. Cartoon, plastic, the kind you would give a child.

Then I put the motherboard back on the nail, straight, the way he likes it, between the skull and the hubcap.

He will find it. Not soon. But he is a boy who takes things apart and he will be in that room one day with nothing to do and he will lift that board, and there will be a key to a door on the third floor of a hotel, and Daudi is the only person in this town who will not put it back down again.

That is what I have got. Not justice. I have looked at justice up close now, in a back room on Ave de Bobozo, and it costs about the same as a second-hand television.

But I have got one boy who keeps things. Who nails broken things to a wall and refuses, in the face of everybody laughing at him, to agree that a thing stops mattering when it stops working.

My father says the lake puts things back in order. He is wrong, and I have not got the heart to tell him. The lake does not put anything back. It keeps things. It holds them down at the bottom in the cold and the dark, exactly as they were, forever, and it does not give them up, and it does not let them rot.

I used to think that was the most frightening thing anybody had ever said to me.

I have changed my mind. I am counting one last time, and this is the first sum all year that has come out even.

Let it keep me.

Two honest notes on that table. **The 11% long-sentence rate is double the master draft's** — deliberate, since Cleland assigns long sentences to interiority and this draft is nothing but interiority, but it is the first thing to check if the voice ever reads as slack. And **the perception-verb count is identical to the master draft's, not better.** Roughly two-thirds here are load-bearing epistemic claims in a voice whose whole argument is about what she knew and when (*I knew what he was*, *I understood him perfectly*, *Nobody read it*), which the craft note in [[Scenario — Arcs, Beats & Scenes]] explicitly sanctions. The remainder — `looked` at 6, `saw` at 4 — are ordinary and should go in a line pass.

**Techniques applied:**

- **[[All the Small Things — Grief Through the Minor Object]]** — the motherboard is planted unweighted (a broken thing behind an internet café), recurs twice as furniture, and detonates in the final movement when we learn she gave it to him and he never knew. The lion fob is planted in the inventory and becomes the story's only act of agency.
- **[[Obfuscated Openness — Detail as Disguise]]** — the hotel is never described. The inventory replaces it: fob, perfume, nails, photograph, vaccination scars. Volume of specific unexplained detail carries what depiction would have cheapened.
- **[[The Funhouse Mirror — AI Output as Cliché Detector]]** — the two most probable defaults for this material were avoided deliberately: the girl does not fail to understand what is happening to her, and the boy who loves her is not her rescuer. She is the most numerate person in the story and the only one who acts.
- **[[The First-Page Cut]]** — opens on a Tuesday and a wrong sum. Specific, physical, unexplained, with the gap open before the reader is comfortable.
- **Cleland** — sentence average is under 20; short sentences reserved for rupture (*I laughed too.* / *I was thirteen.* / *Let it keep me.*). Local language unitalicised throughout.
- **Metaphor sourcing** — every figure Djamela reaches for is money, counting, debt, price, or change. Daudi gets physics. Neither borrows from the other.

3. Should this become Story #2 of the cycle in [[Collection Tracker]], or replace Kalemie as the primary submission? It is arguably the stronger story, because its protagonist acts and Daudi's does not.
4. If it stays a companion, Kalemie must not also explain the motherboard — only one of the two stories can hold that reveal.
