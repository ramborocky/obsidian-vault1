## The root cause behind most of this

In [[2026-07-29 — Chantal (Draft 4, Voice Pass — WORKING)|Draft 4]] the narrator **is** Djamela. In this version she is **Zinduna** (Chantal to Jean Luc), and **Djamela has become a separate schoolmate**. One character was split into two. Items 1, 2 and 11 below are all fallout from that split, and they are cheap to close once you decide who is who.

---

## 🔴 Critical — these are visible to a first-round reader

### 1 · The opening and closing use the same clause with different names

- **Opening:** "Djamela took my arm as she does and I let her"
- **Closing:** "Alicia... took my arm as she does, and we walked out to the gate together"

The repeated clause is clearly a deliberate bookend. But Djamela appears 5 times in the manuscript and **Alicia appears exactly once — here, on the last page, never introduced.** Given the narrator/friend split above, the likeliest reading is that the opening's *Djamela* is the leftover from Draft 4 and *Alicia* is the friend's new name — but as it stands a reader meets a stranger in the final paragraph.

**Question:** who takes your arm, and does the other one exist at all?

### 2 · Four names for the narrator, none of them agreeing with the title

| Where | Name |
|---|---|
| Manuscript title | **Shantal Kardashan** |
| Given to her by Jean Luc | **Chantal** (once) |
| Her own, in her mother's mouth | **Zinduna** (once) |
| The wish | **Kim Kardashian** |

*Shantal* never appears in the story. *Kardashan* never appears either — the body says *Kardashian*. If the title's spelling is her own phoneticised version of the name she wants, that is a strong idea and the manuscript currently gives the reader no way to know it. If it is a typo, it is on the cover page.

This matters more than usual here because the story's own thesis is about names: *"He handed me that name like a gift, and I took it like one, and it was a collar with a good buckle."*

**Question:** is *Shantal* her spelling, and if so where does the reader earn it?

### 3 · Broken sentence in the fourth paragraph

> "Why Tuesday?, and not about the man."

The frame the clause needs is missing, and this sits in the exact place a first-round reader decides whether to keep going.

Related: the first line promises a Tuesday — *"Nobody tells you the exact day your body stops being only yours. Mine picked a Tuesday."* — and **no scene in the manuscript is ever identified as that Tuesday.** (The 30 July draft stated it outright: *"It happened to me on a Tuesday, and I didn't see any clouds in the sky."* The promise survived the revision; the payoff did not.)

## 🟡 High

### 4 · "Je m'appelle, he asked."

He introduces himself with *Je m'appelle Jean Luc*, then uses the same phrase to ask her name. As written it is not a question in French. If the point is that *"his French was worse than he thought it was"*, that is a good joke and it needs to be visibly the joke rather than readable as the author's slip.

### 5 · Four spellings of one vehicle

*dala dala* · *dada dala* · *dadadala* · *daladala*. Pick one and enforce it.

### 6 · Plain typos

| As written | Issue |
|---|---|
| "At **Six** in the **even** when the sun set" | *evening*; also the capitalised *Six* |
| "His mother died having him, he couldn't **breath**" | *breathe*; comma splice; and the reader's first parse is that **the mother** couldn't breathe |
| "He **gnarled** his teeth" | *gnashed* / *ground* |
| "**they** way he always did" | *the* |
| "the way you slit a **chickens** throat" | *chicken's* |
| "a book about a cow's digestive system **.**" | space before the full stop |

> [!note] Item 6, row 3 is already an open question of yours
> Draft 4's header flags a **tense wobble in this exact clause** — *he **would** grind his teeth* (habitual) beside *and **clenched** his fists* (single past). This version reads "He gnarled his teeth and clenched his fists they way he always did", which still has both problems plus the wrong verb. One pass closes all three.

## 🟢 Medium — decisions, not errors

### 7 · Lake geography

> "we watched MV. Liemba pass by, From Kigoma taking the Dagaa to Kivu, and from Kivu taking whatever valuable was left after the fighting"

The Liemba runs Kigoma–Kalemie–Mpulungu on Tanganyika; Kivu is a different lake. If *Kivu* means the province rather than the lake, the sentence still has cargo arriving by boat from a place the boat does not go. Also *MV Liemba* takes no full stop, and *Dagaa* is lowercase elsewhere in the same paragraph.

### 8 · Bahati is in two places

Introduced as the charcoal seller **on the corner** — "I have never once heard anybody use it to her face" — then speaks **on the dala dala** about a man fondling women. Same woman, or slippage? The reader has been told she is a fixture of the corner, not a passenger.

### 9 · "He told us he was a director. Consul général de Belgique."

A consul general is not a director. The narrator notices his French and not this. If the mismatch is his cover story coming apart in real time, it wants one beat to register.

### 10 · Daudi arrives before he is introduced

He is on the porch — "Later I sat on the porch with Daudi" — several paragraphs before "Daudi is the boy from two houses down." Deliberate, or a sequencing accident?

### 11 · Two threads planted and not returned to

- **The father and the black BMW.** "Then Baba came out of the house and got into his car... baba came out of the car with a white envelope, and the man in the car left." That is her father already inside the economy that eats her, planted on page two and never touched again.
- **The two vaccinations.** "low on the arm, near the elbow, not where they usually put them." In the Daudi-POV material this was a clue a reader decoded. Here it is a flat item in the inventory and the reader has nothing to decode it with.

Both may be deliberate — a red herring and a withheld fact are both legitimate. Worth being decisions rather than residue.

### 12 · Street names

*Ave de Tanganyique* · *Ave de Industrielle* · *Ave de Bobozo* — inconsistent French. This is a register choice and may well be intentional; make it a decision and apply it uniformly.
