# CHANTAL — DRAFT 5

Nobody tells you the exact day your body stops being  yours. Mine picked a Tuesday.


That is not how counting is supposed to work.

The bell went, and Djamela took my arm as she usually does and I let her, and we walked in past the notice board with last term's examination timetable still pinned to it, and I sat down at a desk with a wobble, so I put a small pebble where the wobble was, opened a book about a cow's digestive system . We did the digestive system of a cow for three weeks. There are four stomachs, and I can still name all four, and I haven't seen a cow all this time.

Why Tuesday?, and not about the man. The man comes later. The man is not the beginning of anything except his own story.

We live in Mihogoni, the down part of town, where the road stops being a road and becomes a suggestion. My father sells fruit on Ave de Tanganyique in the mornings and lays his hands on people in the afternoons, and both of these are jobs. Last week, we were told by the French teacher to write about what our parents do for a living, I copied what Djamela wrote, homme d' affaires entrepreneur. The teacher started to ask me many questions, like where I live and what business my father does. I answered them just like Djamela wrote. I copied everything.

The boats come over from Kigoma with dagaa and secondhand words, and we keep both, so half of what gets shouted on this road is Tanzanian and nobody here has ever thought to mention it.
Except when we listen to rhumba from Christian Bella, we can tell that he doesn't sound like Fally Ipupa.

There is a gutter that came off our side of the house the year before last and is still lying where it landed. The woman who sells charcoal on the corner is called Bahati, or that is what people call her, and I have never once heard anybody use it to her face. At Six in the evening when the sun set upon our lake, a BMW, black, the windows were pulled up and you couldn't see who or what was inside. Just a silhouette, when light from the dagaa seller's solar powered lamp pierced through the tinted glass. Then Baba came out of the house and got into his car. I could see both of their silhouettes nodding up and down, then baba came out of the car with a white envelope, and the man in the car left.

In Primary Six, Djamela told a whole row that our house smelled of fish, back when she was somebody else's friend and not mine. Everybody laughed, and I laughed too, because that is the fastest way to stop being the thing people are laughing at.

Our house does smell of fish. That is not the part that stayed with me.

I turned thirteen in March and my father bought a watermelon.

Not a cake. A watermelon, with one candle pushed into the top of it, standing crooked because a watermelon is not built to hold a candle, which anybody could have told him.

Baba, I said, you could have got a cake. Even a cheap one.

Watermelons are healthier, he said. More natural. More African. Cakes are a western thing.

My mother said watermelons are sweet, like you, Zinduna, in the voice she uses when she is helping him be right about something.

I want to be fair to him, because I have not always been. He is not a stupid man. He was handed a town that had been emptied out twice before he got to it, and he has nothing to give me except proverbs and fruit, and he decided that if he refused the world outside Kalemie hard enough I would stop wanting it. It did not work like that; it only made the wanting private.

Make a wish, he said. Blow.

I closed my eyes and said: to be Kim Kardashan, amen.

What is Kim Kardashan?, Baba asked.

Mitandao, titoki, Instagram you name it, very famous, my mother said, fast. The way you slit a chickens throat, when you slaughter about to slaughter it.

I blew the candle out. The smoke went up and sat under the iron roof and did not leave.

Later I sat on the porch with Daudi with the lake in front of us while we watched MV. Liemba pass by, From Kigoma taking the Dagaa to Kivu, and from Kivu taking whatever valuable was left after the fighting. He thinks I want things, I said.

Daudi said nothing, which is what Daudi does.

I do want things, all I need is ten thousand followers, I said. I want to control them. I want to conquer like the Europeans, all I need is ten thousand followers.

After turning thirteen, within the first hour I believed all of it. Nobody made me believe it, and I worked it out myself, from the evidence in front of me, which was a town where the people with the cars and the houses enclosed with fences had taken things and the people who walked and rode daladalas living in shacks and makeshift bungalows had been taken from.

Daudi lives to houses down. He is a year and a half older than me and he has a wall in his room where he nails things he has found. A hubcap. The skull of an antelope. A bicycle wheel I am fairly sure he did not find.

He calls it a collection, and everybody else calls it rubbish.

His mother died having him, he couldn't breath  which is a thing that gets said about him rather than by him, and he has built a whole woman out of that absence, patient, soft, never once disappointed in him, because a woman who never existed does not have to be. He has never said one word of it to me, and I have it anyway, entire, the shape of a room nobody has ever put a light on.

When we were small I gave him a motherboard.

I found it behind the internet café that used to be by the market, in a heap of things that had stopped working. It was green, with little gold roads on it going nowhere, and I thought it was the most beautiful broken thing in that heap. I gave it to him because he was the only person who would understand that a thing can be beautiful after it stops being useful.


He nailed it to the wall between the skull and the hubcap. He has never once mentioned where he got it, and I do not think he remembers it was me. Daudi thinks he found everything.

Morisho was still shouting mia tano mia tano and getting smaller. Daudi stood near the door, where he found a place to put his foot down so he wouldn't fall outside the Toyota Hiace minibus. Daudi felt suffocated inside, so he always stood near the door.  The girls did what the girls always did, they talked and talked. Djamela always sits next to the driver, the rest of the school children stand up, since they pay half price, and they have to respect the elders.

At the lights on Ave de Industrielle, Morisho started talking as though he knows everything like he usually does. 
"Now those kids will end up being employed in high places and they will be your bosses. They go to Ecole Monseigneur André Kaseba, they wear checkered skirts with ties and blazers and they sit in the back of their parents' cars looking at nothing." 
Some of what he says is true, when the délestage takes our side of town their windows stay yellow, and I have stood on the road and watched them doing their homework in the light. When our dala dala stops beside one of those cars , we look in and they do not look out. Not because they are proud. Because we are not there.

Morisho continued calling passenger his voice, getting drier and drier, mia tano mia tano, he kept on saying.
"Even if you go to school everyday you will never be like them. He said, look at me, I have finished Form four, but all I have to show is being a dada dala conductor.  

I heard Djamela form the front: You see the man driving the Toyota Prado, taking the girl to school. On Friday he gets on the daladala just like us, he gets off at the dagaa seller's stop. Then I see him get in the black BMW, and Morisho gets at the back talking to them. I said to Djamela, don't lie. I swear to God, I've seen them more than three times she said. Morisho put his hand on her shoulder, and told her to be quiet. Then I heard Bahati, say something about a man fondling women on the daladala. She said, I'm a victim. Her dress ended up being soiled, that day she said.

Then we arrived at our stop, where the mama ntilie sells her chai and mihogo in the morning. The boda boda men started to horn us, blowing whistles, they had been doing that ever since my breasts started to get bigger. 

Then a horn, different from the usual boda boda horns. The window came down with a sound I had never heard a window make, not the clink of a handle going round but a swish, like a word said quietly on purpose.

Je m'appelle Jean Luc, he said. Tu es d'où, ma belle.

I want to tell this part exactly, because everybody who has heard it since has decided they already know.

I said: Rue Saint Michel.

I lied immediately and easily and without deciding to. Rue Saint Michel is where the ghost children live and I have never set foot on it, and it came out of my mouth as though it had been sitting behind my teeth for years, waiting.

Daudi started saying the thing about strangers. Mama alisema —

We shouldn't talk to strangers, said Jean Luc, finishing it for him. And he smiled.

I have thought about that smile more than about anything else. He knew what he was, and he said it out loud, in a joke, in front of both of us, and he was so certain it would cost him nothing that he was right.

He told us he was a director. Consul général de Belgique. He said it in French with Lingala underneath, and his French was worse than he thought it was, and I noticed, and I decided not to have noticed.

That is the part I would like written down somewhere. Not that I was fooled. That I did the deciding.

He wrote a phone number on a piece of paper and put a ten thousand note under it. My eyes went wide, and Daudi has never asked me why. It was not the money, because ten thousand is a school skirt. It was that a man with a car like that had stopped it, in the road, in front of the boda boda men, for me.

Je m'appelle, he asked.

Chantal, I said.

Nobody had asked me for a name before. They had only ever used the one I came with. He handed me that name like a gift, and I took it like one, and it was a collar with a good buckle.

Daudi stood beside me the whole time and did not say one word after mama alisema. He gnarled his teeth and clenched his fists they way he always did when he got upset.  I did not turn my head. The hurt came off him like heat off a wall and I stood in it and warmed my hands, because a girl who has just been chosen wants somebody there to see her be chosen. Daudi set off a sigh of relief as the Black BMW went off at full speed, and he ran after it when he saw the hubcap come off when the back wheel entered a pothole. 

Turning Thirteen is not a sin.  Forgiveness?. No, I am just asking for it to be counted correctly, everything that happened on Tuesday.

The ten thousand note was the evidence, someone noticed me, someone important. I hid it under the mattress on my side. My mother does the wash on Thursdays and has done since before I could count, so I had until Thursday to decide where it was going to live.

The monsoon rains started, in April, hard, as it always does. Daudi's house was nearest, so I ran for shelter.

His room is one room. Baked brick, a window the size of two hands, a foam mattress on the floor with a grey sheet, and buckets set out under the places where the roof has given up. He had physics papers spread across the mattress and he moved them for me without being asked.

Do you understand entropy?, I asked him.

Eleven times he repeated the sale lesson. I asked because I wanted him to be the person who knew something, for once, in front of me.

It is a natural state, he said. Order needs energy. Disorder is what happens when you stop.

My father says the lake is the opposite, I said. He says it puts things back in order, and that it remembers everything in its right place.

Your father sounds like he knows physics, Daudi said. Then, a minute later: it is hydrogen and oxygen, and it has no intelligence.

We sat there and the rain came through the roof and hit the buckets and the sound filled the room so completely that there was nothing else in it. My knee was touching the edge of his knee. I had put it there.

Then what do you want to remember, David Livingstone, I said. Encrypted data? Binary codes? Or just some fun times with each other.

He did not answer and he did not move. I watched a boy who wanted something more than he has wanted anything hold completely still, because moving might have cost him the version of me he kept in his head. I knew if I made a move, he wouldn't remember what happened on Tuesday.

So I kissed him.

It lasted about twenty seconds. I counted, because I count everything.

Then I pulled back and said, see? Not everything falls apart.

His face, when I pulled back, was the face of somebody shown the back door of the universe.

That kiss cost me nothing at all. It was the cheapest thing I have ever given anybody and he has carried it around ever since like the down payment on a house, and I let him, and I had his side of it exactly, the whole time he was having it.

I am not going to describe the hotel.

Here is what I have instead, which is the inventory, because a list is the only thing I have ever been able to hold the world in.

A key fob shaped like a lion, cartoon, plastic, the kind you would give a child. It opens a door on the third floor. I keep it in my left pocket where the seam has gone and never in my right.

A perfume. Shalis by Remy Marquis. Not mine. It gets into the weave of a khanga and will not come out with soap, only with time, so I learned to leave certain clothes at the bottom of the basket for four days before my mother does the wash.

Fingernails, done every Friday and taken off every Monday before school, because at school there are rules and at the hotel there are different rules, and I live between the two of them like somebody crossing a road that never stops.

A photograph on my phone. Me, and at the edge of the frame a hand on my shoulder with a gold ring on it. The ring is not real gold, and he wears it like a crown. I tagged it blessed and forty-one people liked it and none of them asked me anything.

Two vaccinations, low on the arm, near the elbow, not where they usually put them.

Anybody who wanted to could have read that. It was all on the outside of me for months.

We went to Ave de Bobozo about the car.

I say the car. There had been a car, and it came off the road at the corner near the market and did not stop, and my leg has a mark on it going yellow at the edges. That was true, and I want you to know that one of the things I said that year was true.

Daudi came because I did not want to go alone. He said it was because his bicycle was stolen once, so he knew the forms.

The station smelled of dust and men. There was a bench along one wall with three of its slats replaced in a paler wood, and a woman in a black dress was explaining to an officer that it was not prostitution, it was escort services, they had cards with a company number on them.  Everybody laughed except her and me.

The officer who took us was young and typing with one finger, hunting each letter down as though it owed him something. He raised his face to mine and made a sound in his throat. Mhh. What do we have here.

Daudi said, she is reporting a hit and run.

So why are you talking, the officer said. You sound like you know more than she does. And he winked at Daudi, one man to another, over the top of me.

He asked where and what type of car and who was driving, typing at the speed of a man who has already decided what this is worth. So I held out my phone and showed him the photograph with the hand and the ring, because I had worked out on the way there that a hit and run was worth nothing and a man like that was worth something.

We deal with hard evidence, he said. Not AI. Not phantom ghosts.

I leaned close enough to smell onion and whiskey on him, and backed off, and said it.

My menstrual cycle. There is a latency of more than a month.

The air in that room changed as though a door had opened somewhere.

The older one came over, big gut on him, the kind you get from a lifetime of small envelopes and large drinks, and he started reciting. The penal code. A teenage minor. Rape. Thirty years and twelve lashes on top. He said it in the voice the priest at St Augustine uses for the parts he has said ten thousand times. A buccal swab will suffice as conclusive evidence. Shame on the predator, for luring young girls.

The young one kept typing. Then, quietly: where is his number. We will call him. We will summon him now. Yes. We will finish the case, if you understand me.

I understood him perfectly. I have had the measure of men like him since I was eleven, when my auntie's neighbour let his hand sit too long on the back of my chair while a room full of adults agreed together not to see it, the way you decide not to see a crack in a wall you are only renting.

I gave him the phone and dialled the number myself. Jean Luc took twenty minutes.

Twenty minutes means he was already in town, already near, already somewhere he could reach us from, which means that the whole time I had been walking around thinking I was choosing things, I had been the thing being watched.

He came in with his shoulders back and a fat envelope under his jacket and he did not look surprised and he did not look afraid. He looked like a man whose phone had finally rung.

When the older officer said the word minor, something went across Jean Luc's face and was gone, a flicker, like a bulb deciding whether to hold. He had planned for envelopes and not for consequences. Then it closed over and he smiled and it was as though it had never been there.

They took us to the room at the back.

Nothing happened to my body in that room. What happened is that three men worked out what I was worth, out loud, in two languages, in front of me, using the word compensation, and then agreed on it.

I sat on a plastic chair with a crack in it and did the arithmetic along with them, because I cannot help it, I have never been able to help it. And the number they landed on was smaller than the one I had been carrying in my head for eight months.

It came in two parts. The second part was for a doctor in Uvira, and it was the smaller one, and they said it in the same voice they said the first.

I came out of that room bent over. Daudi was there when I came out, and I can tell you what he thought, because he is fifteen and good and he thinks shame is the only thing that can bend a person.

It was not shame. It was arithmetic.

They gave me the envelope on the way out. It had been fat going in, and it was thin coming out, and nobody said anything about that, and nobody had to.

The lake was on my left the whole way home, with the four ships in it.

The house was empty when I got home. My clothes were folded on the end of the bed, all of them, including the two from the bottom of the basket that still had four days to go. Somebody had washed them with soap and then with something after the soap, vinegar, or paraffin, whatever it is you use when soap will not do it.

I put them in the drawer. Then I stood in the doorway of the kitchen for a while, and my mother was not in it, and I went to bed.

School on the Monday. I took the nails off on the Sunday night, the same as always.

Djamlela asked me in the corridor whether it was true about the man with the car. The one from the Dagaa seller's stop, I said what man. She said all right, and took my arm as she does, and we walked out to the gate together, and she has not asked me anything since.

Here is the thing I have worked out and cannot put down. When this finishes, and it is going to finish, I have known that since the twenty minutes, somebody will be taken for it, and it will not be him. Him they will call as a witness, if they call him at all. They will take somebody visible and poor who cannot afford an envelope, and the whole town will agree to be satisfied, and I think it will be Jean Luc. But Morisho might be used to take the fall, since he doesn't have much to lose.

Since he was the one who gave me fare and lunch money, once he called me shemeji. That is the whole of what he did.

I went to my locker at the end of the day when the corridor was empty. There was a good coat of dust on the inside of the door and I wrote his name in it with one finger.

Daudi.

It is a short name, and it does not take long to write and it does not take long to look at.

Then I wiped it off with the side of my hand, because a name in dust is evidence, and everything I have learned this year I have learned about what happens to people when there is evidence lying around with their name on it.

My father was on Ave de Tanganyique with the fruit when I came past. He had the watermelons stacked in a pyramid, which takes him twenty minutes and which nobody has ever bought a watermelon because of. I did not cross over.

On the way home I stopped at Daudi's house, and he was out, and his father was in, and his father does not look at me, which suited me.

I went into the room with the buckets and took the motherboard down off its nail.

There is a board under the third bucket that comes up if you know it comes up, and I have known since I was nine. I put the fob under it, the lion, cartoon, plastic, the kind you would give a child, and I put the board back, and I stood on it, and it did not creak.

Then I hung the motherboard on its nail, straight, as he likes it, between the skull and the hubcap.

He will find it. Not soon. But he is a boy who takes things apart, and one day he will be in that room with nothing to do.

I walked home along the shore counting the ships. There are four of them and there have always been four and they have not moved in my lifetime and they will not move in his. It came out even, the way it does.
