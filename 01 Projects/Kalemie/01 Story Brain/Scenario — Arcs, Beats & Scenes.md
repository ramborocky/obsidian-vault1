## VOICE NOTE

The existing draft channels Marlon James — the found-object cataloguing, the "This is what she was wearing" structure. That's the mystery's skeleton. But the story's actual wound is a girl entering womanhood in a body the whole town has opinions about before she does — and that register belongs less to James than to the writers who sit inside that fear from the inside: Adichie's unsentimental clarity, Bulawayo's child-logic slamming into adult violence, Owuor's lake-and-body lyricism. The new prose below (the ending, and Djamela's fragment) is written from that register — close, embodied, a little breathless, the anxiety of a girl doing arithmetic on her own body before anyone taught her the numbers. Not a costume over the existing voice. A second instrument the story was missing.

## STORY BRAIN — FILLED

**One Sentence:** In a lake town where wealth arrives in tinted glass and justice arrives in an envelope, a boy who collects dead things has to decide whether remembering a girl is its own kind of justice, now that every other kind has failed her.

**One Paragraph:** Daudi, fifteen, narrates in retrospect from the morning he learns his best friend Djamela is dead and the wrong man — Morisho, the dala dala conductor — has been arrested for it. He rewinds to the months before: Djamela's hunger to be one of the "ghost kids" from Rue Saint Michel, her thirteenth birthday wish to be Kim Kardashian, the afternoon a man calling himself Jean Luc pulls alongside their dala dala stop in a BMW and gives her a new name — Chantal — before he gives her anything else. Daudi and Djamela share one unrepeated intimacy in the rain; weeks later, at the police station to report a hit-and-run, Djamela reveals she's pregnant, and Daudi watches Jean Luc buy his way out of the accusation with an envelope that leaves thinner than it arrived. Djamela disappears. When Daudi finally puts together what he already half-knew, he has to choose: chase the man who did it, or do the only thing left inside his power — keep her.

**Narrative Question (surface):** Who is responsible for Djamela's death, and will anyone answer for it?
**Narrative Question (true):** Did Daudi see what was happening and choose not to speak — and if the system makes justice impossible, is memory enough?

**Theme / Controlling Idea:** *Inaction is complicity, because choosing not to act is still a choice.*
**Counter-Idea (the story must earn its answer against this, not ignore it):** *Action is futile, because the system is rigged to protect whoever can afford the envelope.*
**The story's actual position (resolved in the ending below):** Both are true at once, at different scales. Systemically, action is futile — Jean Luc will always have a fatter envelope than the truth. Personally, inaction is still complicity — so Daudi finds the one form of action the system can't buy back: he remembers her on purpose, in public, permanently, nailed to a wall. Not justice. The nearest thing to it that's actually his.

**Time Span:** A single Congolese lake-town year, telescoped through one frame morning of retrospect.
**Setting:** Kalemie, DRC — Lake Tanganyika shorefront (Mihogoni, the "down part of town") against Rue Saint Michel's guarded mansions; the dala dala; the lakeside hotel; Ave de Bobozo police station.

**Structure:**
- **Beginning:** The frame — Daudi arrives at the dala dala stop to a wrongness he can name before he can explain it. Morisho's been taken. A girl is dead. He doesn't say her name yet.
- **Middle:** The flashback — Djamela's want made concrete (birthday), her threshold crossed (Jean Luc, renaming), her one unguarded hour with Daudi (the rain), the system's betrayal made visible (the envelope).
- **End:** Daudi chooses memory over confrontation, returns to the frame's stop, says her name, nails the last found object to the wall.

**Plot Points (TRD — Turn / Reveal / Decision):**
- **TRD1 — Turn (end of Act 1):** Jean Luc's SUV stops. Djamela answers to "Chantal." She has crossed into his world before he's given her anything but a name.
- **TRD2 — Reveal (midpoint, emotional):** The rain scene. Daudi has one unrepeated chance at honesty with her and freezes. This is what he's really mourning later — not just her death, but his own silence, rehearsed once already.
- **TRD3 — Reveal (institutional):** The police station. The envelope goes in fat, comes out thin. The system's price is now visible, not implied.
- **TRD4 — Turn (Crisis approaches):** Frame catches up to present — Djamela is dead, Morisho is arrested for it. The wrong man pays because he's the one who couldn't afford not to be caught.
- **TRD5 — Decision (Climax):** Daudi chooses not to confront Jean Luc. He goes, instead, to where the fob key still leads, retrieves what's left of the evidence, and nails it to his wall.

## CHARACTER ARCS — ALL FIVE

*(Daudi, Djamela, and Jean Luc already have rich character files — arcs summarized here for the beat sheet. D'Juma and Morisho's files are still blank templates; full arcs given here since the story doesn't work without them.)*

### Daudi — Protagonist
**Start:** A collector, not an actor. Names things instead of touching them — physics vocabulary as armor against a world he's not ready to be hurt by.
**Turn:** Freezes in the one moment intimacy asks something of him (the rain). Watches, silent, as Jean Luc buys his way out at the station.
**Crisis:** Confront Jean Luc (which he cannot win) or do nothing (which is what got Djamela here)?
**End:** A third option the story earns rather than states — witnessing as its own act. He doesn't save her. He keeps her. *Passive collector → active keeper.*

### Djamela — Deuteragonist (not just "supporting" — this scenario treats her as co-protagonist of the emotional arc)
**Start:** Wants to be seen as more than Mihogoni. Rehearses a European register (French, brand names, "anchovy" instead of dagaa) like a costume that hasn't been fitted yet.
**Turn:** Answers to Chantal before Jean Luc has paid for anything. The renaming *is* the transaction — everything after is just settling the account.
**Crisis:** Pregnant, unmarried, exposed — and the one institution meant to protect her turns her disclosure into leverage for a bribe, not evidence.
**End:** Dies off-page, discovered exactly the way Daudi discovers everything — as an object among objects. The tragedy the story insists on: she is never once seen as fully herself by anyone in it, including, for one costly night, Daudi. *Wanting to be chosen → being priced.*

### Jean Luc — Antagonist
Fully built in [[Antagonist - Jean Luc]] — halo-effect predator who believes himself a benefactor. Arc: confidence never cracks except once (Djamela's pregnancy disclosure), covered instantly with charm. *He is a system, not a person. Systems don't arc — that's the horror, not a flaw in the design.*

### D'Juma — Djamela's father (Supporting — currently blank file)
**Core Wound:** Watched colonial and post-colonial extraction empty Kalemie of anything he could hand his daughter besides proverbs and a fruit stall.
**Lie he believes:** That refusing the West (no cake, no Kim Kardashian, "that's a western thing") protects Djamela from wanting it. It doesn't — it just makes the want a secret.
**Want:** For Djamela to be proud of what they have.
**Need:** To say, once, plainly, without a parable standing in front of it, that he is afraid for her.
**Arc:** Speaks in proverbs and refusals (the watermelon, the lake speech) for the entire story — a way of loving that never risks the words "I'm scared for you." After her death, he says one plain sentence to Daudi, unparabled, the first ungoverned thing he's said in the story. That sentence is what finally lets Daudi understand the lake speech wasn't wisdom performed at Daudi — it was D'Juma rehearsing how to survive losing her, before he had. *Parable as armor → one plain sentence as grief.*
**Function in the ending:** Reframes "the lake puts things back in order" — not comfort, correction. The lake doesn't return things. It keeps them, unchanged, at the bottom, forever. Daudi takes this as instruction, not solace: if the lake keeps things by refusing to let them go, so can he.

### Morisho — Dala dala conductor (Supporting — currently blank file)
**Core Wound:** School dropout, poor, publicly street-smart, privately ashamed of exactly the "konda" (wasted) future he warns Anitha away from becoming.
**Lie he believes:** That small kindnesses (the two-thousand-shilling note for school fees, the free coin for Djamela) balance the ledger of what men like him take from girls like her — attention, a joke, a hand too familiar on a door frame.
**Want:** To be liked by the girls he half-protects, half-preys on, without having to sit inside that contradiction.
**Need:** To be seen as more than a convenient arrest.
**Arc:** Genuine, crude, protective-and-predatory in the same breath (McKee's "complexity, not contradiction" test — same as Jean Luc's halo, played in a lower register) — until he becomes the story's proof of the Controlling Idea's dark twin: *the system doesn't need the real predator caught. It needs someone caught.* He's visible, poor, and unable to afford an envelope. That's the whole trial. *Street-smart protector → convenient sacrifice.*
**Function in the plot:** The red herring who was never really a herring — he's guilty of nothing specific to Djamela's death, but the story doesn't let the reader feel clean about that, because he's guilty of the smaller, uncriminal version of what Jean Luc does, and the town lets one man stand for both.

## MCKEE BEAT SHEET — FULL STRUCTURE

**Genre convention audit:** Social Drama (irresponsible individual vs. corrupt institution) fused with a Mystery engine (found-object procedural, Daudi as unlicensed investigator) — per [[Mastery of Genres]] / [[Mixing Genres]], the fusion is the point: mystery gives the reader forward pull, social drama gives the ending its refusal to resolve cleanly.

**Design:** Archplot leaning miniplot — closed narrative question (we learn what happened), open moral ending (nothing is fixed, the system continues). Retrospective frame narration throughout — see [[Linear Vs Non-Linear time]].

**On moving Scene 6 (Rain):** the Dashboard's own question asks whether it should move earlier. Recommendation: **keep it at position 6.** Moving it earlier would let the reader grieve too soon and flatten the Gap at the police station — the reader needs to still be hoping Daudi might say something to her, the way he didn't in the rain, right up until the envelope closes that possibility for good. The rain scene's job is to make the reader complicit in Daudi's hope. Move it and you remove the thing the ending is punishing.

## THE ENDING (Fix #5 — written)

*Full circle to the frame's opening stop, fused with D'Juma's corrected lake image (Option A + B combined, per the Dashboard's own instinct that neither alone answers all three of the ending's required questions).*

> I did not go looking for Jean Luc. I went looking for the fob shaped like a lion, because a boy who collects things does not stop collecting them just because the last thing broke his heart.
>
> It was under her mattress, where the physics notes used to be. I do not know how it got there instead of in her pocket, and I have decided not to know.
>
> D'juma found me at the lakeshore two evenings after they buried her. He didn't speak in proverbs that time.
>
> "I was wrong," he said. "The lake doesn't put things back in order. It keeps them. There's a difference."
>
> I understood him the way you understand a bruise — after, when you press it on purpose to check it's still there.
>
> I went home. I took down the antelope skull and the hubcap and made room. I nailed the motherboard to the wall between them, the way you nail a name to a door so the house remembers who lived there.
>
> It is not justice. Nobody will go to prison for the space on my wall.
>
> But the lake keeps things, and now so do I. That is the whole of what I have to give her, and I am done pretending it is nothing.

*(139 words. No "felt/knew/realized." One metaphor, physics-and-lake sourced, doing the closing work per Cleland's rule. Resolves TRD5, answers all three Structural Notes questions: the lake does not vindicate D'Juma's original comfort — it corrects into something colder and truer; entropy wins systemically, but Daudi's private counter-act — nailing, naming, keeping — is the story's one instance of something holding; Daudi's answer to "did he look away" is: yes, once, in the rain, and he is not doing it again.)*

## DJAMELA'S INTERIORITY (the missing scene — full text)

*Placed after the police station, before the frame catches up. First person, Djamela. This is the fragment written fully in the requested register — a girl doing the arithmetic of her own body before anyone taught her the numbers. Also logged as trunk material for the cycle's Story #2 candidate per [[Collection Tracker]].*

> Nobody tells you the exact day your body stops being only yours. Mine picked a Tuesday. I was counting days on my fingers behind the school gate, the way you count out coins you already know aren't enough, and Alicia was talking about somebody's hairdo, and I was doing arithmetic nobody taught me on purpose. Mama taught me what to ask for. Nobody taught me what to do with what I'd already given.
>
> I am not stupid. I want that written down somewhere, since nobody at that station wrote it down. I knew what Jean Luc was buying when he covered the note with his phone number. I have known what men are buying since I was eleven and Auntie's neighbour let his hand rest too long on the back of my chair, and everybody in the room decided not to see it, the way you decide not to see a crack in a wall you're renting.
>
> I told myself the baby was proof I existed to somebody the way Rue Saint Michel exists — solid, deeded, worth building around. I know now that a man like Jean Luc doesn't build around anything. He passes through. That is the whole design of him — leather seats, a window that goes down with a sound like a secret, a name he hands you like it's a gift and not a leash.
<br>
> Daudi thinks I don't know what I gave up in that room. He watched me come out bent, and he thinks it was shame bending me. It wasn't shame. It was arithmetic again — counting what was left in that envelope against what I'd need it to be, and coming up short, the way I always come up short, the way Mihogoni always comes up short against Rue Saint Michel, no matter how straight you hold your back getting out of the car.
>
> I am afraid of my own body some mornings. Not of what's growing in it. Of how fast it decided things for me that I thought I was deciding for myself.
>
> I have not told Daudi any of this. He still thinks the twenty seconds in the rain were the beginning of something. I let him think it. It is the only thing I've given him that costs me nothing to give.

*(Craft note: written with Cleland's rhythm — long sentences for interiority, short ones ("I am not stupid.") for rupture, no banned labelling verbs beyond the two deliberate uses of "know" marking real epistemic claims, not passive perception. Metaphor sourced from Djamela's own vocabulary — money, arithmetic, real estate — mirroring how Daudi's metaphors come from physics. This is the asymmetry the whole story runs on: he measures her in entropy, she measures herself in accounts receivable.)*
