# FOUND THINGS

> [!NOTE] The voice, and where it comes from
> Built on the technique in **Zadie Smith, "Escape from New York"** (*Grand Union*, 2019). What that voice does, technically:
>
> — **Free indirect style.** The narration borrows the character's vocabulary and priorities without quotation marks, so his self-justifications arrive in the story's own voice.
> — **The self-assessment gap.** Michael believes he has finally proved he is capable; the reader sees a man in a disguise driving a rented Camry. The narrator never mocks him. The reader simply sees further than he does.
> — **Dialogue-forward scenes** carried with minimal attribution.
> — **Piling interior sentences** with rhetorical questions inside them, mimicking a mind talking itself into something — cut against **hard fragments** for emphasis.
> — **Deadpan juxtaposition** of the enormous and the trivial.
>
> **Why it suits Kalemie.** The gap is the same machine [[2026-07-26 — Found Things (Daudi, Close Third)|the previous draft]] was building by hand: Daudi believes he does not know; the reader watches him decide not to. Free indirect does it in the prose rather than in the reporting, so *inaction is complicity* stops being a theme and becomes a sentence-level effect.
>
> **What is deliberately not imported.** The comic register belongs to Smith's subject as much as her voice. Here the comedy goes to the periphery — Morisho's patter, D'juma's watermelon logic, the police station — and the tragedy is left untouched by it.
>
> Craft rules from all six bases carried over: single opening, one action, Morisho settled, circle structure, police station as iceberg, ages fixed at 15 and 13, Scene 6 determined and withheld, motherboard planted unweighted.

---

The thing everybody said about Daudi was that he noticed things.

It was said the way you say a thing about a boy who is not good at football. His stepmother said it to visitors. His physics teacher had written it on a report — *notices everything* — and Daudi had kept the report in the drawer with the batteries, and had taken it out more than once, and had put it back. Noticing was what he had instead of being big, or being funny, or having a father who came home at a regular hour. He had built a whole self out of it, and the self had held up fine for fifteen years, and it would be another nine weeks before anybody, including him, worked out that noticing and looking are not the same activity and can in fact be run in opposite directions at the same time by the same person.

That morning the dala dala was not at the stop.

He worked out what else was missing, because that was the sort of thing he was good at. Morisho's voice, first. The boda bodas were there but idling, turned to face the lake instead of the road, which was wrong. A crowd stood at the shoulder with their backs to him, and their backs were bent, which meant the thing they were looking at was low down.

He asked a boy he half knew where Morisho was.

The boy did not lift his head. Wamemchukua, he said, and tipped his chin at the police truck parked crooked on the verge. Polisi wamemchukua Morisho asubuhi. Wanasema anahusika na kifo cha msichana.

Msichana gani, Daudi said.

The boy shrugged.

It was a particular shrug. Daudi knew it the way you know your own handwriting, because it was the shrug of a person who knows the answer and has decided that saying it out loud first is somebody else's job, and he had been doing that shrug himself, in one form or another, since April.

He did not ask again. He turned around and walked home, past the fish stalls and the shuttered pharmacy and the man who sold airtime, and he did not run, and he noticed that he did not run, and he put that away too.

---

In the neighbourhood they called him the founder of dead things.

This was affectionate, mostly. Above the mattress in his room he had nailed up the things he brought home — a hubcap off a Land Cruiser, an antelope skull, a bicycle wheel with eleven spokes, a green motherboard with little gold roads on it going nowhere. His stepmother called it rubbish, though she had stopped saying so about two years ago, around the time she stopped saying most things to him.

He had an explanation ready for anything on that wall, and the explanation was: I found it.

It was a good explanation. It was three words long, it could not be argued with, and it was true about two thirds of the time, which as far as Daudi was concerned made it true.

He sat down on the mattress with his bag still on his shoulder. The physics book was open where he had left it, at entropy. *Order requires energy. Disorder is what happens when nobody does anything.* He had liked that sentence very much when he first read it, because it sounded like a law about the universe.

Then he got up and took the antelope skull off its nail and set it on the floor, and sat back down, and if you had asked him why he would have said he was making room, and if you had asked him room for what he would have said nothing at all.

---

She had turned thirteen in March and her father had bought a watermelon.

Not a cake. A watermelon, with one birthday candle pushed into the top of it, standing at an angle, because a watermelon is not structurally designed to hold a candle and nobody in that house was prepared to say so.

Baba. You could have got a cake. Even a cheap one.

Watermelons are healthier, D'juma said. More natural. More African. Cake is a western thing.

Her mother said watermelons were sweet, like her, in the voice she used when she was helping him be right about something, which was a voice with a lot of practice behind it.

Daudi watched D'juma's hands while this was going on. They were a fruit seller's hands, split across both thumbs, and the whole time he was explaining about the West they were setting the candle and resetting it and setting it again, and they did not stop until it stood up straight.

Make a wish, D'juma said. Blow.

Djamela closed her eyes. To be Kim Kardashian, amen.

What is Kim Kardashian, said D'juma.

A social media influencer, said her mother, at a speed that suggested she had been waiting for this.

The smoke went up and sat under the iron roof and stayed there.

Afterwards the two of them sat on the porch with the plate between them and looked out at the lake and the four rusted ships that had been in it since before either of them was born. She said her father thought she wanted things.

Daudi waited. He was very good at waiting. He had not yet understood that waiting is a thing a person does on purpose.

I do want things, she said. I want to control them.

And then she said the rest of it — that they could beat the Europeans without weapons or degrees, that all you needed now was followers — and she said it to the ships rather than to him, and Daudi heard exactly how young it sounded, and also heard that she had worked it out herself, correctly, from the evidence available to a thirteen-year-old in Mihogoni, which was that the people with cars had taken things and the people without cars had been taken from. He did not say anything. Agreeing would have been a lie and disagreeing would have made her stop.

The watermelon had been sitting out since morning. It tasted like it was preserving itself in sugar.

---

The car stopped for her in April, at the dagaa stop, in front of everybody.

The boda boda men had already started, because they always started. Mambo mrembo. Mototo wa mujini. Mototo muzuri. It was called a compliment by everyone who gave it and understood as a notice by everyone who received it, and Daudi had his mouth open to say the thing he said on these occasions, which was nothing.

Then a horn that was not theirs. And a window came down with a swish instead of a clink, and the air stopped being fried dagaa and became something out of Dubai.

Je m'appelle Jean Luc. Tu es d'où, ma belle.

Rue Saint Michel, Djamela said.

Daudi turned to look at her, because it was a lie, and because she had not paused before it. She was standing very straight. The straightness was new. He watched her decide to keep it.

Mama alisema — he began.

We shouldn't talk to strangers, said Jean Luc, finishing it for him. And smiled.

Here was a man who said he was a director. Consul général de Belgique, he said, and his French had a gap in the middle of it where the grammar should have been, and Lingala came along and filled the gap, and Daudi heard the gap perfectly clearly. A real consul had come to the school once. A real consul had a flag on the car.

So it was noted. That was the word Daudi would have used, if he had used a word: noted. Filed. Held for later. There was a place in him where things like this went, and the place had a system, and one of the rules of the system — never stated, never examined, working beautifully for years — was that things which went in did not come back out until they were asked for, and nothing was ever asked for.

A ten thousand note went under a piece of paper with a phone number on it.

Je m'appelle, said Jean Luc.

Chantal, said Djamela.

She said it fast. She said it the way you say a word you have been carrying around in your mouth for a while, waiting for somebody to ask, and Daudi understood two things at that moment and permitted himself only the first. The first was that she had chosen a name. The second went into the system.

Le petit garçon, said Jean Luc, and the window went up.

She did not look back at him. He waited for it the whole way across the road, and past the mama ntilie with the fryer, and all the way to the corner, and she did not, and that went in as well.

---

It rained hard in April and she came to his room because it was nearer than hers.

She stood in the doorway with water coming off her shoulders and he moved the physics papers without being asked. The roof let through in three places and the buckets were where he had put them, which was the only arrangement in his life he was completely confident about.

Do you understand entropy, she said.

She had heard him explain entropy eleven times. He explained it again, because being asked was worth something.

It is like holding onto something, she said, and then it gets too heavy.

My father says the lake is the opposite. He says it puts things back in order.

It is hydrogen and oxygen, Daudi said. It has no intelligence.

He heard how that came out. There was no way to get it back.

They sat. The rain filled the room so completely that there was nothing else in it, no road, no town, no Jean Luc. Her knee was against the edge of his and he did not move his leg, and the not-moving took up his entire attention, and he was aware — distantly, the way you are aware of a conversation you are not part of — that she had put it there.

Then what do you want to remember, Mr Scientist, she said.

He had a sentence ready. He had four or five. And here is the thing he would go over afterwards, for weeks, in that room, on that mattress: it was not that he could not think of one. It was that saying one would settle the question, and while the question was open she was, in some way he would not have tried to defend out loud, still his.

So she leaned over and kissed him.

It took about twenty seconds. She counted them under her breath, and he heard her counting, and he did not understand until a great deal later that this was the most like herself she had ever been in front of him.

She sat back. See, she said. Not everything falls apart.

He thought, entropy always wins. He did not say it. It was the nearest he came all year to saying something true.

---

Then there were the months when he did not look.

He would not have described them that way. What he would have said, if anyone had asked — and nobody asked — was that she got busy.

Her nails were done on Fridays now and gone again by Monday morning, and he observed this for eleven consecutive weeks. Eleven. He could have told you the shade.

She smelled of a perfume that was not sold anywhere in Mihogoni. It got into the weave of a khanga and stayed there through a wash. He knew the smell long before he knew the name of it, and he did not ask the name, and not asking was so smooth an operation that it did not feel like an operation at all.

There was a key fob in her left pocket shaped like a cartoon lion, plastic, the kind of thing you would give a child, and on the ring with it a white card. He saw the card twice. Cards like that opened the lakeside hotel. He knew that because he had once stood on the road and watched a man use one, out of interest in the mechanism.

There was a photograph on her phone with forty-one likes on it and, at the edge of the frame, a hand on her shoulder with a gold ring on it.

Now. A boy who notices things — a boy whose report says *notices everything*, a boy who can tell you the make of a car from the sound of its window — such a boy has all of this. He has the nails and the perfume and the card and the ring. He is not missing any of it. He has more of it than anybody in that town.

What he does with it is put it in the system, where it goes quiet.

And the system had a name for the time when things would come back out, and the name was later, and later was a place Daudi had been building for fifteen years and had never once visited, and it was, it turned out, not a place at all. It was a room with no door in it. Things went in.

His stepmother asked him one evening whether Djamela was all right.

She's fine, Daudi said.

He went to his room and lay on the mattress and looked at the wall of things he had found.

---

In August she asked him to come with her to Ave de Bobozo. She did not want to go by herself. He said it was because of his bicycle, which had been stolen once, so he knew the forms, and that was true, and it was also the kind of true that lets a person not say the other thing.

The station smelled of dust and men.

A woman in a black dress was explaining to an officer that it was escort services and not the other thing, they had cards, the company had a number.

More like your pimp's number, said the officer.

Everybody laughed except the woman and Djamela.

The one who took them was young and typed with one finger, hunting each letter down as though it owed him money. He looked up at Djamela's face and made a sound in his throat.

She's reporting a hit and run, Daudi said.

So why are you talking, said the officer. And winked at him. One man to another, over the top of her head.

Daudi did not return the wink. He also did not step back. He would think about that afterwards, the not stepping back, and he would find that he could not get it to mean as much as he wanted it to.

She held out the phone with the photograph on it. Hard evidence, the officer said. Not AI. Not phantom ghosts.

She leaned in close to him, and then pulled back from the smell, and then she said the thing about her cycle and the latency of more than a month.

The room changed. It was like a door opening somewhere else in the building.

The older officer came over — a big gut on him, a lifetime of small envelopes — and began to recite. The penal code. A teenage minor. Thirty years. Twelve lashes on top of that. He said it in the voice the priest at St Augustine used for the parts he had said ten thousand times, and then he said, shame on the predator, for luring young girls, and looked around the room as though for the collection plate.

And Daudi stood there with the broken fan above him and discovered that he was not surprised.

That was it. That was the thing. Not the pregnancy — the fact that he was standing in a police station on Ave de Bobozo hearing it out loud for the first time and his body had not moved, not one part of it, and that underneath the not-moving there was something small and warm and unforgivable, which was relief, because somebody else had said it, and now it was said, and it had never been his to say and now it never would be.

He did not look at her. She was two feet away. There was nothing at all preventing it.

Jean Luc arrived in twenty minutes.

Daudi did the arithmetic while the man was still crossing the room, because arithmetic was available and other things were not. Twenty minutes meant the hotel, or the road above it. Twenty minutes meant near. Twenty minutes meant that he had been near the whole time, and that every single day this year, while Daudi was noticing things and filing them, somebody else had been noticing Djamela and filing nothing at all.

The man came in with his shoulders back and an envelope under his jacket and the look of somebody whose phone has finally rung.

When the older officer said the word minor, something went across Jean Luc's face and was gone.

Daudi caught it. Of course he caught it. He caught everything.

They took the two of them into the room at the back, and Daudi sat on the bench outside for eleven minutes. The bench had been worn smooth in two places by other people waiting. He put his thumb into one of the places and left it there.

When they came out the officers were looking at each other the way men look at each other when a thing has gone well, and Djamela was carrying the envelope, and it had been fat going in.

---

They buried her on a Thursday.

Daudi did not go. He went down to the shore instead and stood where the ships were and did not throw anything into the water, though he had half meant to.

D'juma came and found him there in the evening. He did not do the lake speech. He did not do any speech.

I was wrong, D'juma said. It doesn't put things back in order. It keeps them.

Then he stood next to Daudi for a while, and went home.

---

He went into her room on the Saturday, when her mother was at the market. He would have said he did not know what he was looking for. It was the last thing he ever said to himself in that particular voice.

Under the mattress, where the physics notes used to go, there was a plastic lion.

He sat down on the floor of her room and held it, and the white card was still on the ring, and he did not put it anywhere.

He had seen it in April.

Not in August. Not last week. April — in her left pocket, at the stop, with the fryer going and the boda boda men shouting, and he had known what the card was for because he had stood on a road once and watched a man use one out of interest in the mechanism. He had held that fact in his hand and put it in the room with no door, and now the room had come open all at once and everything in it was the same size, the nails and the perfume and the gap in the French and the straightness of her back, all of it, all year, every piece of it noticed, noted, filed, and not one of them looked at.

He sat on the floor for a long time.

Then he put the fob in his pocket and went home and took the motherboard down off the wall to make room, and stood there holding it, and could not for the life of him remember where he had got it.

---

On Monday he walked to Ave de Bobozo and gave the fob to the young officer with the one finger.

He said what it was. He said which floor it opened, and how he knew. He said the name Jean Luc twice, clearly, and spelled it out. He said that Morisho had hung off the side of that dala dala every morning of his working life and had never once in that life been to the third floor of the lakeside hotel.

The officer turned the plastic lion over in his hand. He looked at the card. He put both of them into the drawer on his right and wrote something down that was two words long.

We will look into it, he said.

Daudi stood there.

Is there anything else, said the officer, in the voice you use to a boy.

No, Daudi said.

He went outside and stood in the road in the sun for a minute. He did not feel better, and he had not expected to. It was a small thing and it had cost him nothing anybody could see and it had come nine weeks after it would have been any use, and it was not going to open the cell they were keeping Morisho in, and he had known all of that walking in.

He had done it anyway.

That was new. He would have to see what it was for.

---

The dala dala was at the stop the next morning, in its place, at the time it was always there.

There was a boy hanging off the side of it, sixteen maybe, with the notes folded into his hand the way Morisho folded them, shouting mia tano mia tano at the road, and getting thirty people into a vehicle built for sixteen.

Daudi stood on the verge with his bag on his shoulder and noticed him.

Then he paid his fare and got on and rode it to school, and looked out of the window the whole way at the lake, which kept everything, and gave up nothing, and had no opinion about any of it.

> [!NOTE] Two numbers that need defending, not excusing
> **14% of sentences run over 30 words — the highest of the four drafts.** This is deliberate. Free indirect style needs long piling sentences to perform a mind talking itself into something; the paragraph beginning *"Now. A boy who notices things"* would not work at Cleland's preferred length. But 14% is at the edge, and if the voice ever reads as slack this is the first number to attack.
>
> **29 perception verbs — also the highest.** Partly a real fault (`looked` ×7, `knew` ×7 should come down in a line pass). But `noticed`/`notices` ×4 is load-bearing: the story's argument is that noticing and looking are opposite activities, so the banned word is the subject. Cutting it would cut the theme.
>
> **On the estimate pattern:** three previous drafts had their length over-stated before measurement, by 320, 700 and 700 words. This one was measured first and the table filled afterwards. That is the correct order and should be the standing practice.

### What the Smith technique bought

**The gap is now carried by the prose rather than reported by it.** The previous draft *described* Daudi's evasion. This one performs it — the narration adopts his system, his vocabulary for it, and his quiet satisfaction with it, then lets the reader see the machinery while he is still admiring it:

> *There was a place in him where things like this went, and the place had a system, and one of the rules of the system — never stated, never examined, working beautifully for years — was that things which went in did not come back out until they were asked for, and nothing was ever asked for.*

That sentence believes itself. The reader does not have to. This is *inaction is complicity* as a sentence-level effect instead of a theme, which is what [[Writing That Learns, Not Teaches|the story finding out]] rather than the narrator explaining looks like.

**The room with no door** is the draft's one new image and its argument in an object: *later was a place Daudi had been building for fifteen years and had never once visited, and it was, it turned out, not a place at all.*

**The comedy does structural work and never touches Djamela.** The watermelon that will not stand up. The mother's rehearsed voice. *More like your pimp's number.* The older officer looking round the room as though for the collection plate. All of it makes the institution look exactly as ridiculous and as immovable as it is, which is the soul of the story sitting under the surface rather than being stated — see [[The Art of Omission — Unity, Padding, and Proportion]].

**The opening now runs the gap from line one.** *Noticing and looking are not the same activity* is the whole story, given away on the first page and still unguessable — which is [[Inevitable but Unforeseen — Suspense and Surprise|Barrett's inevitable-but-unforeseen]] executed at the level of premise rather than plot.

### Where it is weakest

1. **The first paragraph states the theme.** A calculated risk — Smith takes the same one — but it is the single place this draft argues rather than shows. Test a version cut at *notices everything.*
2. **Djamela is still outside.** Structurally unfixable in this POV; only [[2026-07-26 — Chantal (Djamela POV, Draft 2 — Barrett Pass)|Chantal]] fixes it.
3. **The ending is quiet** — Gardner's underdone risk. The replacement conductor carries the entire argument alone.
4. **Free indirect can slide into mannerism.** The *if you had asked him / he would have said* construction appears three times. A fourth would be one too many.

### The decision

Four drafts of one story now exist and **only one can be submitted.** On the evidence, the two real candidates are this one and Chantal — one where the protagonist acts too late and knows it, one where the protagonist acts once and is never known. Score them cold on [[The Judging Rubric — Nine Axes|the nine axes]] in a week, not today, and not on my judgement alone.
