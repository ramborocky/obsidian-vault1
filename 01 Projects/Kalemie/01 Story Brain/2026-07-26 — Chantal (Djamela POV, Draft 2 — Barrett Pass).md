# CHANTAL

> [!NOTE] What changed from Draft 1, and why
> Draft 1 is kept at [[2026-07-26 — Chantal (Djamela POV Draft)]]. This pass runs the whole story against [[Short Story Writing by Charles Raymond Barrett|Barrett]]. Almost every change is a cut.
>
> — **The aphorism rate is down by roughly two-thirds.** Barrett's warning about the first-person narrator who "prefers moralizing and philosophizing to the legitimate furthering of the plot" ([[Methods of Narration — The Author Must Vanish]]) and his rule that a story saddled with a moral "ceases to be a story and becomes an argument" ([[The Art of Omission — Unity, Padding, and Proportion]]) both land on the same sentences [[The Funhouse Mirror — AI Output as Cliché Detector|the mirror test]] flagged. Draft 1 closed a paragraph with a neat inversion roughly twenty times. This one does it five.
> — **Sentence rhythm rebalanced.** Draft 1 ran 63% of sentences under ten words — past Cleland's guidance and into what Barrett calls the "bumpety-bump" style. Target here is nearer 50%, with more medium declaratives carrying the interior passages.
> — **The ending now stops at the act.** Barrett: climax and conclusion should be as nearly simultaneous as possible, and the climax proper runs about six words. Draft 1 ended on three paragraphs of reflection about the lake. This one ends on what she does.
> — **Morisho's fate is paid off.** Barrett requires the climax to decide the destinies of *all* characters and the fate of all their schemes. Neither previous draft disposed of the innocent man arrested for her death. She now foresees it — which costs 40 words and is the sharpest thing in the story.
> — **Two characters consolidated.** Alicia and Béatrice merged into one girl. [[The Isolation Principle — One Emotion, One Incident|Barrett's ceiling is two speaking parts]]; this does not reach it, but it stops adding.
> — **Title kept.** "Chantal" passes all five of [[Titles Good and Bad|Barrett's title tests]]; "Kalemie" fails three.

Nobody tells you the exact day your body stops being only yours. Mine picked a Tuesday.

I was behind the school gate counting on my fingers, the way you count coins when you already know they are not enough. Alicia was talking about somebody's hairdo. Thirty-four days. I counted twice, and the first time I got thirty-three, and I wanted the smaller number to be the true one, so I counted again and it got bigger.

That is not how counting is supposed to work.

The bell went. Alicia took my arm the way she does and I let her, and we walked in, and I sat down at a desk with a wobble in it and opened a book about the digestive system of a cow.

I am telling you about the Tuesday first because everybody wants to start with the man. The man comes later. The man is not the beginning of anything except his own story.

---

We live in Mihogoni, the down part of town, where the road stops being a road and becomes a suggestion. My father sells fruit on Ave de Tanganyique in the mornings and lays his hands on people in the afternoons, and both of these are jobs, and neither is the kind you say out loud at school.

In Primary Six, Alicia told a whole row that our house smelled of fish, back when she was somebody else's friend and not mine. Everybody laughed. I laughed too, because that is the fastest way to stop being the thing people are laughing at.

Our house does smell of fish. That is not the part that stayed with me.

There are children in this town we call the ghost kids. They go to Monseigneur André Kaseba and they wear checkered skirts with ties and blazers and they sit in the back of their parents' cars looking at nothing. When our dala dala stops beside one of those cars at the lights on Ave de Industrielle, we look in and they do not look out. Not because they are proud. Because we are not there.

Morisho hangs off the side of the dala dala and shouts mia tano mia tano until his voice goes. He told us once that those children are ghosts to us, that we will never see them or talk to them. He meant it as a warning. I heard it as a price list.

---

I turned thirteen in March and my father bought a watermelon.

Not a cake. A watermelon, with one candle pushed into the top of it, standing crooked because a watermelon is not built to hold a candle, which anybody could have told him.

Baba, I said, you could have got a cake. Even a cheap one.

Watermelons are healthier, he said. More natural. More African. Cakes are a western thing.

My mother said watermelons are sweet, like you, Djamela, in the voice she uses when she is helping him be right about something.

I want to be fair to him, because I have not always been. He is not a stupid man. He was handed a town that had been emptied out twice before he got to it, and he has nothing to give me except proverbs and fruit, and he decided that if he refused the world outside Kalemie hard enough I would stop wanting it. It did not work like that. It only made the wanting private.

Make a wish, he said. Blow.

I closed my eyes and said: to be Kim Kardashian, amen.

What is Kim Kardashian, my father said.

A social media influencer, my mother said, fast, the way you move a knife off a table.

I blew the candle out. The smoke went up and sat under the iron roof and did not leave.

Later I sat on the porch with Daudi and looked at the lake with the rusted ships in it. He thinks I want things, I said.

Daudi said nothing, which is what Daudi does.

I do want things, I said. I want to control them. I want to conquer, the way the Europeans did, except now you do not need weapons or degrees. You only need followers.

I was thirteen years and one hour old and I believed all of it. Nobody made me believe it. I worked it out myself, from the evidence in front of me, which was a town where the people with cars had taken things and the people without cars had been taken from.

---

Daudi is the boy from two houses down. He is a year and a half older than me and he has a wall in his room where he nails things he has found. A hubcap. The skull of an antelope. A bicycle wheel I am fairly sure he did not find.

He calls it a collection. Everybody else calls it rubbish.

His mother died having him, which is a thing that gets said about him rather than by him, and he has built a whole woman out of that absence — patient, soft, never once disappointed in him, because a woman who never existed does not have to be. He has never said one word of it to me. I know it the way you know the shape of a room in the dark.

When we were small I gave him a motherboard.

I found it behind the internet café that used to be by the market, in a heap of things that had stopped working. It was green, with little gold roads on it going nowhere, and I thought it was the most beautiful broken thing I had ever seen. I gave it to him because he was the only person I knew who would understand that a thing can be beautiful after it stops being useful.

He nailed it to the wall between the skull and the hubcap. He has never once mentioned where he got it. I do not think he remembers it was me. Daudi thinks he found everything.

---

The car was a BMW, black, with windows you could not see into.

We had got off the dala dala at the dagaa stop and the boda boda men were doing what boda boda men do, which is call you beautiful in a way that is not a compliment but a notice — a way of saying that everyone has agreed you are available for comment now.

Then a horn, different from theirs. The window came down with a sound I had never heard a window make. Not the clink of a handle going round. A swish, like a word said quietly on purpose.

Je m'appelle Jean Luc, he said. Tu es d'où, ma belle.

I want to tell this part exactly, because everybody who has heard it since has decided they already know.

I said: Rue Saint Michel.

I lied immediately and easily and without deciding to. Rue Saint Michel is where the ghost children live and I have never set foot on it, and it came out of my mouth as though it had been sitting behind my teeth for years, waiting.

Daudi started saying the thing about strangers. Mama alisema —

We shouldn't talk to strangers, said Jean Luc, finishing it for him. And he smiled.

I have thought about that smile more than about anything else. He knew what he was. He said it out loud, in a joke, in front of both of us, and he was so certain it would cost him nothing that he was right.

He told us he was a director. Consul général de Belgique. He said it in French with Lingala underneath, and his French was worse than he thought it was, and I noticed, and I decided not to have noticed.

That is the part I would like written down somewhere. Not that I was fooled. That I did the deciding.

He wrote a phone number on a piece of paper and put a ten thousand note under it. My eyes went wide and Daudi saw them go wide and has never asked me why. It was not the money. Ten thousand is a school skirt. It was that a man with a car like that had stopped it, in the road, in front of the boda boda men, for me.

Je m'appelle, he asked.

Chantal, I said.

Nobody had asked me for a name before. They had only ever used the one I came with. He handed me that name the way you hand somebody a gift, and I took it the way you take a gift, and it was a collar with a good buckle.

Daudi stood beside me the whole time and did not say one word after mama alisema. I did not look at him. I could feel the hurt coming off him like heat off a wall and I stood in it and warmed my hands, because a girl who has just been chosen wants somebody there to see her be chosen.

I was thirteen. I am not asking to be forgiven for that. I am asking for it to be counted correctly.

---

It rained in April, hard, the way it does, and I ran to Daudi's house because it was closer than mine.

His room is one room. Baked brick, a window the size of two hands, a foam mattress on the floor with a grey sheet, and buckets set out under the places where the roof has given up. He had physics papers spread across the mattress and he moved them for me without being asked.

Do you understand entropy, I said.

I knew what it was. I had heard him say it eleven times. I asked because I wanted him to be the person who knew something, for once, in front of me.

It is a natural state, he said. Order needs energy. Disorder is what happens when you stop.

My father says the lake is the opposite, I said. He says it puts things back in order. He says it remembers everything in its right place.

Your father is a wise man, Daudi said. Then, a minute later: it is hydrogen and oxygen. It has no intelligence.

We sat there and the rain came through the roof and hit the buckets and the sound filled the room so completely that there was nothing else in it. My knee was touching the edge of his knee. I had put it there.

Then what do you want to remember, Mr Scientist, I said. Encrypted data? Binary codes? Or a colourful moment with no meaning in it?

He did not answer and he did not move. I watched a boy who wanted something more than he has wanted anything hold completely still, because moving might have cost him the version of me he kept in his head.

So I kissed him.

It lasted about twenty seconds. I counted, because I count everything.

Then I pulled back and said, see? Not everything falls apart.

He looked at me as though I had shown him the back door of the universe.

That kiss cost me nothing at all. It was the cheapest thing I have ever given anybody and he has carried it around ever since like the down payment on a house, and I let him, and I knew exactly what he thought it was while he was thinking it.

---

I am not going to describe the hotel.

Here is what I have instead, which is the inventory, because that is how I have always understood the world.

A key fob shaped like a lion, cartoon, plastic, the kind you would give a child. It opens a door on the third floor. I keep it in my left pocket where the seam has gone and never in my right.

A perfume. Shalis by Remy Marquis. Not mine. It gets into the weave of a khanga and will not come out with soap, only with time, so I learned to leave certain clothes at the bottom of the basket for four days before my mother does the wash.

Fingernails, done every Friday and taken off every Monday before school, because at school there are rules and at the hotel there are different rules, and I live between the two of them like somebody crossing a road that never stops.

A photograph on my phone. Me, and at the edge of the frame a hand on my shoulder with a gold ring on it. The ring is not real gold. He wears it like a crown. I tagged it blessed and forty-one people liked it and none of them asked me anything.

Two vaccinations, low on the arm, near the elbow, not where they usually put them.

Anybody who wanted to could have read that. It was all on the outside of me for months.

---

We went to Ave de Bobozo about the car.

I say the car. There had been a car, and it came off the road at the corner near the market and did not stop, and my leg has a mark on it going yellow at the edges. That was true. I want you to know that one of the things I said that year was true.

Daudi came because I did not want to go alone. He said it was because his bicycle was stolen once, so he knew the forms.

The station smelled of dust and men. A woman in a black dress was explaining to an officer that it was not prostitution, it was escort services, they had cards with a company number on them. He said, more like your pimp's number. Everybody laughed except her and me.

The officer who took us was young and typing with one finger, hunting each letter down as though it owed him something. He looked up at my face and made a sound in his throat. Mhh. What do we have here.

Daudi said, she is reporting a hit and run.

So why are you talking, the officer said. You sound like you know more than she does. And he winked at Daudi, one man to another, over the top of me.

He asked where and what type of car and who was driving, typing at the speed of a man who has already decided what this is worth. So I held out my phone and showed him the photograph with the hand and the ring, because I had worked out on the way there that a hit and run was worth nothing and a man like that was worth something.

We deal with hard evidence, he said. Not AI. Not phantom ghosts.

I leaned close enough to smell onion and whiskey on him, and backed off, and said it.

My menstrual cycle. There is a latency of more than a month.

The air in that room changed as though a door had opened somewhere.

The older one came over, big gut on him, the kind you get from a lifetime of small envelopes and large drinks, and he started reciting. The penal code. A teenage minor. Rape. Thirty years and twelve lashes on top. He said it the way the priest at St Augustine says the parts he has said ten thousand times. A buccal swab will suffice as conclusive evidence. Shame on the predator, for luring young girls.

The young one kept typing. Then, quietly: where is his number. We will call him. We will summon him now. Yes. We will finish the case, if you understand me.

I understood him perfectly. I have understood men like him since I was eleven, when my auntie's neighbour let his hand sit too long on the back of my chair while a room full of adults agreed together not to see it, the way you decide not to see a crack in a wall you are only renting.

I gave him the phone. I dialled the number myself.

Jean Luc took twenty minutes. Twenty minutes means he was already in town, already near, already somewhere he could reach us from — which means that the whole time I had been walking around thinking I was choosing things, I had been the thing being watched.

He came in with his shoulders back and a fat envelope under his jacket and he did not look surprised and he did not look afraid. He looked like a man whose phone had finally rung.

I saw one thing. When the older officer said the word minor, something went across Jean Luc's face and was gone. A flicker, like a bulb deciding whether to hold. He had planned for envelopes. He had not planned for consequences. Then it closed over and he smiled and it was as though it had never been there.

They took us to the room at the back.

Nothing happened to my body in that room. What happened is that three men worked out what I was worth, out loud, in two languages, in front of me, using the word compensation, and then agreed on it.

I sat on a plastic chair with a crack in it and did the arithmetic along with them, because I cannot help it, I have never been able to help it. And the number they landed on was smaller than the one I had been carrying in my head for eight months.

I came out of that room bent over. Daudi saw me come out and I know what he thought, because he is fifteen and good and he thinks shame is the only thing that can bend a person.

It was not shame. It was arithmetic.

They gave me the envelope on the way out. It had been fat going in. It was thin coming out, and nobody said anything about that, and nobody had to.

---

School on the Monday. I took the nails off on the Sunday night, the same as always.

Here is the thing I have worked out and cannot put down. When this finishes — and it is going to finish, I have known that since the twenty minutes — somebody will be taken for it. Not him. Him they will call as a witness, if they call him at all. They will take somebody visible and poor who cannot afford an envelope, and the whole town will agree to be satisfied, and I think it will be Morisho, because he is the one who shouts on the road every morning where everybody can see him.

He gave me a coin off my fare once and called me shemeji. That is the whole of what he did.

I went to my locker at the end of the day when the corridor was empty. There was a good coat of dust on the inside of the door and I wrote his name in it with one finger.

Daudi.

It is a short name. It does not take long to write and it does not take long to look at.

Then I wiped it off with the side of my hand, because a name in dust is evidence, and everything I have learned this year I have learned about what happens to people when there is evidence lying around with their name on it.

On the way home I stopped at his house. He was out. His father was in and his father does not look at me, which suited me.

I went into the room with the buckets and took the motherboard down off its nail.

There is a board under the third bucket that comes up if you know it comes up. I have known since I was nine. I put the fob under it — the lion, cartoon, plastic, the kind you would give a child — and I put the board back, and I stood on it, and it did not creak.

Then I hung the motherboard on its nail, straight, the way he likes it, between the skull and the hubcap.

He will find it. Not soon. But he is a boy who takes things apart, and one day he will be in that room with nothing to do.

I walked home along the shore counting the ships. There are four of them and there have always been four and they have not moved in my lifetime and they will not move in his. It came out even, the way it does.

> [!WARNING] Two claims in this note were wrong before measurement
> This table was first written asserting ~3,720 words and ~50% short sentences. The real figures are **3,399** and **60%**. Both are recorded above as measured. The discipline is [[The Cold Read Protocol|the vault's own]]: an assessment containing no verified number is noise.
>
> **The sentence-rhythm fix substantially failed.** Draft 1 ran 63% of sentences under ten words; Draft 2 runs 60%. That is a three-point move where the intent was ten, and it means Barrett's bumpety-bump objection is still live. The cuts removed whole *paragraphs* of commentary rather than rebalancing rhythm inside surviving sentences — which is why the word count fell 700 while the ratio barely moved. **This is the outstanding job on the next pass**, and it is a read-aloud job, not a counting one.

**What Barrett bought this draft:**

- **The Morisho foresight** is the single best addition and it came directly from [[Climax and Conclusion — The Story in Six Words|his rule about settling all destinies]]. It costs 40 words, pays the debt both earlier drafts left, and makes her the only person in the story who sees the machine clearly — which is the characterisation the whole piece is built on.
- **The ending stops at the act.** The lake paragraphs are gone. The last image is a girl counting four ships that have not moved and will not move, which carries the theme without stating it — [[The Art of Omission — Unity, Padding, and Proportion|his rule that the soul of the story sits under the surface]], not on it.
- **Roughly 380 words came out**, almost all of it commentary. Per [[The Balance Test — Length as a Function of Idea]], a shorter story that has arrived beats a longer one still travelling — and it opens ~1,280 words of headroom under the prize ceiling.

**Where it still fails Barrett, knowingly:**

- **Speaking parts: six.** His ceiling is two. Not fixable without gutting the world.
- **Time and place unities: broken.** A year, six locations. Held together by voice and single action, which is the modern solution he did not have.
- **First person.** He advises against it — but his central objection, that the narrator has come safely through, is void here. She has not. See [[Methods of Narration — The Author Must Vanish]].

**Open questions:**

1. Is the Morisho passage in the right place? It currently sits after the envelope and before the locker. It could open the story instead, which would make the whole piece an explanation of a prediction.
2. Barrett wants the climax proper in about six words. Candidate here: *"I put the board back, and I stood on it."* Test whether the ships paragraph dilutes it.
3. Five aphorisms may still be one too many. The weakest is likely *"It was a collar with a good buckle."*
4. If this becomes the primary submission rather than Kalemie, the motherboard reveal must be cut from Kalemie — only one story can hold it.
