## OPENING — New Version (Option C)

Port de Kalemie. The air thickly filled with an infusion of colonial coffee and rotten carcasses hidden under the guise of mineral mines, full of corpses aged twelve to seventeen, yet not much younger than me.

In our neighbourhood they called me *the founder of dead things*.

That morning I knew something was wrong before I knew what it was. The way you know when a room has fewer people in it than it should. The dala dala was not there. Morisho's voice — *mia tano mia tano* — was not there. Just the boda bodas idling in the wrong silence, and a small crowd with their backs turned to something by the lakeshore road.

I asked one of them, a boy not much older than me, where Morisho was.

He wouldn't meet my eyes.

*"Wamemchukua."* He said it low, his chin tilting toward the police truck parked crooked at the edge of the road. *"Polisi wamemchukua Morisho asubuhi. Wanasema anahusika na kifo cha msichana."*

The ground did something strange under my feet. The same thing it had done the day I found the antelope skull in the bush — a small, private collapse nobody else could see.

*"Msichana gani?"*

He shrugged. The way people shrug when they already know but don't want to be the first to say the name out loud.

Standing there in the morning light that smelled of lake and dagaa and wrong, I began arranging the things I already knew. The way I arranged the things on my bedroom wall — the motherboard, the hubcap, the antelope skull. Found things. Things that told you something had happened before you arrived.

Her fingernails, meticulously polished every Friday and removed every Monday before school.

The small faded vaccination scar near her elbow, not the upper arm — not where they usually put them. Vaccinations like that are usually done six months before giving birth.

The smell — somewhere at the back of my mind, the name of it refusing to come — of a cologne mostly used by Congolese men.

The fob shaped like a cartoon lion in her left pocket. The door access card attached to it. The kind that opens expensive apartment buildings. The kind found at the lakeside hotel.

The last image on her phone: a blurry selfie tagged *#blessed*, and at the edge of the frame, a man's hand on her shoulder, a gold ring on his finger.

And the last thing. The thing I kept coming back to.

She had written my name — *Daudi* — in the dust on the inside of her school locker the week before she left. And erased it before walking out for the final time.

D'jamela's father had once told me: *some women resemble the lake.*

I was beginning to understand what he meant.

> 2. The vaccination detail (six months before giving birth) is a big reveal dropped quietly. Is it too subtle here, or does it land correctly as something Daudi notices but the reader has to sit with?
> 3. Should the cologne be named here — *Shalis by Remy Marquis* — or left #smell
> 4. nameless to preserve the slow reveal to Jean Luc?
> 5. The opening line ("Port de Kalemie... corpses aged twelve to seventeen, yet not much younger than me") — does it earn its weight or does it front-load too much?
> 6. Is withholding Djamela's name from the boda boda boy effective dread, or does it feel like a writer's trick?
> 7. What single line in this scene is the weakest and why?

## SCENE 1 — D'juma's Words / The Lake

D'jamela's father D'juma's words have rung in my mind like a school bell. Although incomprehensible to me at first, yet I'm beginning to understand them as the days go by.

"Some women resemble the lake." He said.

"Daudi the lake is cold just like some women. Many people come and go, yet it remains the same. Dark, deep and keeps swallowing things down." He said.

"And knows more secrets than anybody." He said.

I realised how the lake pulls things down whilst engaged in the futile pastime of skimming pebbles on the lake surface. A temporal experiment of gravity. Pleasurable until the 5th or 6th throws, right where boredom starts to set in and it becomes clear that gravity does exist. But I had yet to experience how a woman would pull you in like the lake.

The thoughts of holding things together was the subject of our physics class on entropy. Yet it made me wonder, whether things were held together or pulled like those stones at the bottom of the lake.

> [!AI] Questions for this scene
> 1. D'juma's three-part speech about the lake feels like it wants to be a refrain — should it appear more than once in the story, perhaps at the end?
> 2. The skimming pebbles paragraph moves from D'juma to Daudi's own reflection — is the transition clean or does it need a beat?
> 3. "I had yet to experience how a woman would pull you in like the lake" — this is retrospective knowledge. Does it belong here or does it belong at the end, after everything has happened?
> 4. Entropy appears for the first time here through physics class. How do we make sure entropy becomes a genuine structural motif rather than a clever idea that gets forgotten?
> 5. Should D'juma know at this point that Djamela is already in trouble? What does it change if he does?

## SCENE 2 — The Neighbourhood / Djamela's Introduction

The down part of town is where we resided. Thronged fishermen and boda boda drivers looking for an easy catch. A lively population of a mixture of the people of the lake who seem to wander throughout the countries adjacent to the lake. The lake seemed to accord immunity from any passports or visa restriction, you seldom knew which person came from which country. Things like someone's pointed nose, or how short or tall, or if you believed in Jesus didn't matter to someone who came from Kalemie.

Djamela took her mother's figure, she was tall and beautiful just like her mother.

D'jamela is probably the closest person that I have been next to, besides my mother. Who had cared and caressed me during infancy and childhood.

> [!AI] Questions for this scene
> 1. This is the first physical description of Djamela — "tall and beautiful just like her mother." It's thin. What is Daudi actually seeing when he looks at her? What does he notice that he doesn't allow himself to say?
> 2. The neighbourhood paragraph reads like scene-setting that hasn't quite found its voice yet. Can it be cut or compressed into the dala dala scene that follows?
> 3. "The lake seemed to accord immunity from any passports or visa restriction" — this is a strong geopolitical observation embedded casually. Should it be given more room, or is the casualness the point?
> 4. "The closest person I have been next to, besides my mother" — is this the right place for this admission, or should it land later, after we have seen them together?

## SCENE 3 — The Dala Dala / Morisho / The Ghost Kids

The last time me and Djamela were together was on a dala dala, a 16-seater Japanese Toyota Mini Hiace. A cheap and affordable favoured transport for the locals. The conductor, a school dropout, called Morisho.

"Mia tano mia tano." He continued using his skill of managing 30 passengers already in the vehicle, and yet managed to magically lure more in.

There would be occasional brushes with teenage school girls, which often led to their skirts stained with a frothy fluid of sorts on the back of their skirts.

"Mototo muzuri, soma shule usije ukawa konda kama mimi," said Morisho.

"Chukua hii elfu mbili ukatumie shule," he said as he took out a banknote from his stack of notes he had neatly arranged in the side of his draws.

Anitha took the note, and hid it inside her bra, as though it was a treasure to be protected. She said nothing but coyly looked at him as a thirteen-year-old school girl would who thought she was being given something precious. Anitha dropped off at the Ecole Secondaire de Kitumaini stop as she usually did before seven thirty, but today she was early as it was her class that was cleaning the school yard according to the timetable.

As Anitha got off the dala dala, her best friend Djamela followed her with eyes of envy.

"She's eating chips kuku today," said Djamela.

"The chicken and chips will be digging her own grave," I whispered to D'jamela. And added "HIV," motioning my eyes to Morisho.

A Toyota Prado SUV parked beside us on the traffic lights stop on the double road along Ave de Industrielle.

"Look. Look at them," said D'jamela excitedly in haste, before the lights turned green.

"The way they wear those checkered skirts with their ties and blazers," she said, pointing at the school children quietly sitting at the back of the vehicle.

"They go to Monseigneur André Kaseba Catholic School," I told her, pointing to their checkered uniform.

"My mother said it's the most expensive school in town," I said like I knew everything about the school.

I had always thought about how we hardly met up with them on the dala dalas, and how maybe through entropy or gravity, we might be pulled together with them.

"Once they entered their parent's SUVs they went to their school, and then entered their red clay roofed mansions guarded by gates and security guards," said Morisho.

"Those children are ghosts to you, you will never see them nor talk to them," he said in a clear voice, making sure it sank deep in our hearts.

The ghost kids weren't the subject of envy, especially on my part. But I envied the way they were secured and safe, living in luxury. Probably eating European things like baguettes, pizzas and burgers, everything European. Not the ugali dagaa from our school.

> [!AI] Questions for this scene
> 1. Morisho gives Anitha two thousand shillings. Daudi warns Djamela about HIV. Now Morisho is arrested for Djamela's death. How do we calibrate this scene so Morisho reads as suspicious but not obviously guilty — keeping him as a genuine red herring?
> 2. "The ghost kids" motif is strong. Djamela wanting to be one of them drives the whole story. Is there one more detail we can add here — something Djamela says or does — that makes her desire more specific and more heartbreaking?
> 3. Daudi uses "HIV" as a warning. Does this land correctly — teenage boy, shorthand, protective — or does it need unpacking?
> 4. Morisho's "those children are ghosts to you" is one of the story's thesis lines. Should it be set apart somehow — a paragraph break, a beat of silence from Daudi — or does the current flow work?
> 5. "Through entropy or gravity, we might be pulled together with them" — Daudi applying physics to class. Is this too neat or does it feel like how this specific boy thinks?

## SCENE 4 — Djamela's Birthday / The Watermelon

Anitha was first to turn thirteen, barely three months before D'jamela's birthday, which we had celebrated a few weeks ago.

My first lesson on how women can pull things happened when D'jamela turned thirteen. As she made her wish and blew the candle.

The watermelon supposedly symbolizing the birthday cake.

"Baba surely you could have gotten me a cake, even a cheap one," said D'jamela.

D'jamela's father worked at the fruit market in Ave de Tanganyique for most of the mornings, before handing the stall to her mother in the evening whilst he attended his traditional African healing customers.

"Watermelons are more, healthier and natural," said her father D'juma.

"Besides it's more African. And we don't have to celebrate birthdays with cakes, that's a western thing," he said.

"Look at it this way — watermelons are sweet like you D'jamela, they symbolize the natural element of sweetness and love," said her mother, trying to convince D'jamela of the truth of her father's words.

"Make a wish and blow," said her father D'juma, looking as if he was making his own wishes.

"To be Kim Kardashian, amen," said D'jamela.

"What is Kim Kardashian?" asked D'juma.

"A social media influencer," said D'jamela's mother quickly, attempting to disregard the name.

With her eyes closed as if culminating a ritual, she blew the candle. The smoke partially rose up gathering into a dark cloud just above the shiny corrugated iron roof. It coagulated, like Leopold never wanting to leave Brazzaville. She handed me a piece of the red watermelon, and in those moments of devouring its sweet juice, trying to bite off every inch of it, down to its bare green skin. It tasted like it was preserving itself in sugar.

To me it felt as though it was Djamela's father's desperate attempt to stave off the inevitable rot of the whole situation.

Later, on the porch, we looked out at the Lake. With its rusted colonial ships.

"He thinks I want things," she said, in a low and steady voice.

"I want to control things; I want to conquer like the Europeans."

When we were children, six or seven years old, she told me that the lake was a mirror:

"If you stare long enough, you can see your own death," she told me, looking steadily with her eyes directed to the farthest point of the lake.

"Don't you see Daudi, we can beat them at their own game. No nuclear weapons or school degrees, just followers," she continued.

> [!AI] Questions for this scene
> 1. "Like Leopold never wanting to leave Brazzaville" — this is the story's most explicit colonial reference. Does it land or does it feel like it's reaching? Should it be unpacked slightly or left as is for the reader who gets it?
> 2. D'juma refuses a cake because "that's a western thing" — and then Djamela wishes to be Kim Kardashian. This irony is the whole story in miniature. Is it too neat? Or is neatness exactly right for a birthday scene?
> 3. "To be Kim Kardashian, amen" — the "amen" is doing a lot of work (birthday wish as prayer, aspiration as religion). Should Daudi notice this? Comment on it? Or leave it unremarked?
> 4. "If you stare long enough, you can see your own death" — Djamela says this at age six or seven, and she's right. How do we handle this line without making Djamela feel like a prophecy rather than a person?
> 5. "I want to conquer like the Europeans / just followers" — Djamela's ambition is stated directly. Is this too explicit for her character, or does the directness fit a thirteen-year-old girl who hasn't learned yet to disguise what she wants?
> 6. D'juma as traditional healer, fruit seller, and philosopher — is he fully realized enough? What one additional detail would make him three-dimensional?

## SCENE 5 — The Dala Dala Continued / The Dagaa Stop / Jean Luc Appears

An empty stomach, the strong smell of the freshly fried dagaa from mama ntilie vendors brought me back to the moment. Realising we had reached our stop.

"They call them anchovy," said D'jamela. She always made things seem more chic using trendy European words.

I nodded in return.

"You mean the dagaa," I said, pointing out the obvious.

"Shusha konda," I said, banging the side of the minivan to signal the driver to stop at the next stop.

"Sawa shemeji," said Morisho, giving Djamela a five-hundred-shilling coin.

"Ya kwako hiyo shemeji yangu," said Morisho.

As we were about to disembark, the boda bodas started greeting us with their horns. It was as though they were awaiting our arrival.

— Mambo mrembo

— Mototo wa mujini.

— Mototo muzuri.

They all said whilst gesturing at Djamela's beauty.

Usually my voice would be squirmish trying to respond to their unwanted comments, but then something stopped me.

A man gestured to me from the other side of the tinted glass of a dark black SUV, like the ones the ghost kids from Monseigneur André Kaseba Catholic School used. We pretended not to notice him and kept on walking.

His horn blazed in our direction, a different tune to the boda bodas and dala dalas. The air suddenly changed from roasted dagaa from the fresh lake catch to perfumes from Dubai as he lowered the windows with a swish — not the usual clink.

The car radio was blazing soft rhumba of Fally Ipupa. The man lowered the window with a quiet swish. "Wow, just look at that BMW X5," said Djamela, as we made a U-turn from the school direction and started looking at the beast. In between the beats of Fally Ipupa a sound emitted from the interior:

"Je m'appelle Jean Luc," said the man.

"Tu es d'où, ma belle?" said the man in his mixed French and Lingala.

"Rue Saint Michel," answered Djamela, with a different flavour to her voice.

The ghost children came from Rue Saint Michel. They wore the checkered uniforms. Not like us — we came from Mihogoni or Manzese. Not Rue Saint Michel.

"Mama alisema," I started to say, visualizing a script taught to us at our Sunday school at St. Augustine Parish along Ave Kabanyo Mute.

"We shouldn't talk to strangers," said Jean Luc, as though he had also learned the same from Sunday school.

As he made the dépassement with his black beast on the curb and drew us into his world of leather upholstery and smells of colognes from Dubai:

"Le contraire, mon garçon."

"Directeur. Le consul général de Belgique," he said.

Listening to his broken lines of French and street Congolese Lingala.

"Ask the boda bodas — they know the town like a Google map," I pointed out to him.

"Le petit garçon," he called me. His smell of cologne and leather upholstery didn't deter me from continuing my policy of not talking to strangers.

Remembering the words of my Sunday school teacher at St. Augustine Parish:

*"Many men bring ruin to young school girls and abandonment of dreams."*

D'jamela should have listened to me, but she continued the dialogue with the man who called himself Jean Luc.

He took out a piece of paper and wrote down his phone number and covered a ten thousand shillings note with it.

Djamela's eyes widened. It wasn't the ten thousand shillings note that astonished her. It could have been the SUV. The fact that she had wanted to be somebody, like those kids that rode in SUVs. Like Kim Kardashian.

"Je m'appelle?" he asked Djamela.

"Chantal," she said, as her eyes widened with her body emitting a new frequency, a new signature vibe.

> [!AI] Questions for this scene
> 1. Djamela gives herself Jean Luc's name — "Chantal." This is the moment she steps into the ghost-kid world, renames herself to fit it. Is this moment given enough weight, or does it go by too fast?
> 2. Morisho gives Djamela a 500-shilling coin as she exits ("ya kwako hiyo shemeji yangu") — why? Is this a habit? Affection? A claim? This detail should mean something given he's later arrested. What is the right read?
> 3. Jean Luc says "we shouldn't talk to strangers" before Daudi can finish the Sunday school line. This is the story's most sinister joke. Does it land clearly enough as threat-disguised-as-charm?
> 4. "Le consul général de Belgique" — Jean Luc claims a diplomatic title. Is this credible to Djamela? To us? What does it tell us about what kind of predator he is?
> 5. The cologne from Dubai — this is the cologne Daudi can't name in the opening. Should there be a micro-recognition here, Daudi smelling it for the first time, so the echo in the opening lands harder?
> 6. The boda boda men calling out to Djamela — harassment coded as compliment. Daudi is about to say something and then stops. What stopped him? The SUV? Something else? This needs one more beat.

## SCENE 6 — The Rain / The Mattress / The Twenty Seconds

The look in D'jamela's eyes reminded me of the incident when we lived near Marché le Lubuye. The time we shared a moment in the rain. A cordial event compared to what was happening now.

That sweet aroma of damp clothing infused with perfumed khangas filled the air of my lowly dwellings. It felt as if all life existed in the confines of those four walls of baked bricks. The half-soaked dark brown foam mattress laid out on the floor. The two-foot sealed window provided little if no lighting. Yet D'jamela shone with an aura that was unforgettable, as she stood at my bedroom door, running from the rain.

The droplets seeping through the tin roof bounced off her shoulders. The rest collected itself in the bucket I had meticulously positioned.

"Come in D'jamela," I said to her, cautioning her not to kick the buckets. I motioned her to the only sensible part of the room, a dry patch of the brown mattress.

"Do you understand entropy?" she posed the question, looking at the papers spread out on the mattress along with the half-torn physics book spread out on the grey sheets that covered the mattress. The sheets devoid of the fresh smell of detergent and smeared with patches of evidence of dreams and nightmares.

"It's a natural state," I said, trying to sound like my everyday vocabulary.

"Order requires energy; disorder is its default," I told her.

"It's like holding on to something, and once unbearable you have to let go," said Djamela.

"My father says the Lake is the opposite. He says it puts things back to order. It remembers everything in its right place," she continued.

"Your father sounds like a wise man, we could learn a lot from him," I said to her, looking for signs of a reaction. I saw none.

"It's a fusion of Hydrogen and Oxygen. It doesn't have any intelligence," I continued, using my new-found vocabulary.

Moments of pauses passed by. Then it came without warning, a surprise that scattered the notes on entropy and the physics book placed on the mattress.

She knelt down on the mattress, sat next to me with her feet touching a wet patch of floor now laminated with droplets of rain that bounced off the edge of the bucket.

Her eyes flowed, yet restful and inattentive to everything but the space between us.

"Then what do you want to remember, Mr. Scientist? Encrypted data? Binary codes? Or just a colourful moment without meaning?" she said.

Sitting there in those few moments seemed like a dream that should never have ended. The edge of my knee had accidentally touched her left hand thumb that supported the weight of her body. My mind told me that she felt it and had wanted me to do something. A soft touch, a tender word.

Not a word came out of my mouth but silence.

*Tell her to go home, it was late. This isn't right, you shouldn't be here. Do you want me to touch you?*

With all the premeditation and calculations. Nothing happened except motionless head chatter.

Then that unpredicted glow from her eyes happened, the bioluminescent stream of pure everything.

The air, along with the perfumed wet kangas, now had an added aura of a minty after-smell of PK bubble gum and lip gloss, with the darkness of the room staring at us. Lost in the stream of a subliminal moment, things dictating themselves as if they had life in them.

Tangent and sweet it felt at first. Then perfect and seamless, as if awaiting a timestamp. Right there in the midst of inconsistent heartbeats.

It took only twenty seconds.

I gasped for air.

Things went back to normal, with a heart-breaking realisation of the *désvu* that took place.

She pulled away. Her breath, short and sharp. The glow faded from her eyes, replaced by a sudden, vulnerable uncertainty.

She looked at me as if she had revealed the secret backdoor to the universe.

"See?" she whispered, her voice barely audible.

"Not everything falls apart."

She was wrong, of course. Entropy always wins. But in that moment — in the dusty rainy moment of obliviousness — it felt as though she was right.

It was the most beautiful and catastrophic twenty seconds of my life.

> [!AI] Questions for this scene
> 1. This is the story's emotional core. But it's buried deep. Should it be moved earlier — closer to the opening — so the reader knows what Daudi has lost before they see him losing it?
> 2. "The bioluminescent stream of pure everything" — this is the story reaching for something beyond its usual register. Does it earn it, or is it overwritten?
> 3. Daudi's internal monologue — *tell her to go home, do you want me to touch you* — is in italics, dissociated. Good. But does it need one more line that shows his failure is not just passivity but fear? What is he actually afraid of here?
> 4. "Not everything falls apart" / "Entropy always wins" — this is the story's thematic thesis in two lines. Does it work as the payoff of the entropy motif, or does it feel too stated?
> 5. The twenty seconds appears twice in the draft — here and in the Jean Luc scene (Djamela's eyes widening the same way). Is this echo intentional and strong enough? How can it be sharpened?
> 6. After the kiss Djamela says "see?" — as if she has proved something. What has she proved? To herself? To Daudi? Should Daudi wonder about this, or leave it as a mystery?

## SCENE 7 — The Police Station / Jean Luc / The Envelope

Then Jean Luc entered our lives. Having once reported my stolen bike at the police station, the tedious ritual of filing a complaint was still alive in my mind. It was only because Djamela didn't want to go alone, especially to Ave de Bobozo police station, fearing that she might not get out quickly.

It was Djamela that was next to me, but in reality Chantal had already taken over her — with a new pair of unnaturally blue jeans and a blouse with a designer logo that looked like a blatant counterfeit. In truth she was trying to be one of them. She even had an air of superiority she had disguised herself in. Her back arched upwards and her chin high. Chantal looked more ruthless compared to D'jamela. Although a sense of disappointment had set in her by the looks of her eyes and tired face. She had lost most of her pre-conceived hopes of a rags-to-riches life that Jean Luc was to give her. She had wanted so much to be like the ghost kids, those of Rue Saint Michel. *À notre belle patrie.*

Throngs of people filled the police station with sighs of delayed justice. The smell of dusty benches worn out by the sliding buttocks of motionless police due process. The biodegradable odour mixed with the tiredness of the officers coming off the shift almost crippled the atmosphere.

Other appellants trying to state their cases.

"He didn't — it was stolen. He bought it off the internet legitimately," a mother pleading on behalf of her sixteen-year-old boy who was in possession of a stolen phone.

Another woman, wearing a tight black dress, whose mascara was being washed by fake tears, stressing a point to another officer in the background.

"It's not prostitution, it's escort services. We even have cards with the company number," she pleaded with wet alluring eyes to the officer.

"More like your pimp's number," answered the officer she was pleading to.

Then came our turn. The policeman, a young man that looked as though he hadn't had much experience with a QWERTY keyboard, was one-handedly poking the typewriter, whilst listening to the row of people lined up. He attended them at the same rate as his typing speed.

He looked up and saw Djamela's face, but couldn't help himself from making a grunting sound. "Mhh — what do we have here," he said.

Djamela looked at me and motioned me to go forward.

"She's reporting a hit and run," I said, gesturing the officer toward her.

"So why are you talking if she's the one who's reporting?" he said.

"You sound like you know what happened more than she does," he said, gesturing and winking at me.

"No, it's not like that," I looked at him seriously, as though D'jamela was my sister.

"Where was the location, what type of car, and who did the running?" he said, resuming his one-handed typing as if we were wasting his time.

"Look at my socials. My status," said Djamela, holding out her phone as though it were some precious tablet bearing vital inscriptions.

The officer looked at the picture of Jean Luc holding D'jamela in his arms behind a G-Wagon Mercedes Benz.

"We deal with hard evidence. Not AI or phantom ghosts," he said to D'jamela.

Having exhausted all the pleading to the officer and witnessing his blatant indifference, she leaned towards him, but backed away after flinching at the smell of his onion-whiskey breath.

"My menstrual cycle. There's a latency of more than a month," she said.

The change in the policeman's face was immediate, as if something had changed the air.

From the foreground came an older policeman, who had a protruding gut testament to a lifetime of consuming bribes and whiskey.

"The penal code. A teenage minor. Rape. Thirty years. And twelve lashes on top of that," said the older officer as though he were reading from a pre-written script.

The thought of someone like Jean Luc being jailed was a blessing in disguise. It made me think of how D'jamela would be free from his evil clutches.

"A mere buccal swab will suffice as conclusive evidence," the older officer said.

"Shame to the predator, for alluring young girls," he continued, as though preaching at St. Augustine's parish.

Whilst the younger officer was still poking away at the typewriter, he looked up at D'jamela and quietly whispered, as if he wanted to finish off Jean Luc:

"Where is his number. We will call him now. Summon him to come."

"Yes, we will finish the case — if you understand me," he said, assuring D'jamela that justice had already taken place.

D'jamela took out her phone and handed it to the officer, whilst dialling Jean Luc's number.

A cold acidic taste entered my mouth. My blood pressure had already risen. The broken fan above didn't make it easier as things began to heat up.

It only took twenty minutes for Jean Luc to arrive at the station.

He came in with all airs, as if he had prepared for the situation. Underneath his Congolese suit, he had a fat envelope that seemed to be the object of attention of the police officer as he took it out.

The officers became more relaxed and took Jean Luc and Djamela to a room at the back of the station.

It didn't take long for them to come out. The officers looked happily at each other as though they had won the lottery. Djamela's head and back were bent down in a stoop, as if something demeaning had happened to her.

Djamela held the brown envelope that Jean Luc had brought with him — although its contents had depleted along the way between going to and from the backroom. Making it look thinner.

> [!AI] Questions for this scene
> 1. "Chantal had already taken over her" — this is one of the saddest lines in the draft. Djamela has already lost herself before she's lost. Can this observation be expanded slightly — one more physical detail of what Daudi sees that isn't Djamela anymore?
> 2. The police station waiting room — stolen phone, escort services, one-fingered typing — functions as a portrait of institutional rot. Compare this to the opening of *Immaculate* by Marlon James. Is it achieving the same satirical weight, or is it moving too fast?
