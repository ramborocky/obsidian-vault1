# KALEMIE
### *Claude's version — draft 1, 2026-07-23*

Port de Kalemie smelled of colonial coffee and something underneath it that the coffee was there to cover. The mines did that. Twelve years old, some of the boys who went into the hillside and didn't come back out. Not much younger than me.

They called me the founder of dead things, in our part of town. I never minded it. A found thing tells you something happened before you got there — a hubcap off a BMW X5 dumped by the lakeside hotel, an antelope skull kicked loose in the bush by something running from hunters, a motherboard from a satellite phone nobody would ever ask about. I nailed them to my wall in the order I found them. It was the only order I trusted.

That morning the dala dala wasn't at the stop. Morisho's voice wasn't there — *mia tano mia tano*, the sound the whole street set its watch by. Just boda bodas idling wrong, engines running but nobody calling out fares, and a crowd with its back to the lakeshore road, facing something none of them wanted to be first to name.

A boy about my age stood at the edge of it. I asked him where Morisho was.

He wouldn't look at me. *"Wamemchukua,"* he said, chin tilting toward a police truck parked crooked at the edge of the crowd. *"Polisi wamemchukua Morisho asubuhi. Wanasema anahusika na kifo cha msichana."*

The ground did the thing it had done the day I found the antelope skull. A small collapse nobody else could see.

*"Msichana gani?"*

He only shrugged — the shrug of someone who already has the name in his mouth and won't be the one to say it first.

So I built her the way I built everything on my wall. Piece by piece, in the order the pieces arrived.

Her fingernails, polished every Friday, bare again by Monday, like something she put on and took off depending on who was watching.

A vaccination scar near her elbow, not the shoulder where they usually go. The kind they give six months before a birth.

A cologne I couldn't name, worn by Congolese men who wanted people to think they were something else.

A fob shaped like a cartoon lion, and on it a door card for buildings with real locks — the kind you find by the lakeside hotel, not in Mihogoni.

A blurry selfie, *#blessed*, a man's hand at the edge of the frame with a gold ring on it.

And the last piece, the one I keep going back to. My name, *Daudi*, written in the dust inside her locker the week before she left — and rubbed out again before she walked out for good.

Her father told me once: some women are like the lake. I was starting to understand exactly what that cost.

---

D'juma's voice has a way of getting into my skull and staying there, ringing like the bell at St. Augustine's long after the service has ended.

*"Some women are like the lake, Daudi."* He was mending a fishing net when he said it, not looking up. *"They take everything in and give nothing back. The lake doesn't get lighter for what it swallows."*

I threw stones at the water while he talked, because that's what you do with your hands when a grown man is trying to tell you something you don't want to hear yet. Five skips, six, and then the stone goes down and doesn't come back — gravity doing what gravity does, patient about it, no hurry.

We'd just started entropy in physics class. Order costs energy. Disorder is free. Nobody has to work to fall apart. I didn't tell D'juma that. I filed it next to what he'd said, the way I filed everything, and waited to see which one would turn out to be true.

---

We lived in the part of town where the fishermen dried their nets in the road and the boda boda men called out to anyone who looked like they might be lost, which in Mihogoni was nobody, because nobody who lived there could afford to look lost. Passports didn't matter down by the lake. You could be from three countries and one shoreline and nobody would ask which.

Djamela had her mother's walk before she had her mother's height — a way of crossing a room like she already knew everyone in it was going to look. I'd known her since before either of us had a reason to notice that about each other. She was the only person besides my mother who'd ever put a hand on the back of my neck and left it there.

---

The last time we rode the dala dala together, Morisho was working the door like a man conducting an orchestra nobody else could hear. *"Mia tano, mia tano"* — thirty people already wedged into a sixteen-seater and somehow, always, room enough for one more.

He liked the schoolgirls best, the shy ones. Handed Anitha a folded note without looking at her properly. *"Chukua hii elfu mbili, ukatumie shule."* Go to school, don't end up thin like me. She tucked the money into her bra like it was something that might be taken back, and said nothing, and got off at Kitumaini early, because it was her week to sweep the yard.

Djamela watched her go with something that wasn't quite envy and wasn't quite its opposite.

*"She's eating chips kuku today."*

*"That chicken's digging her a grave,"* I said, and made a small show of my eyes toward Morisho. *"HIV."* She laughed the way you laugh at thirteen when a word is more dangerous as a joke than a fact.

A Prado idled next to us at the lights, checkered uniforms in the back seat, blazers, ties, the whole costume of somewhere else.

*"Look at them,"* Djamela said, pressing her face near the glass like it might let her closer.

*"Monseigneur André Kaseba,"* I told her, like I hadn't just read it off their collars myself.

Morisho caught the look on both our faces in the mirror. *"Those ones — into the SUV, into the school, into the house with the gate and the dog and the guard. Ghosts, both of you. You'll never touch them, never talk to them."* He said it kindly, which somehow made it worse.

I didn't envy their pizza or their baguettes. I envied the gate.

---

Djamela's birthday came with a watermelon instead of a cake, because her father had decided, the way fathers decide things, that watermelon was more honest. *"Healthier. More African. We don't need a Western cake to prove we love you."*

*"You could have got a cheap one,"* she said. Not sulking. Filing it away, the way I filed things.

*"Watermelon is sweet like you are,"* her mother offered, patting the fruit like a blessing that needed a body to land on.

*"Make a wish,"* D'juma said, and I noticed his own mouth moved first, some small private thing he wanted before he'd let his daughter want anything.

*"To be Kim Kardashian. Amen."*

*"What is Kim Kardashian?"*

*"Nothing, Baba,"* her mother said too quickly, and blew out the candle for her.

The smoke gathered under the tin roof and sat there like it had nowhere better to be. I ate my slice down to the green, and something about how carefully she'd handed it to me — like the fruit itself was an apology — made me think her father knew, on some level under the level where he'd admit it, that a watermelon wasn't going to be enough to hold back whatever was coming.

Later, on the step, she looked at the lake the way she always did — like she was already somewhere past it.

*"He thinks I want things. I don't want things. I want to run things. Like the Europeans did."*

She'd told me once, when we were six, that if you stared at the lake long enough you could see your own death in it. I didn't believe her then. I mostly believed her now.

*"No nuclear weapons,"* she said. *"No degrees. Just people who'll follow you."*

I didn't have an answer for that. I don't think she wanted one.

---

We got off near the dagaa stalls, the smell of them frying pulling me back into my own body the way hunger does.

*"They call it anchovy,"* Djamela said, the way she said most things now, like there was a better word somewhere in Europe and she'd found it first.

*"It's dagaa."*

The boda boda men greeted her the way they always did — *mrembo, mototo wa mujini, mototo muzuri* — words thrown like coins at a beggar who hasn't asked for anything. I usually had something to say back. That day I didn't, because a black SUV had eased up on the far side of the road, windows tinted, engine barely audible under Fally Ipupa coming soft through the speakers, and something in me had already decided not to look at it directly.

The window went down anyway. Cologne first, then the voice.

*"Je m'appelle Jean Luc."*

*"Tu es d'où, ma belle?"*

*"Rue Saint Michel,"* Djamela said, in the voice she used for people she wanted to impress.

We weren't from Rue Saint Michel. We were from Mihogoni, from Manzese, from the part of the map that doesn't get a checkered uniform.

*"Mama alisema —"* I started, the line St. Augustine's had drilled into all of us about strangers.

*"We shouldn't talk to strangers,"* Jean Luc said, before I could finish it, smiling like he'd been to the same Sunday school and outgrown it faster.

He called me *le petit garçon* and I hated that it worked, that the smell of his leather seats and his cologne from somewhere further than Dubai made me feel like a child in a way the boda boda men never had. He wrote his number on a slip of paper and laid a ten-thousand-shilling note over it like a magician closing a trick.

*"Comment tu t'appelles?"* he asked her.

*"Chantal,"* she said. Not her name. A name she'd been keeping somewhere for exactly this moment, and I understood, watching her give it to him, that some part of her had already left Mihogoni and simply hadn't told the rest of her yet.

---

Rain came the night she showed up at my door, and I let her in before I let myself think about what letting her in meant. My room held rain better than most — a bucket in the right place, a mattress in the only dry corner, and Djamela, soaked, glowing anyway in a way that had nothing to do with water.

*"Do you understand entropy?"* she asked, looking at my physics papers scattered over the sheets like she'd rather talk about the universe than whatever had brought her here.

*"Order costs energy,"* I told her. *"Disorder is free."*

*"It's like holding on to something,"* she said. *"And then it's unbearable, and you let go."*

*"My father says the lake's the opposite. It puts everything back where it belongs. Nothing's ever really lost in it."*

*"Your father sounds wise."* I was watching for something in her face when I said it. I didn't find it.

*"It's hydrogen and oxygen,"* I said instead, retreating to what I knew. *"No intelligence in it at all."*

She didn't answer that. She knelt closer, feet in the wet patch by the bucket, and asked me, quiet, *"What do you actually want to remember, Mr. Scientist? Encrypted files? Binary code?"* A pause long enough to hold something. *"Motema."* Heart. Then, soft again: *"Or just a moment that doesn't mean anything at all?"*

I didn't have an answer ready. My knee found her hand by accident and stayed there on purpose. Twenty seconds, start to end, the most complete twenty seconds I have ever lived through and I did not say one true thing inside them.

*"See?"* she whispered after, like she'd proven a theorem. *"Not everything falls apart."*

She was wrong. Entropy always wins in the end; I know that better than most thirteen-year-olds ever will. But in that room, with the rain doing its patient work on the roof, I let her be right, because some proofs you don't correct.

---

Jean Luc came all the way into our lives the week I had to report my bike stolen, and Djamela — no, by then it wasn't only Djamela anymore, Chantal had moved in and hadn't left — didn't want to go to Ave de Bobozo alone.

She'd dressed for it. Jeans too blue to be anything but new, a logo that wanted to be real and wasn't quite. Her chin was up in a way that looked like confidence from far away and looked, up close, like someone holding her own face in place with her hands.

The station smelled like every bad afternoon anyone had ever had inside it. A mother pleading her son's innocence over a stolen phone. A woman in a tight black dress explaining that what she did was escorting, not the other thing, while an officer told her, not unkindly, that he knew whose number that really was.

Our turn came. The young officer looked up, saw her face, made a sound with no words in it. *"What do we have here."*

*"She's reporting a hit and run,"* I said.

*"Then why are you the one talking?"* Wink at me, like we were sharing something.

She held her phone out, a photo of Jean Luc's arm around her, the G-Wagon behind them both. The officer glanced at it the way you glance at a child's drawing.

*"We deal in evidence. Not phantoms."*

She leaned in anyway, and something in his onion-and-whiskey breath made her draw back before she'd finished. Then she said the thing that changed the air in the room entirely.

*"My cycle's late. More than a month."*

An older officer arrived from somewhere behind the desk like the words had summoned him. *"Penal code. A minor. Thirty years, and lashes on top." Testament to a lifetime of consuming bribes and his own certainty, he told her a buccal swab would settle it, told the room he'd finish the case himself, dialed the number she gave him like a man already spending the money he hadn't been offered yet.

It took Jean Luc twenty minutes to arrive. Twenty minutes meant he'd been close. Close meant he'd known. I filed that too, the way I file everything, though by then I understood some things you file are things you should have shouted about instead.

He came in wearing a suit too good for a police station and an envelope too fat to be anything but what it was. The officers took him and Chantal to the back room the way you'd take honored guests somewhere private.

When they came out, the men were smiling at each other like they'd won something. Chantal's back was bent in a way I'd never seen on her before, bent the way you bend when something's been taken and you're the one who has to carry the shape of the theft home.

The envelope in her hand was the same envelope. It just wasn't fat anymore.

---

I never found the ending to this, not in the version I have access to, and I'd rather say so than invent a resolve I haven't earned. What I know is this: the lake still swallows things and gives nothing back, the mines still take their twelve-year-olds, and somewhere the fat envelope became thin between two rooms in a police station that will never write down what actually happened in between.

D'juma was right about women who resemble the lake. He never said what happens to the boy left standing on the shore, watching, doing nothing, calling it collecting.
