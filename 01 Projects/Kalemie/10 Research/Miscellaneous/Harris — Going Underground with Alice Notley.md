# GOING UNDERGROUND WITH ALICE NOTLEY

**Will Harris** · on Alice Notley's *The Descent of Alette* · stored for study, copyright the author's

> [!important] The most directly useful piece of research in this vault for Chantal's biggest formal risk
> See [[Miscellaneous]] for the application. Short version: **unquoted dialogue has a tradition, and the tradition is a good one.**

---

## Full text

The first thing you notice when you open Alice Notley's epic poem *The Descent of Alette* is the quotation marks: there are lots of them and they're in all the wrong places. This is how it starts:

> 'One day, I awoke' '& found myself on' 'a subway, endlessly'
> 'I didn't know' 'how I'd arrived there or' 'who I was' 'exactly'
> 'But I knew the train' 'knew riding it' 'knew the look of'
> 'those about me'

It's clear that quotation marks are not being used here in ordinary ways – to indicate either direct speech or quotation from another text. Perhaps they're performing another of their main roles: to point out a received idea, suggesting the author's knowledge (and disavowal) of a choice of words – this is a 'classic' usage. Reading *Alette*, you sense Notley weighing up each phrase in this way, inspecting every word through the wry spectacles of quotation.

'One day, I awoke' has a received – which is to say, generic – feel. It immediately puts the poem in a lineage. We are about to embark on a quest, there will be dream visions, a tyrant will be confronted, and the shades of loved ones will be met. But those quotation marks also have an ulterior affect, introducing a stutter to the speaking voice. In a more conventional narrative like Kafka's *Metamorphosis*, which has a similar opening – 'One morning, when Gregor Samsa woke . . .' – we are delivered smoothly and directly into the story. Here, we are delivered into the tentative hands of quotation.

Look at the apparently ironic clipping around 'exactly' at the end of the second line. It is impossible not to make internal 'bunny ears' while reading this, if just because the question of 'who I was' (who anyone is) can never be resolved 'exactly'. This is language interrogating itself – which might be a feature of poetry generally, where the self-aware line replaces the hurried sentence. In Notley's work especially, punctuation gives shape to that self-awareness.

But this is getting away from the overwhelming fact about Notley's quotation marks: that they are purposefully, consistently 'wrong'. After *Alette*'s initial publication in 1992, so many people expressed confusion about them she added a note to subsequent reprintings. Their purpose, she writes in the note, is to 'make the reader slow down and silently articulate – not slur over mentally – the phrases at the pace, and with the stresses, I intend.' Which sounds simple but evades what makes them so compelling.

The power of those wrongly used punctuation marks is to expose something deeper (righter?) about punctuation itself, the extent to which it is expressive, changeable, gesturing outside itself and beyond the page. And there is a long tradition of poets who use punctuation just like this – wrongly, excessively, against 'normal' usage. Think of Emily Dickinson's slashes, Elizabeth Bishop's parentheses, John Ashbery's commas, Frank Bidart's caps lock, and Bhanu Kapil's colons.

In *My Emily Dickinson*, Susan Howe writes that Dickinson abolished conventional punctuation in order to 'subtract arbitrary authority. Dashes drew liberty of interruption inside the structure of each poem.' The same is true of Notley's quotation marks. *Alette* is a poem literally framed against tyranny, embodied by the male despot the protagonist eventually overthrows. '"He owns form," "doesn't he?" "The tyrant" "owns form",' says Alette. If Notley cannot 'own' form, she can at least use punctuation to distort it; this is her way of drawing 'liberty of interruption' into the poem.

When I first read *Alette* I was journeying back and forth on the underground. I worked in care homes in east London and my commute involved two trains, sometimes three. I was reading a copy of *Alette* from the National Poetry Library, tea-stained and dog-eared, and I never got beyond Book One (there are four books) because I was always too exhausted. But I loved reading the stanzas of that first book – set on an endless, infernal subway journey – and transposing them onto my commute:

> 'we rode the trains' 'Got on, got off' 'Sat &
> watched, sat' '& slept' 'Walked from car to car' 'Stood in stations'
> 'We were caught up' 'in movement' 'in ongoingness' '& in ongoingness'
> 'of voices,' 'for example' 'Which of us spoke? did' 'it matter?'

There was one time the guy opposite me commented on the phone case of the woman next to him and suddenly the two of them were flirting. She said her name. 'Come again?' The rails screeched. 'Beatrix!' she shouted. The next woman over smiled. Three people further down the carriage were sitting with their eyes closed; another was applying mascara. A woman in a grey cardigan stifled a yawn and it looked like she was about to burst into tears.

Alette says: '"Gradually" "I perceived that" "each person" "was surrounded by / a ghostly" "second image" "was encased in it" "& each" "of those images," "those encasings," "was exactly the same".' A man stood with his backpack between his knees, eyes closed, holding onto the handrail overhead, swaying with the train. Lots of people had their eyes closed, especially in the mornings. And if I closed mine too, it felt like I could float up into my 'second image', become part of the same 'ongoingness' that encased everyone. '"Which of us spoke? did" "it matter?".'

Notley said *Alette* came to her on the New York City subway in the late-1980s. Her brother Albert – a Vietnam war veteran with severe PTSD – had just died of an accidental overdose. 'It was the big homelessness era, and there were all these Vietnam vets living on the streets and sleeping in the subway cars,' Notley said in an interview with the *Paris Review* the year before she died. 'I would go from car to car and imagine these fantastic scenes. I conceived of the subway as being this place that no one could leave.'

In the schema of *Alette*, the subway is hell – a place no one can escape. But Alette does get out, and in Book Two, having left the subway, she looks down at her arms and legs and feels sad: '"a clear single edge" "delineated them".' She remembers her subway companions, the sense of their arms and legs being 'plural' – all those limbs making one conjoined being, multiple hands grabbing at the same pole for support. Sadness is a kind of separation from yourself and others; it's the awareness when you close your eyes on an early morning commute of what could be – the murmur of collective possibility beyond the screech of rails.

'Empirical domain of revolution and revaluation,' Howe writes, about unconventional punctuation, 'where words are in danger, dissolving . . .' Notley's quotation marks put words in danger; we hear words differently through them. Paul Celan described the quotation mark as a rabbit (or bunny) ear 'listen[ing], not without fear, for something beyond itself, beyond words.' Notley's constant 'wrong' use of quotation marks brings out this quality. In breaking up the line with so many ears, the 'clear single edge' of each clause dissolves into the next, as voices (and bodies) do on a train. We're closer to language and what's beyond it. '"Which of us spoke? did" "it matter?".' Even in the midst of hell, Alette has a realisation: '"Perhaps heaven" "is voices, I thought," "speaking voices".'

## For Kalemie

**Chantal removes quotation marks entirely.** [[The NAPLAN Floor — Ten Mechanical Criteria|The NAPLAN floor]] docked it for that; [[The Three Gates — Unified Instrument|Gate 1a]] passes it as a *consistent system*. This essay supplies the third thing neither rubric could: **a reason.**

Three transferable claims:

**1 · Punctuation is expressive, not merely correct.** *"Gesturing outside itself and beyond the page."* The lineage — Dickinson, Bishop, Ashbery, Bidart, Kapil — is not experimental fringe. It is the centre.

**2 · Removing a mark can be a political act inside the story.** Howe on Dickinson: conventional punctuation abolished *"to subtract arbitrary authority."* Notley writes against a tyrant who *"owns form."*

> **Chantal is a story in which a girl's account of herself is repeatedly overruled — by her father, by the police, by a man with a car, by three men in a back room pricing her out loud.** Removing the marks that fence and license speech is the same gesture. The narration refuses to indicate where her voice is permitted to begin and end.

**3 · The reader's pace is the author's to set.** Notley's stated purpose — *make the reader slow down and silently articulate* — is the same effect Chantal's unmarked dialogue produces. There is no typographic boundary between what was said and what she has decided to remember, so the reader must do the sorting.

> [!warning] The honest limit
> Notley added an explanatory note because readers were confused. **A prize entry cannot append a note.** So the defence is real but it does not remove the risk — it only makes the risk a chosen one. Test it on a cold reader ([[10 The Three Moves — Reader, Winners, Record|Move 1]]) and ask specifically whether they were ever unsure who was speaking.
