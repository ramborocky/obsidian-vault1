# MCKEE — SCENE DESIGN

> A scene is a story in miniature: action through conflict, in a continuity of time and space, that turns the value-charged condition of a character's life. A single shot of a hand turning over a playing card can be a scene; ten minutes of battlefield action can fail to be one.

## Turning Points — Four Effects

Every real scene is a Turning Point (minor), Sequence Climax (moderate), or Act Climax (major). Never a flat, static delivery of information. A Turning Point delivers, in order:

1. **Surprise** — the gap between expectation and result jolts.
2. **Increased curiosity** — the audience asks *why?* and rushes back through the story so far.
3. **Insight** — the answer reconfigures everything seen before into a new, deeper pattern.
4. **New direction** — the story pivots on this new understanding toward what comes next.

> Self-expression happens here, not in dialogue. The unique way a writer turns a story — what they believe happens to people, and why — is the real authorial voice. Words are the surface; the turn is the substance.

## Setups and Payoffs

- A **setup** plants knowledge early with one apparent meaning; the **payoff** later reveals a second, deeper meaning, closing the gap the Turning Point opened.
- Calibrate the plant: too subtle and the audience misses it; too heavy and they see the turn coming. **Overprepare the obvious, underprepare the unusual** is the exact failure to avoid — flip it.
- Every payoff tends to become the next setup — a chain (McKee traces this through *Chinatown* and *The Empire Strikes Back*, where one revelation both closes and opens a gap in the same breath).
- **Logic in story is retroactive.** An impossible-seeming turn (loyal brother suddenly betrays his brother) can be made to feel inevitable by going back and quietly planting the buried motive (envy) under every earlier scene. Imagination comes first; making it logical is a rewrite problem, not a reason to reject the idea.

## Emotional Dynamics

- At root there are only two emotions: **pleasure and pain.** Everything else (joy, dread, grief, ecstasy) is a variation. An emotion requires: empathy with the character, knowing and wanting what they want, and understanding the values at stake — then a **transition** in those values is what actually moves the audience, not adjectives or swelling music.
- **Law of Diminishing Returns**: the same emotional beat repeated back-to-back loses power fast — first time full effect, second time half, third time it can flip into its opposite (three tragic scenes in a row: cry, sniffle, laugh). Comedy is the exception only because laughter isn't the emotion — it's a release valve inside whatever emotion the setup built.
- **Feeling vs. emotion**: emotion is short, sharp, and burns out fast; feeling is the long background mood that colors how a given emotion lands (the same bad news devastates on a dark day, is merely sad on a good one). In film, feeling = **mood** (light, color, tempo, music) — mood makes an emotion *specific*, but mood can never substitute for the transition of values that creates the emotion in the first place. A pretty sunset over an undramatized scene is decoration, not drama.

## The Nature of Choice — Dilemma, Not Doubt

> The choice between good and evil, or right and wrong, is no choice at all.

- Human beings always act toward what they perceive as good or right, from their own point of view — even a tyrant or a thief. So a clear moral-vs-immoral choice is dramatically trivial; the audience already knows the answer.
- **True choice is dilemma**, and comes in two forms:
  1. **Irreconcilable goods** — two desirable things, character wants both, can only take one.
  2. **Lesser of two evils** — two undesirable things, character wants neither, must pick one.
- A **two-sided conflict** (A wants B / A doesn't get B) is not dilemma, just vacillation — it has no real ending ("she loves me / she loves me not" can't close, whichever way it lands the audience assumes it'll just flip again).
- **Triangulate.** Add a third corner (C) and the relationships multiply into real dramatic material, and — critically — **closure becomes possible**: A is forced to choose between B and C, something is genuinely sacrificed, and that irreversible loss is what ends the story with weight.
