# MCKEE — THE SUBSTANCE OF STORY

> The stuff of story is not words. Language is only the medium. The true substance is intangible: the gap that splits open between what a person expects to happen and what actually happens.

## The Protagonist — Required Qualities

A protagonist (single, plural, or multi; human or not) must have:

1. **Will** — quantity doesn't matter as much as *quality*. Even a passive-seeming character (Blanche DuBois) can hide a powerful unconscious will.
2. **Conscious desire** — a known object of desire. Pulled aside and asked "what do you want?", the character has an answer.
3. **(Often) a self-contradictory unconscious desire** — the most memorable characters want, beneath awareness, the *opposite* of what they consciously pursue. The audience senses it before the character does.
4. **Capacity appropriate to the desire** — believable enough that we accept they *could* be doing what we're watching, win or lose.
5. **At least a chance** — a protagonist with zero possibility of getting what they want cannot hold interest. Hope needn't be reasonable; it must be hypothetical ("if I just... then maybe").
6. **Will and capacity to go to the end of the line** — to the human limit the setting and genre allow. A story must end at a final action beyond which the audience cannot imagine another — no "but shouldn't she have also...?" on the way out.
7. **Empathy, not necessarily sympathy** — *sympathetic* = likable. *Empathetic* = "like me," a shared humanity the audience recognizes even in a monster (McKee's example: Macbeth is empathetic because Shakespeare gives him a conscience — we feel his guilt is "just like mine"). **Empathy for the protagonist is not optional; sympathy is.**

## The First Step — Minimal, Conservative Action

- Every character, like every organism, always takes the **minimum, conservative action** from their own point of view first — never more effort or risk than the moment seems to require.
- In life, this minimal action usually works — 99% of the time the world reacts the way we expect. **Never, ever in a story.** Story lives exactly in the moment the expected reaction fails to arrive.

## The Gap

> Story is born in the place where the subjective and the objective touch.

- Character takes an action expecting a reaction (**probability** — what they believe will happen). The world instead responds differently or more powerfully (**necessity** — what actually happens).
- **The Gap** = the crack between probability and necessity, between expectation and result. This is where energy lives — for the character and, because the audience empathizes, for the audience too.
- Once a gap splits open, the character must take a harder, riskier second action — one that reaches deeper into will and capacity, because minimal effort no longer works. This repeats, escalating, to the end of the line.
- **True action** opens gaps and creates real change. **Mere activity** is behavior where expectation and result match — nothing happens. A knock on a door that's politely answered is activity, not a scene, and any competent editor will cut it.

## The Risk Test

> The measure of the value of a desire is in direct proportion to the risk taken to pursue it.

- Ask of any story: **what does the protagonist stand to lose if they don't get what they want?** If the honest answer is "life goes back to normal," the desire has no value and the story isn't worth telling.
- Value and risk rise together — we reserve "ultimate value" for what demands ultimate risk: freedom, life, the soul.

## Writing From the Inside Out

- Don't ask "how *should* someone act here?" (clichés/moralizing) or "how *might* someone act?" (clever but dishonest) or even "if my character were here, what would *he* do?" (guessing from outside — also clichés).
- Ask: **"If I were this character, in these circumstances, what would I do?"** — Stanislavski's "Magic if." Act the role in your imagination until the emotion is real for *you*; only then will it be real for the reader.
- To build the next beat, step back out and ask the dialectical question: **"What is the opposite of that?"** — pry the gap back open. Writing is a rhythm of diving into a character's point of view, then stepping outside to break their expectation, then diving into the next character's point of view to react, and so on.
- Fine writing emphasizes **reactions**, not actions. The action (detective finds a clue, lovers meet) is often genre-expected and therefore uninteresting on its own. What earns the scene is *to whom* it happens, *why*, and *how they react* — beat after beat of action → surprising reaction → gap → next action.
