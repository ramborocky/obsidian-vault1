# MCKEE — THE INCITING INCIDENT

> The Inciting Incident radically upsets the balance of forces in the protagonist's life — then arouses in him the desire to restore that balance.

## What It Must Do

1. **Upset the balance** — swing the value-charge of the protagonist's world sharply negative or positive. A change of scenery with no change of *value* (bored student moves city, stays bored) is not an Inciting Incident.
2. **Arouse desire to restore balance** — out of the imbalance, the protagonist forms a conscious **Object of Desire**: the thing that would set the world right again.
3. **(In complex characters) arouse an unconscious desire too** — one that contradicts the conscious one. The audience senses it before the character does (McKee's examples: *Carnal Knowledge*'s Jonathan consciously seeks "the perfect woman," unconsciously wants to degrade women; *The Crying Game*'s Fergus is consciously fighting for a political cause, unconsciously just wants to love and be loved).
4. **Launch the protagonist into pursuit** — a refusal to act is still a reaction, but it can't hold for long; everyone wants some sovereignty restored over their own life.
5. **Raise the Major Dramatic Question** in the audience's mind — a version of "how will this turn out?"
6. **Project the Obligatory Scene** — the confrontation with the story's ultimate antagonistic force that the audience now knows must happen before the story can end. Promise it, and you're obligated to deliver it.

## The Spine

- The **Spine** (a.k.a. Through-line / Super-objective) is the deep desire driving the protagonist to restore balance — the one thread every scene, image, and line ultimately serves.
- If there's no unconscious desire, the conscious goal *is* the Spine (Bond: defeat the villain — nothing else drives him, and nothing else needs to).
- If there **is** an unconscious desire, it becomes the Spine — deeper, more durable, and it lets the character's *conscious* goal shift and zigzag scene to scene while the story stays unified underneath (Ahab's obsession vs. Ishmael's real, unconscious quest to face his own destructive urges in *Moby Dick*).

## Design: Random or Causal

- An Inciting Incident happens only two ways: **by decision** (protagonist's own, or someone else's with power over him) or **by coincidence** (tragic or serendipitous). No third option.
- The Central Plot's Inciting Incident **must happen onscreen** — never in backstory, never between scenes. (This is already flagged as a live fix in the Dashboard.)
- Occasionally it needs a **setup + payoff** (Jaws: shark kills swimmer → sheriff finds the body). The payoff cannot be delayed by pages of unrelated material, or the story becomes "a sandwich of episodic slices of life between two halves of an Inciting Incident" (McKee's example of *The River*'s failure).

## Where to Place It

- Rule of thumb: within the **first 25%** of the telling (first ~30 minutes of a 2-hour film).
- **Test**: how much does the audience need to know about protagonist and world to react *fully* — emotionally and rationally — the instant it happens? Not a scene earlier, not a scene later.
- Archetypal incidents need **no setup** — they can open the story cold (*Kramer vs. Kramer*'s wife walks out in the first two minutes; *Jaws* opens on the shark).
- If placement runs past ~15 minutes, a **subplot** is needed to hold the audience while the world and characters are established (*Taxi Driver*, *Rocky*, *Casablanca* all do this) — but the wait must be earning its keep, not just delaying.
- Economy beats exposition: Bergman tells us almost nothing directly about his characters (widowed novelist, doctor, student, schizophrenic daughter) and trusts the audience to fill in the implications. **Less is more** — most writers over-explain the opening and under-trust the audience.

## Quality of the Event

- An event can be as small as "a woman putting her hand on the table and looking at you a certain way" (Henry James) — scale doesn't matter, *function* does. Test any candidate event against the six requirements above.

## Two Generative Questions for Finding It

1. **What is the worst possible thing that could happen to my protagonist — and how could that become the best?** (*Kramer vs. Kramer*: wife leaves → forces him to finally become a loving father.)
2. **What is the best possible thing — and how could it become the worst?** (*Death in Venice*: recuperation trip → fatal, hopeless obsession.)

Stories may cycle this more than once. Story lives at the extremes, not the middle ground.

## For Kalemie

- The Dashboard's #1 flagged fix **is** this chapter's first requirement: move the Inciting Incident — Daudi learning Djamela is dead — fully onscreen. Right now it's implied/offscreen, which means the audience never gets the upset-balance moment in real time, and the Major Dramatic Question never properly ignites.
- Identify Daudi's Spine now, using the unconscious-desire test above: does he consciously want to stay uninvolved/safe, while unconsciously wanting to matter, to be someone who doesn't look away? If so, *that* unconscious desire — not the conscious one — is the Spine, and it should survive every scene even as his conscious goals shift (investigate quietly → confront Jean Luc → do nothing → witness).
- The Obligatory Scene this incident should project: the audience needs to sense, from the moment Daudi learns of Djamela's death, that a confrontation with Jean Luc (or the system he represents) is coming and must be shown — not summarized.
- Worst-becomes-best test for Daudi: the worst thing (Djamela's death) — how does it become the best, i.e., what does it force into being that wasn't there before (his choice to actively witness rather than passively collect debris)?

## Questions for AI

1. Does Daudi's Inciting Incident happen onscreen, in real time, or is it currently reported/implied?
2. What conscious Object of Desire does it give Daudi — and what unconscious desire, if any, contradicts it?
3. What Obligatory Scene does this incident promise the audience, and am I certain to deliver it?
