# MCKEE — SCENE ANALYSIS (TEXT, SUBTEXT, THE FIVE-STEP METHOD)

> "If the scene is about what the scene is about, you're in deep shit." — writing "on the nose" means writing the subtext directly into the text, and it kills the scene.

## Text vs. Subtext

- **Text** = the sensory surface: what's said, what's shown, what's done.
- **Subtext** = the life underneath: thoughts and feelings, known and unknown, that the text conceals.
- Nothing is what it seems — this is not cynicism, it's how real people function. We wear a public mask because we must; even alone, we hide truths from ourselves. **Subtext exists even in first-person narration, direct address, and soliloquy** — a character confessing to camera can still be lying to themselves.
- A scene with no subtext is unactable — candlelight, swelling music, and "I love you, I love you" meant literally is dead on arrival. Actors build from the inside out and need something underneath to play *against* the words.
- Practical trick: **write the surface activity as something else entirely** (two people fixing a flat tire) and let the real subtext — attraction, guilt, a goodbye — live entirely in what's unsaid. The audience reads through the surface and feels the pleasure of insight life rarely gives them.

## The Five-Step Analysis

Use this to diagnose a scene that reads flat, or to build one from scratch:

1. **Define Conflict** — Who drives the scene? What do they want (phrase it as an infinitive: "to convince her that…")? What force of antagonism opposes them, and what does *it* want? In a working scene, the two desires are in **direct opposition**, not just tangential.
2. **Note Opening Value** — Name the value at stake (love, freedom, faith, trust…) and its charge (+/–) at the scene's start.
3. **Break into Beats** — A beat is one exchange of action/reaction. Name each side's *behavior* with an active gerund that captures the subtext, not the literal activity — "Groveling at her feet," not "kneeling." The beat continues as long as the pattern holds, even through repetition; a **new beat begins only when behavior visibly changes.**
4. **Note Closing Value, Compare to Opening** — If the value's charge is unchanged, the scene is a **nonevent**: exposition may have passed, but nothing happened, and it should be cut or rebuilt. If it changed, the scene turned.
5. **Survey the Beats, Locate the Turning Point** — Read the full gerund sequence top to bottom. A well-built scene shows an arc where each beat tops the last, demanding more will and risk, until the exact moment the gap springs open — that's the Turning Point.

## Two Model Patterns (from McKee's own analyses)

- **Casablanca's bazaar scene** — the Turning Point is a **revelation** (Ilsa was already married to Laszlo in Paris): built almost entirely through dialogue, each beat topping the last (approaching → protecting → apologizing → guilt-tripping → propositioning → devastating reveal), all while both characters stay outwardly composed.
- **Through a Glass Darkly's attic scene** — the Turning Point is an **action** (the hallucinated spider-god's assault), built through blocking and physical behavior with almost no explanatory dialogue, beats escalating from comic (Martin can't find his shoes) to terror.
- Lesson: the Turning Point can live in a line of dialogue *or* in physical action — whichever the scene's conflict actually calls for. Diagnose by beat-gerund first; the medium follows.
