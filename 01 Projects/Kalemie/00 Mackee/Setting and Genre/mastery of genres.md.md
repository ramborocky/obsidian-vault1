# MCKEE — MASTERY OF GENRE

## The Principle

> To anticipate the anticipations of the audience you must master your genre and its conventions.

---

## Why Genre Mastery Matters

1. **You must fulfill audience anticipations** — or risk confusion and disappointment
2. **You must lead expectations to fresh moments** — or risk boring them
3. **This two-handed trick is impossible without a knowledge of genre that surpasses the audience's**

---

## How to Study Genre

### Step 1: List
> List all those works you feel are like yours, both successes and failures. (The study of failures is illuminating … and humbling.)

### Step 2: Analyze
> Study the films stop and go, turning pages with the screen, breaking each film down into elements of setting, role, event, and value.

### Step 3: Synthesize
> Stack these analyses one atop the other and ask: What do the stories in my genre always do? What are its conventions of time, place, character, and action?

---

## Genre Study Questions

1. What does the genre always do?
2. What are its conventions of time, place, character, and action?
3. What are its values at stake?
4. What are its typical turning points?
5. What are its typical endings?

## Positioning the Audience

> "Positioning the audience" means: We don't want people coming to our work cold and vague. We want them to settle into their seats, warm and focused with an appetite we intend to satisfy.

### For Kalemie
- **Title:** What will the title position?
- **Opening:** What does the first scene promise?
- **Genre:** How do I signal the genre?
- **Tone:** What mood do I establish?

---

## Creative Limitations

> Genre conventions are the rhyme scheme of a storyteller's "poem." They do not inhibit creativity, they inspire it.

### The Challenge
- **Keep convention** (the rhyme scheme)
- **Avoid cliché** (the stale, overused rhyme)

### For Kalemie
- **Convention:** Passive protagonist
- **Cliché to Avoid:** The passive protagonist who does nothing and learns nothing
- **Fresh Take:** Daudi's passivity IS the action. He chooses not to act. That's a choice.
