# MCKEE — CREATIVE LIMITATION

## The Principle

> Limitation is vital. The first step toward a well-told story is to create a small, **knowable** world.

---

## The Paradox

> **The irony of setting versus story is this:**
>
> **The larger the world, the more diluted the knowledge of the writer, therefore the fewer his creative choices and the more clichéd the story.**
>
> **The smaller the world, the more complete the knowledge of the writer, therefore the greater his creative choices.**
>
> **Result: a fully original story and victory in the war on cliché.**

---

## What "Knowable" Means

> The world of a story must be small enough that the mind of a single artist can surround the fictional universe it creates and come to know it in the same depth and detail that God knows the one He created.

**By the time you finish your last draft, you must possess a commanding knowledge of your setting in such depth and detail that no one could raise a question about your world—from the eating habits of your characters to the weather in September—that you couldn't answer instantly.**

---

## Examples of Small, Knowable Worlds

| Story | World | Characters |
|-------|-------|------------|
| Crime and Punishment | Microscopic | Few |
| War and Peace | Russia in turmoil | A handful of families |
| Dr. Strangelove | Three sets | Eight principals |

**These worlds are SMALL because they are KNOWABLE.**

## For Kalemie

### What Makes Kalemie "Small"?
- One town
- A handful of neighborhoods
- A lake that is always present
- A few key locations: dala dala, police station, hotel, Daudi's room

### What Must I Know?
- The geography of Kalemie
- The social structure
- The political reality
- The lake (as a character)
- The smells, sounds, textures of the place
- What people eat, how they speak, what they believe

---

## The War on Cliché

> Cliché is at the root of audience dissatisfaction. The source of all clichés can be traced to one thing and one thing alone: **The writer does not know the world of his story.**

### The Cure: Research

> The key to winning this war is **research** — taking the time and effort to acquire knowledge.

**Three Methods:**
1. **Memory:** What do I know from personal experience?
2. **Imagination:** What would it be like to live my character's life?
3. **Fact:** What can I learn from research?

---

## The Research Methods

### 1. Memory
> Lean back from your desk and ask, "What do I know from personal experience that touches on my characters' lives?"

**Example:**
- You're writing about fear. Memory takes you back to the day you were locked in a closet. Write that down. That becomes working knowledge.

### 2. Imagination
> Lean back and ask, "What would it be like to live my character's life hour by hour, day by day?"

**Example:**
- How does Daudi collect things? When does he find them? What does he feel when he finds them?

### 3. Fact
> You're blocked because you have nothing to say. Do research. Feed your talent.

**Example:**
- Research the DRC. Research Lake Tanganyika. Research the police system. Research class structure. Research colognes, BMW models, school uniforms.

---

## The "Virgin Birth" Phenomenon

> Research from memory, imagination, and fact is often followed by a phenomenon that authors love to describe in mystical terms: Characters suddenly spring to life and of their own free will make choices and take actions that create Turning Points.

**The Truth:** This marks the moment when a writer's knowledge of the subject has reached the saturation point. The writer becomes the god of his little universe.

---

## Research Warning

> While research provides material, it's no substitute for creativity.

- Research is meat to feed the beasts of imagination and invention.
- Never an end in itself.
- Research must not become procrastination.
