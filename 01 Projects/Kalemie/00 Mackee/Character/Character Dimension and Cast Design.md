# MCKEE — CHARACTER: DIMENSION, CAST DESIGN, SELF-KNOWLEDGE

> A character is no more a human being than the Venus de Milo is a real woman — a character is a work of art, a metaphor for human nature, designed to be knowable in a way real people never fully are.

## Dimension Means Contradiction, Not Decoration

- A common mistake: piling on quirks (breakdances, plays sax, has a black belt) to make a character feel "three-dimensional." This produces a flat character with decorations stuck on, not a deep one.
- **Dimension = contradiction** — either *within* deep character (Macbeth: ambition **and** guilt, not just ambition — the contradiction is what generates the tragedy) or *between* characterization and deep character (a charming thief). Hamlet isn't three-dimensional, he's practically uncountable-dimensional: tender then callous, courageous then cowardly, lucid then mad — a living contradiction, consistently so.
- Contradictions must be **consistent**, not random — it doesn't add dimension to write someone as kind for the whole story then have them kick a cat once for shock value.
- **The protagonist must be the most dimensional character in the cast.** If a supporting character or antagonist out-dimensions the lead, empathy drifts to them instead and the story's Center of Good decenters (McKee's example: *Blade Runner*'s Roy Batty out-dimensioned Deckard, and the audience's attachment shifted to the "wrong" character, muddying the film's reception).

## Motivation — Understand It, Don't Over-Explain It

- Ask of any character: what do they want, now / soon / overall, consciously / unconsciously? Clear answers give command of the role.
- Resist reducing motivation to a single tidy cause ("it's because of his childhood trauma") — real behavior has many tangled causes, and over-explaining diminishes a character in the audience's imagination. Understand the *why* fully yourself, but leave a residue of mystery on the page for the reader's own life experience to fill in.
- **Self-explanatory dialogue is a red flag.** A character who accurately narrates their own psychology out loud is both boring and false — people don't have that kind of clean self-knowledge. What a character *says* about themselves is a data point, not the truth; only choice under pressure confirms or contradicts it.

## Cast Design — the Solar System Model

- The protagonist is the sun; every other character exists, first and foremost, **because of the relationship they strike to the protagonist** and what dimension of them that relationship reveals.
- If the protagonist is compassionate-then-cruel, fearless-then-fearful, you need different characters to *pull out* each contradictory side in different scenes — the supporting cast's job is to make the lead's complexity both visible and credible.
- Supporting roles can be scaled-down but still dimensional (even one sharp contradiction, like the Terminator's machine/human duality, makes a compelling antagonist). **Bit parts should be flat but not dull** — one fresh, specific trait for their moment onscreen, no more. Giving a minor character *too much* dimension creates false anticipation — the reader will expect them to return.

## The Comic Character — Blind Obsession

- Dramatic characters can step back from risk ("this could get me killed"). **Comic characters can't — they're marked by an obsession they cannot see in themselves.** The instant a comic character *sees* their own obsession and names it, the comedy ends and they become a dramatic/romantic figure instead (*A Fish Called Wanda*'s Archie Leach realizing his fear of embarrassment mid-film, becoming "Cary Grant").
- Practical diagnostic for a character who's supposed to be funny but isn't: find their un-seen mania first — the joke follows from that, not from clever line-writing.

## Three Working Principles

1. **Leave room for the performer.** Don't over-specify every gesture, tone, and inflection — trust the medium (actor, or in prose, the reader's own inference) to do the work a line doesn't need to spell out. Overwritten stage direction reads as distrust of the collaborator.
2. **Fall in love with all your characters — especially the villains.** A writer who visibly holds contempt for a character produces a flat, insulting caricature. Sociopaths in life are typically charming, not mustache-twirling — write from inside a villain's own conviction that they're doing the reasonable thing, never from outside contempt.
3. **Character is self-knowledge.** We only ever fully know one person — ourselves — and yet all humans share the same fundamental thoughts and struggles underneath surface difference. "If I were this character in these circumstances, what would I do?" is always answerable honestly, because the honest answer is always the human answer. The deeper your own self-knowledge, the deeper every character you write.
