# MCKEE — STRUCTURE AND CHARACTER

> Structure is character; character is structure. They are the same thing seen from two sides. If you change one, you have changed the other.

## Character vs. Characterization

- **Characterization** = every observable trait: age, job, style of speech, dress, habits, IQ, sexuality. The sum of what a private investigator could write down. This is the *mask*.
- **TRUE CHARACTER** is revealed only in **the choices a human being makes under pressure** — to pursue a desire in one way rather than another. The greater the pressure, the truer the revelation. As he chooses, he *is*.
- A choice made when nothing is at risk reveals nothing. Telling the truth when a lie costs you nothing is trivial; telling the truth when a lie would save your life exposes the core.

## Why Contradiction Beats Consistency

- Fine storytelling puts true character in **contrast or contradiction** to characterization. "What seems is not what is."
- If a character stays exactly what he first appeared to be, the story has told us nothing. Match between mask and depth = boring, predictable, flat (McKee's example: Rambo after the sequels flattened him into his own poster).
- Longevity of a character (Bond across ~20 films) comes from the **repeated pleasure of a deep nature contradicting the surface mask** — playboy exterior, thinking-man's killer underneath.
- Minor roles can stay flat. **Principals must be written in depth** — they cannot be at heart what they seem to be at face.

## Character Arc — the Five-Beat Pattern (Hamlet as model)

1. Establish characterization (surface: melancholy student home for a funeral).
2. Choices begin exposing true nature (cautious intelligence battling rash passion).
3. True nature contrasts/contradicts the surface persona ("mad north-north-west").
4. Story escalates pressure, forcing harder and harder choices.
5. By the climax, the choices have **changed** the character's inner nature, for better or worse.

> The finest writing doesn't just reveal true character — it *arcs* it. The person who exits the story is not the person who entered it.

## Structure's Job vs. Character's Job

| | Function |
|---|---|
| **Structure** | Builds progressively harder pressure, forcing progressively harder choices, revealing character down to the unconscious self |
| **Character** | Brings the traits (age, strength, worldliness, wit) needed to make those choices *credible* — the audience must believe this person could and would do this |

- Change the events → you've changed the character. Change deep character → you must reinvent the events to express it. "Character-driven story" is redundant: **all** story is character-driven; what people usually mean is *characterization*-driven (a well-drawn mask over an undeveloped interior).
- Complexity of character must match genre: Action/Farce wants simple character (complexity distracts from derring-do); Education/Redemption plots demand complexity (simplicity would rob the insight the genre exists to deliver).

## Climax Is Where Character Is Paid Off

- "Movies are about their last twenty minutes" — no matter how good the first 90 are, a flat final act kills the film retroactively (McKee: *Blind Date* vs. *Kiss of the Spider Woman*).
- ~75% of a writer's total effort goes into interlocking character with event design; of *that*, ~75% goes into engineering the final act's climax. The climax is the ultimate expression of who this person turned out to be.
- If the ending you want doesn't fit the character you built, **rewrite the characterization, not the true character** (McKee's example: keep the 75-year-old's tenacity, just make him 35 so the desert climax is credible).
