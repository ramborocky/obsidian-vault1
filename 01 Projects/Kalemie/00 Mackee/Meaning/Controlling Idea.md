# MCKEE — STRUCTURE AND MEANING

> Aesthetic emotion: in life, idea and emotion arrive separately — you see a body, feel shock, understand meaning only later. Story fuses them into one instant. That fusion is what art gives you that life can't.

## Premise vs. Controlling Idea

- **Premise** = the open question that starts the writing — "What would happen if…?" Not precious; abandon it the moment the evolving story outgrows it.
- **Controlling Idea** = the story's final, closed meaning, proven (not stated) by the last act's climax.
- A story **proves** an idea through the consequences of action — it never explains itself through a character's mouth. If a line of dialogue exists to voice the theme, cut it. The audience must believe, not just understand.

## The Formula: Value + Cause

> A Controlling Idea is a single sentence describing how and why life changes from one condition at the opening to another at the close.

- **Value** = the charge (positive/negative) of the critical value at climax. ("Justice is restored…")
- **Cause** = the dominant reason that value changed. ("...because the protagonist is more violent than the criminals.")
- Half a sentence ("Crime doesn't pay") is not a Controlling Idea — it's only the value. You need the *cause* too, or you have no guidance for what belongs in the story and what doesn't.
- The Controlling Idea is a **Creative Discipline**: it tells you what's appropriate to keep and what's irrelevant and must be cut.
- You don't invent the Controlling Idea and impose it — you build toward a climax that moves you, then **work backward** from that climax to find the sentence it was proving all along. Idea comes from action, not the reverse.

## Idea vs. Counter-Idea

- A story is a dialectical argument: sequence by sequence, the positive **Idea** and negative **Counter-Idea** trade blows, escalating, until they collide at Crisis and one wins at Climax.
- **Didacticism trap**: if you slant the argument — make the Counter-Idea's case weak so your Idea wins too easily — you get a sermon, not a story (McKee's Social Drama warning: "war is a scourge cured by pacifism" repeated until the audience wants to pick up a gun). Give the *other* side its full, honest power, or your ending proves nothing.

## Three Emotional Charges

| Charge | Pattern | Feel |
|---|---|---|
| **Idealistic** | up-ending | life as we wish it were |
| **Pessimistic** | down-ending | life as we dread it is |
| **Ironic** | up/down at once, in the *same* action | life as it actually, relentlessly is |

- Irony is the hardest and longest-lasting: not ambiguity (a blur) and not coincidence (an accident) — a single, clearly-motivated action that is *both* a gain and a loss at once, and the two charges must stay legible side-by-side rather than cancelling out.
- Modern "Redemption Plot" irony: protagonist wins by *refusing* to act on the very obsession that drove the story — a nonaction that must be staged to feel like a positive.

## Self-Recognition

> You do not dictate meaning to the story; the story tells you its meaning.

- The Controlling Idea a writer finally lands on is often a shock — a self-recognition of what was "skulking inside" them. If your ending works out exactly as first planned, you weren't loose enough with your instincts.

## Social Responsibility

- McKee's line: the writer's only responsibility is **to tell the truth**. Before finishing, ask: *Do I believe this?* If no, throw it out. If yes, get it into the world — an honest Controlling Idea, however dark, is itself an act of responsibility.

## For Kalemie

- Dashboard already has a working Controlling Idea: *"Inaction is complicity because choosing not to act is still a choice"* with Counter-Idea *"Action is futile because the system is rigged."* This is real Value + Cause — keep it pinned and test every scene against it.
- Per the didacticism warning above: give the Counter-Idea real teeth. Don't let "the system is rigged" be a throwaway line — dramatize a moment where acting *actually* fails or costs someone dearly (Jean Luc's power going unpunished, the police station's thin envelope), so that Daudi's final choice to witness rather than act is won honestly, not by a stacked deck.
- The likely emotional charge is **ironic**, not purely down-ending: Daudi doesn't get justice (negative) but he keeps faith with Djamela's memory instead of looking away (positive) — gain and loss in the same final gesture. Make sure both halves stay legible in the ending image (per the Dashboard's "finds the envelope, nails it, smiles" beat) rather than blurring into pure bleakness or pure uplift.
- Test scene-by-scene: does this scene argue "inaction is complicity" or does it argue "action is futile"? If a scene does neither, it's not yet earning its place.

## Cross-Reference (McKee ↔ Cleland)

- [[Know Your Readers|Narrative Question]] (Cleland) — the Controlling Idea (Value + Cause) is retrospective/thematic, proven through the climax's action rather than stated in dialogue. Cleland's Narrative Question is the same organizing promise viewed reader-side and forward-looking: raised early enough that readers buy in before the meaning is earned. Know your ending's meaning (McKee), and plant the question it answers early enough (Cleland).
