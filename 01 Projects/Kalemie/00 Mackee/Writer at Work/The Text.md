# MCKEE — THE TEXT (DIALOGUE, DESCRIPTION, IMAGE SYSTEMS, TITLES)

> "Speak as common people do, but think as wise men do." — Aristotle

## Dialogue Is Not Conversation

- Real conversation is full of dead air, non sequiturs, and going nowhere — that's fine in life ("keeping the channel open") but unusable on the page. Two friends discussing "the weather" are really saying "I'm your friend, let's reaffirm that" — **the text is never the subtext; the scene is never about what it seems to be about.**
- Screen/page dialogue needs the *swing* of natural talk with content well above normal density:
  1. **Compression and economy** — say the maximum in the fewest words.
  2. **Direction** — every exchange must turn the beat, never repeat the last one.
  3. **Purpose** — every line executes a step toward the scene's Turning Point.
- Never write a line that calls attention to itself as "fine writing" — the instant you think a line is particularly clever or literary, cut it.

## Short Speeches and the Suspense Sentence

- The engine of good dialogue is **stichomythia** — rapid, short exchanges. Long, uninterrupted speeches are a last resort; if one is necessary, break it with the listener's silent reactions (a look away, a wince) so the speaker is visibly reacting to a reaction, not reciting a prepared statement — **there is no such thing as a monologue in life; even alone, a person reacts to their own last thought.**
- **The Suspense Sentence** (the periodic sentence): delay the meaning to the very last word — "If you didn't want me to do it, why'd you give me that... [gun/kiss/look]?" — forces the reader to stay to the end of the line rather than drifting once the point lands early.
- Avoid dialogue that limply trails off with unimportant words at the end (prepositional-phrase filler) — meaning should land, not dribble.

## The Silent Page — Write for the Image First

> The best advice for writing dialogue is: don't. Never write a line where a visual/physical beat can do the same work.

- Attack every scene by first asking: could this turn without a single line of dialogue? Obey the Law of Diminishing Returns — the more dialogue accumulates, the less any single line lands. Lean dialogue set against mostly-physical, mostly-visual writing has power precisely because it's scarce; a wall-to-wall talk scene buries its own best lines.
- McKee's model scene (*The Silence*): a seduction written entirely through one physical gesture (a dropped napkin, a slow inhale) — zero dialogue, fully legible, more erotic and more efficient than any line of talk could be.

## Description — Concrete Over Abstract

- **Vivid action in the now**: favor the sharpest possible verb and the most specific possible noun. Not "the carpenter uses a big nail" but "the carpenter hammers a spike." Not "he moves slowly across the room" but "he shuffles / lurches / creeps / totters across the room" — each specific verb *is* a different character choice, not decoration.
- **Eliminate "is/are" wherever possible.** Static "to be" constructions ("there is a big house on a hill") flatten description into inventory. Replace with active verbs that put the image in motion: "a mansion guards the headlands above the village."
- **Cut metaphor/simile that can't be pictured or sensed.** "The sun sets like a tiger's eye closing in the jungle" is unfilmable and, in prose, just as unearned — it draws attention to the writer's cleverness rather than to the image. A metaphor should be checked against: can the reader actually *see or hear* this, concretely?

## Image Systems

- An **Image System** is a category of imagery (an element of nature, a class of object, a repeated sensation) that recurs from beginning to end, in variation, **without being announced** — a subliminal channel that deepens meaning without ever surfacing to conscious notice. The instant an audience/reader can point at it and say "Symbol!", it has died as a device and become a hollow intellectual curiosity.
- **External Imagery**: borrows a symbol that already means something in the wider culture (a flag = patriotism, a crucifix = faith) and uses it straight. Effective but the most obvious, most "student-film" option.
- **Internal Imagery**: takes something with no fixed outside meaning — or deliberately *inverts* its usual meaning — and builds an entirely new private meaning for this story alone (McKee's example: *Les Diaboliques* turns water, normally a symbol of purification and life, into the story's engine of dread and death, through sheer patient repetition).
- A story can carry multiple Image Systems at once (*Casablanca* runs imprisonment, particular-to-archetypal, and linking/separating simultaneously) as long as each stays quiet enough to work beneath notice.

## Titles

- A title should **name something solid actually in the story** — character, setting, theme, or genre — not a vague, "literary"-sounding abstraction. The strongest titles name two or three of these at once (*Kramer vs. Kramer*: two characters + divorce theme + genre, in four words).

## Questions for AI

1. Could this scene's key beat be written with zero dialogue, purely through physical action?
2. Is any line here just "conversation" (going nowhere) instead of turning the beat?
3. Does any image in this draft explain its own symbolism, or does it stay quiet enough to work subliminally?
4. Is there a generic noun/verb + adjective/adverb here that should collapse into one sharp, specific word instead?
