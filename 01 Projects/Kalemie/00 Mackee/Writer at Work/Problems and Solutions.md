# MCKEE — PROBLEMS AND SOLUTIONS

> Recurring craft problems, each with a durable solution.

## The Problem of Interest — Curiosity + Concern

- **Curiosity** = the intellectual need to close an open question ("how will this turn out?"). Every Turning Point reopens it.
- **Concern** = the emotional need to root for positive values (justice, love, survival). The audience instinctively hunts for the **Center of Good** — whoever represents the positive, even in a story full of criminals — because everyone, however flawed, believes their own heart is in the right place, and looks for a character to match.
- You don't need a likable cast, just *one* clear positive core the audience can attach to (even Michael Corleone's "family loyalty" among criminals, even Hannibal Lecter's wit and calm against a crueler system around him).

### Three Audience/Story Relationships

| Mode | Who knows what | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Mystery** | Audience knows *less* than characters | Pure curiosity — a guessing game (red herrings, withheld facts). Native to the Murder Mystery genre only. |
| **Suspense** | Audience knows the *same* as characters | Curiosity + Concern together — ~90% of all stories. We don't know *what* happens, and we care who it happens to. |
| **Dramatic Irony** | Audience knows *more* than characters | Pure concern — dread and compassion replace anxiety, because we already know where this is heading and can only watch it arrive. |

- Most stories aren't purely one mode — they shift a scene or sequence at a time between them for effect (*Casablanca*'s Paris flashback is Dramatic Irony; the Crisis-decision scene switches to Mystery by hiding Rick's choice from us).
- **False Mystery**: withholding information not because the story needs to, but to disguise boring, undramatized material as a "tease." A cheap trick, not a technique — if a fact could be given now without loss, giving it late for suspense's sake is a stall, not a design.

## The Problem of Surprise

- **True surprise**: the Gap between expectation and result, followed by a rush of *insight*.
- **Cheap surprise**: a jolt that exploits the audience's vulnerability in the dark with no insight behind it — a false scare, a fake-out dream, a shock with no truth underneath. Aristotle: "to be about to act and not to act is the worst — shocking without being tragic."

## The Problem of Coincidence

- Coincidence is part of life — don't ban it, but obey two rules:
  1. **Bring coincidence in early**, so there's time for it to *gather meaning* rather than stay a random, pointless event (Jaws's shark starts as random chance; by staying and menacing, it accrues the meaning of malevolent nature).
  2. **Never use coincidence to turn an ending.** That's **deus ex machina** — "the writer's greatest sin." A convenient accident that resolves the climax (a sudden storm, a lucky accident) erases meaning and insults the audience, because real life demands we make meaning through our own choices, not luck. As a rule of thumb, **stop leaning on coincidence past the story's midpoint** — hand the ending fully to the characters' own decisions.

## The Problem of Point of View

- Two senses: the literal camera/POV shot, and the deeper question — **from whose vantage is each scene, and the whole story, imagined?**
- Disciplining an entire story to the protagonist's Point of View — living every event only as they encounter it — is harder than hopscotching for convenient exposition, but produces a tighter, more memorable, more deeply empathetic result. More time spent inside one character's experience = more empathy earned.

## The Problem of Melodrama

> Melodrama is not the result of overexpression. It's the result of under-motivation.

- A scene isn't melodramatic because the events are extreme — human beings are capable of anything, and none of it is inherently "too much." A scene reads as melodrama only when **the motivation doesn't match the size of the action.** The fix for an "overwritten" scene is almost never to shrink the event — it's to build the causes underneath it until they equal or exceed what the character does.

## The Problem of Holes

- A hole = a missing logical link in cause and effect. Two honest options:
  1. **Close it** if you can, without creating an awkward, purposeless patch-scene just to plug the gap.
  2. **If you can't close it, ask: will the audience notice, moving at story speed rather than sitting still with the page?** If a small hole will pass unnoticed in the flow of the telling, it may not need fixing.
  3. If it will be noticed, **name it rather than hide it** — give a character a line acknowledging the oddity (*Casablanca*'s Ferrari: "Why I'm doing this I don't know...") rather than pretending it isn't there. Audiences forgive an acknowledged human inconsistency; they don't forgive being condescended to.

## Questions for AI

2. Am I using Mystery, Suspense, or Dramatic Irony here — and is that the deliberate choice, or did it happen by accident?
