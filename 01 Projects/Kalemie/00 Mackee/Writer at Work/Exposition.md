# MCKEE — EXPOSITION

> Skill in exposition means making it invisible. You don't keep an audience's interest by giving it information — you keep it by withholding information, except what's absolutely necessary for comprehension.

## Show, Don't Tell — Exposition as Ammunition

- Never have characters tell each other things they both already know just so the audience overhears it ("Harry, we've been friends twenty years, since college..."). That's "table dusting" — 19th-century maids exposition-dumping while dusting furniture, and it's still everywhere in weak writing.
- **Convert exposition into ammunition.** Characters know their history, their world, each other — let them weaponize that knowledge in the *immediate conflict*, and the facts pass to the audience as a side effect, not the point. (McKee's fix for the "twenty years" example: Jack needles Harry about the same juvenile stunts that got him kicked out of school twenty years ago — the audience gets the fact while watching a jab land.)
- Two rules for what to even include:
  1. **Never explain what the audience can reasonably assume.**
  2. **Never pass on a fact unless withholding it would cause confusion.**

## Pacing Exposition — Save the Best for Last

- Least important facts early, most important later, **the critical facts — secrets — last**, at maximum pressure. This is why the biggest exposition dumps (Evelyn's incest confession, Vader's paternity reveal) land at Crisis/Climax, not in Act One.
- Avoid "California scenes" — strangers trading unearned, intimate trauma over coffee within minutes of meeting. Real secrets come out only under real pressure, from people who have a real relationship, and never voluntarily, only when squeezed by dilemma.
- You can also **manufacture the need to know** — plant a "why?" in the audience's mind, and once curiosity is aroused, even dense factual exposition goes down easily.

## Backstory — The Engine of Revelation Turns

- Scenes turn only two ways: **on action**, or **on revelation.** There's no third option.
- Backstory is the reservoir of significant past events the writer can detonate at exactly the right moment to create a Turning Point — not dribbled out early "for the reader's convenience." (*Chinatown*'s incest, *Empire*'s paternity — both could have been explained early by a minor character; both are saved instead to blow open a major Climax.)
- **In medias res** as default: because a Spine strong enough to span a whole lifetime is rare, most stories should start as close as possible to the climactic event, giving the character maximum accumulated life (and things to lose) *before* the Inciting Incident — not narrating the whole biography from childhood.
- Corollary: **write about people who have something to lose.** A life with nothing at stake (McKee's example: a homeless person with literally nothing left to risk) can only produce a static portrait of suffering, not a story — jeopardy requires an existing balance to be thrown off.

## Flashback, Dream, Montage, Voice-Over — Handle With Suspicion

- **Flashbacks**: only good if dramatized as a real scene with its own Inciting Incident and turn — not a flat, static "remembering." And never bring one in before the audience actively *wants* to know (Casablanca holds the Paris flashback until curiosity about Rick and Ilsa is already burning).
- **Dream sequences**: "exposition in a ball gown" — almost always a lazy disguise for information, rarely earns its place.
- **Montage**: rapid-cut time-compression that keeps the eye busy while smuggling in plain information — "a lazy attempt to substitute decorative editing for dramatization." Avoid by default.
- **Voice-over narration**: test it by cutting it out. If the story still stands and plays well without it, the narration was decorative at best — cut it, or keep it only if it adds something no other technique can (ironic counterpoint, à la Woody Allen), never to explain plot, upbringing, or feelings directly.

## Questions for AI

1. Is this fact being told directly, or is a character using it as ammunition in a live conflict?
2. Is this secret being revealed too early, robbing a later scene of its Turning-Point power?
3. If I cut this piece of narrated backstory/interiority entirely, does the scene still work? If yes, cut it or convert it into a live scene.
4. Does the reader actually want to know this yet — have I built the curiosity that makes this exposition welcome rather than a chore?
