# CLELAND — ISOLATE YOUR PROTAGONIST (AND EVERYONE ELSE)

> Isolation tempers the strong.

## Two Kinds of Isolation, Three Uses

- **Physical isolation**: prevented from interacting with others (solitary confinement, a deserted island).
- **Social isolation**: feeling like you don't fit, being shunned, or something internal that precludes belonging.
- Either kind can function as a plot point/TRD, a theme, or backstory explaining a character trait — decide which job it's doing before you write it.

## Isolation Drives Believable Characterization

- Humans are wired to socialize; isolation (of either kind) is linked to depression, anxiety, and — in extremis — violence. Understanding *why* a character is isolated, even if never stated on the page, makes every line about them carry unstated weight.
- "Hidden in plain sight" isolation is often the most powerful: characters (and real people) who present a happy face precisely because the truth would invite ridicule or attack. Presenting only one side of yourself is, by definition, self-isolation.

## The Metaphor Machine

- Five-step process for turning an abstraction into an image: (1) state the abstract idea/emotion in one phrase; (2) attach it to one of the five senses + "like"; (3) brainstorm freely, no editing; (4) find the pattern among your answers; (5) write the sentence.
- A metaphor (X *is* Y) beats a simile (X *like* Y) — it commits instead of merely gesturing at a resemblance.

## For Kalemie

- Djamela's isolation is social, not physical, and it's the engine of her entire arc: wanting to be one of the "ghost kids," feeling permanently on the outside of Rue Saint Michel. Cleland's framework clarifies that this isn't background color — it's the backstory-as-character-trait use of isolation, and it's what makes her vulnerable to Jean Luc credible rather than contrived. The Craft Log's own Structural Notes question ("what does Djamela know that Daudi doesn't?") is really asking: what does her isolation feel like from the inside, which the story currently only shows from Daudi's outside view.
- Daudi experiences a colder, more literal isolation by the story's end — the retrospective narrator who saw the signs and said nothing. His hidden-in-plain-sight quality (a boy who "arranges things" — the motherboard, the hubcap, the antelope skull — rather than feels them) is exactly Cleland's point about presenting one side of yourself: his scientific detachment is itself a form of self-isolation the story could make more visible as isolation, not just as quirk.
- The lake metaphor is already, without needing Cleland's Metaphor Machine spelled out, doing real Metaphor Machine work: D'juma's "the lake is cold just like some women... it remembers everything" and Djamela's "if you stare long enough you can see your own death" both commit to metaphor (lake *is* memory, lake *is* death) rather than settling for simile.

## Questions for AI

1. Is Djamela's social isolation — wanting in to the ghost-kids' world — shown through incident, or does the story still rely on Daudi telling us she envies them?
2. Does Daudi's habit of "arranging found things" (motherboard, hubcap, antelope skull) ever get named, even obliquely, as a coping mechanism for his own isolation — or does it read only as a character quirk?
3. Running the Metaphor Machine on Daudi's specific dread (not just entropy in the abstract) — what does the fear of having looked away *feel like, smell like, sound like* to him specifically, using his physics vocabulary?
