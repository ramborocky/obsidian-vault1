# CLELAND — SET THE STAGE

> The atmosphere here isn't like anywhere else.

## Character (or Plot) Dictates Locale — Never the Reverse

- A setting only feels earned when it flows from an established personality trait or situation. A gullible character has chance encounters at parties; an insecure one feels intimidated among genuine experts. Plant the trait *before* the scene needs it, so the reader's reaction is "of course," not "why is she there."
- You can also work backward: decide the plot event first (Cleland burns down a condo for a TRD), then retrofit a character reason for being there (the condo's owner gets sick; the protagonist visits with flowers).

## Less Is More

- Contemporary readers don't want lengthy geographic depiction — they want details that *could only occur at that place, at that time*, filtered through incident, not exposition.
- Let characters interact with the setting rather than describing it directly (Rex Stout's Archie Goodwin on the mountain ledge: we feel the cold and the fear through his wit, not through a weather report).

## Align Setting With Theme

- The strongest settings either mirror the theme (an isolated house mirroring a mother's total, isolating love) or contrast with it (a searing love affair conducted in a dingy prison visiting room) — both work; a setting with no relationship to theme is wasted real estate.
- Sensory specificity (sight, sound, smell, taste, touch) is what makes a setting suspenseful rather than merely described — see Figure 4.3's method: name what the character sees, hears, smells, tastes, touches, and feels physiologically and emotionally, in that scene, in that place.

## For Kalemie

- Kalemie already does the theme-alignment move correctly without needing to be told: Lake Tanganyika is both mirror and antagonist ("some women resemble the lake... it remembers everything") — the setting *is* the entropy motif, not backdrop for it. That's exactly Cleland's "setting as metaphor" principle already working.
- The colonial detail (rusted colonial ships, "like Leopold never wanting to leave Brazzaville," the Belgian consul-general lie) is doing double duty as both character-revealing (Jean Luc's claimed authority) and thematic (extraction/colonization echoing the extraction of Djamela herself) — Cleland's "opposites attract" setting principle applies directly: the ghost kids' clay-roofed mansions behind gates sit deliberately next to Daudi and Djamela's baked-brick, tin-roof world, and that contrast is the engine of Djamela's whole arc.
- Less-is-more check: the Craft Log already flagged Scene 2's neighbourhood paragraph as "scene-setting that hasn't quite found its voice" — this is precisely the failure mode Cleland warns against, description for its own sake rather than incident revealing setting. The dala dala scenes, by contrast, succeed because we get the setting entirely through action and dialogue (Morisho's patter, the boda boda catcalls, the SUV's tinted glass) — no paragraph stops to describe Kalemie as a place.
