# CLELAND — REVEAL ANSWERS SLOWLY

> If we knew all the answers, we'd be bored.

## Unreliable Narration Comes in Flavors

- **The Innocent**: unreliable through youth/inexperience, not deceit (Huck Finn misreading Tom Sawyer's schemes).
- **The Guilty**: actively concealing (Gone Girl, Murder of Roger Ackroyd).
- **The Biased/Stressed/Mentally Ill**: unreliable through a distorting internal state, not malice (The Girl on the Train's Rachel and Anna, each wrong for different reasons).
- Memory itself is unreliable by cognitive default — we *reconstruct*, not replay, so a character can insist on an inaccurate memory with total sincerity. That's not a plot hole; it's how memory actually works.

## Three Techniques to Set Up a Slow Reveal

1. **Statements with multiple meanings** — "he was such a nice, quiet young man" reads differently once you know what's coming.
2. **Statements open to interpretation** — deliberately ambiguous sentence structure a reader will resolve one way, then have to revise later.
3. **Statements that beg questions** — a detail dropped without explanation ("the makeup didn't quite cover Lydia's black eye") that a reader files away and waits on.

## Don't Slow the Pace, Slow the Reveal

- Action and dialogue keep pace brisk even while information is deliberately withheld — reflective exposition is what actually drags. The two (pace and reveal-speed) are independent dials, not the same dial.

## For Kalemie

- Daudi is a hybrid unreliable narrator by Cleland's own taxonomy: partly **the Innocent** (a boy whose physics vocabulary is a real limit on what he can interpret, not just a stylistic tic) and partly **the Biased/Stressed** (a retrospective narrator whose grief and guilt shape what he chooses to arrange and dwell on). Naming this hybrid status explicitly could resolve the Structural Notes' open question about how strong the retrospective voice should be — an Innocent-leaning narrator earns the reader's trust even while withholding; a Guilty-leaning one would need the withholding itself flagged as suspicious.
- The Option C opening is already executing "statements that beg questions" as a technique, at high density: the vaccination scar "not where they usually put them," the fob "shaped like a cartoon lion," the selfie with a stranger's ring-handed grip on her shoulder. Cleland's chapter confirms these are fair-play plants only if each is eventually, explicitly answered on the page — an audit against [[Know Your Readers]]'s Chekhov's-gun list applies directly here.
- Djamela's name-change to "Chantal" is a strong "statement with multiple meanings" candidate: on a first read it's a flirtation detail; on a second, it's the moment she steps into a different self entirely. The Craft Log's own AI question ("is this moment given enough weight, or does it go by too fast") is really asking whether this is currently functioning as Cleland's slow-reveal technique #1, or whether it's landing as a throwaway line.
