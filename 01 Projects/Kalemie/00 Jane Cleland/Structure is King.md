# CLELAND — STRUCTURE IS KING

> The only mistake you can make is having no structure at all.

## Two Reliable Options

- **Linear (chronological)**: clarity and simplicity. Use when the story's own timeline is inherently the point.
- **Nonlinear**: drama and complexity, but a real risk of confusion. Only choose it when it's harmonious with the underlying theme — not because it's "more interesting."

## Structure, POV, and Perspective Are Three Different Dials

- **Structure**: your organizational framework. **Point of View**: your voice (first/second/third/omniscient). **Perspective**: whose story is being told. Conflating them leads to muddled craft decisions.
- Single POV is the most intimate option and the standard for stories where the reader must experience everything the protagonist does, as it happens.
- Omniscient narration is tempting for its flexibility but comes with a trap: it makes *telling* easy and *showing* optional. Vigilance against that trap is the cost of using it.

## Enhancements That Tighten Structure

- **Bookends**: begin and end on the same motif — gives readers thematic symmetry and a sense of completion.
- **Categories**: organize content (places, people, objects) around your theme.
- **Flashbacks/flash-forwards**: control the flow of backstory by showing, not explaining it. Best practice: connect the end of the current-time scene to the beginning of the flashback so the time-shift feels seamless, not jarring. Flash-forwards intensify dread — the reader knows disaster looms before the character does.

## For Kalemie

- Kalemie is already using the most powerful nonlinear enhancement in the book: the flash-forward. The Option C opening ("That morning I knew something was wrong before I knew what it was") is a textbook flash-forward — Daudi (and the reader) knows Djamela is dead before the chronological scenes begin. Cleland's chapter confirms this is a legitimate, load-bearing structural choice, not a gimmick, *as long as* the chronological scenes that follow keep paying off the dread the opening promised.
- The single-POV, first-person structure is correct for this story's contract (per [[Know Your Readers]]) — the reader needs to be trapped inside Daudi's retrospective knowledge and his blind spots, not given an omniscient view of what Jean Luc or Morisho are actually doing.
- Bookend candidate already logged in the Craft Log: D'juma's lake speech ("some women resemble the lake... it remembers everything") appears at Scene 1 and is flagged as wanting to recur at the end. Cleland's bookend principle says: don't just repeat it — return to the *same motif* with the *reader's changed understanding*, so the second telling lands differently than the first.
