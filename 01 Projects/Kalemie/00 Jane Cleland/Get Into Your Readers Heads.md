# CLELAND — GET INTO YOUR READERS' HEADS

> Betrayal is the only truth that sticks.

## Readers Crave Stability; Craft Denies It to Them

- Per Maslow, safety/stability is a near-universal, near-biological need — right after physiology. Readers root for characters precisely because they want the disrupted order restored.
- The formula: create a protagonist readers care about, then force confusion and betrayal onto them. Readers won't put the book down until they see stability return (or understand why it can't).
- Confusion and betrayal aren't the same as TRDs (see [[Jane's Plotting Road Map]]) — they're the *underpinning* that makes TRDs matter emotionally. What counts as betrayal is entirely defined by a specific character's values, not a universal standard.

## Know Your Character Well Enough to Betray Them Specifically

- Cleland's Figure 9.1 questionnaire (occupation, key relationships, quirks, values, what confuses them, what they'd perceive as betrayal) exists to close "knowledge gaps" — the places where an author thinks they know a character but hasn't actually decided.
- The two most important final questions: *what is likely to confuse your character*, and *what will your character perceive as a betrayal* — these should be so specific to the individual that they couldn't be copy-pasted onto a different character in the same story.

## Write From Deep Understanding, Not Generality

- Vague, general character traits produce vague, general suspense. The more idiosyncratic and specific the character's fears/values, the more the plot's betrayals land as *personal*, not generic.

## For Kalemie

- Stability-vs-betrayal is Kalemie's whole shape at the macro level: Daudi's ordered, physics-explained world (found objects arranged like evidence, entropy as a governing law) is the stability; Djamela's death and his own complicity-by-silence is the betrayal that never resolves back to order. Cleland's chapter argues this isn't melodrama, it's the reader's most basic psychological hook — which validates keeping the story's ending honest (entropy *does* win) rather than manufacturing false closure.
- Running Cleland's Figure 9.1 questions against Daudi specifically (not "a grieving boy" generically): what confuses him is explicitly logged already — encountering Djamela's transformation into "Chantal," a version of femininity and ambition his physics vocabulary has no model for. What he'd perceive as betrayal is also already on the page but not named as such: D'juma's warning ("Keba") going unheeded, or his own silence being the thing he can't forgive in himself — Cleland's framework suggests naming this explicitly as *Daudi's specific betrayal* (of himself, not just of Djamela) would sharpen the ending.
- Djamela's own knowledge gaps are the Craft Log's own open question: "what does Djamela know that Daudi doesn't?" Cleland's method says close that gap deliberately — decide, even if it's never stated on the page, exactly what Djamela believes Jean Luc offers her and exactly what she'd call betrayal (the thinned envelope? her own mother's silence about "watermelon not cake"?) — because a reader can feel the difference between an author who has decided and an author who hasn't.

## Questions for AI

1. What specifically does Daudi consider his own betrayal — of Djamela, of himself, or both — and is that distinction currently legible on the page, or does the story leave it as generic grief?
2. Running Cleland's knowledge-gap questionnaire on Djamela specifically: what does she believe she's getting from Jean Luc's world, and at what exact point (if any) does the story show her realizing she's been betrayed by that belief?
3. Is there a moment where "stability" is restored falsely — a scene that resolves too neatly — that should instead honor Cleland's (and the draft's own) thesis that entropy wins?
