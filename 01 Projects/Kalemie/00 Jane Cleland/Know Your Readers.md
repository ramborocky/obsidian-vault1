# CLELAND — KNOW YOUR READERS

> Two features nearly every published book shares: it's simultaneously fresh and familiar, and it touches readers' hearts by making them feel characters' yearnings.

## The Genre Contract

- Every genre carries conventions readers expect *honored*, not merely met — the trick is to layer the unexpected onto the familiar, not replace the familiar with something unrecognizable.
- Literary fiction is character-driven (a transitional passage — coming-of-age, facing mortality — or a reflection on a global theme); popular fiction is plot-driven. Know which contract governs your story before you can knowingly bend it.
- Narrative nonfiction (memoir, literary journalism) uses fiction's suspense techniques to tell true stories — the craft toolkit is identical regardless of genre.

## The Narrative Question

- The overarching question posed early that must be answered by the end — a contract with the reader. Rarely phrased as an actual question; usually implicit in the situation you create.
- The sooner it's introduced, the better. Readers won't patiently wait through scene-setting for it to arrive.
- Follows Chekhov's-gun logic: don't raise a question you don't intend to answer, and don't hang a rifle on the wall you don't intend to fire.

## Analyze the Best of Your Genre

- Before plotting your own story, track protagonist motivation, antagonist motivation, narrative question, theme, setting, pace, structure, and suspense across six or more books you admire in your genre.
- This isn't imitation — it's calibration. It tells you what your specific readers already expect so you know exactly where you're allowed to surprise them.

## For Kalemie

- Kalemie's genre contract is literary short fiction aimed at the Commonwealth Short Story Prize — character-driven, retrospective, built around a transitional passage: Daudi losing Djamela, and with her, losing a version of himself who didn't look away. The "fresh and familiar" test: Commonwealth judges have rewarded specific, code-switched African fiction before (Emezi, Ilo, Ngamije, already logged in the Craft Log's Structural Notes) — that's the familiar half of the contract. The fresh half has to come from Daudi's physics-obsessed narrating voice, not from the premise of a girl's disappearance, which on its own is a familiar shape.
- The narrative question is already logged two ways in the Craft Log: the surface one (*who killed Djamela, will Daudi do what the police won't*) and the deeper one underneath (*did Daudi see the signs and look away*). Cleland's chapter is a direct instruction to pick one to foreground as the contract with the reader — a story that tries to honor both with equal weight risks answering neither cleanly.
- Chekhov's-gun audit: the Option C opening plants the vaccination scar, the lion-shaped fob/door card, the unnamed cologne, and the "#blessed" selfie as promises. Each is a narrative sub-question the reader now expects answered on the page — not resolved silently in Daudi's head between scenes.

## Questions for AI

1. Which narrative question is Kalemie actually answering by its last line — the whodunit, or the did-he-look-away question? Is the current draft honoring both equally, and if so, is that diluting either?
2. Does every planted object from the Option C opening (motherboard, vaccination scar, cologne, fob, selfie) get its answer delivered on the page before the story ends, or does any resolve only in summary?
