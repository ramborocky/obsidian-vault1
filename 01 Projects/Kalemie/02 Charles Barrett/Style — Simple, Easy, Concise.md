# BARRETT — STYLE

> Every sentence shall tell.

## The Three Words

**Simple. Easy. Concise.** And above all appropriate to the matter — Barrett's complaint about amateurs is that they treat serious subjects with unbecoming levity and dress commonplaces in absurdly ornate style.

## Against Fine Writing

The signature failure. *He sought the arms of Morpheus* for *he went to bed*; *he partook of an abundant repast* for *he breakfasted*. It comes from believing that common things are vulgar, and it is the worst possible way to handle commonplaces because **it attracts particular attention to the very things it is meant to hide.**

The related failure is the opposite one — hackneyed stock phrases, the ready-made garments of the mentally lazy. Barrett rates sensationalism above this, since it at least has vividness.

## How To Handle Commonplaces

The problem is real: life is full of them, literature will not tolerate them, and a story that omits them stops resembling life. His solution is proportion — **crowd them into the least possible space and couch them in ordinary language**, because thoughts rendered unusual by their expression become conspicuous.

By "ordinary language" he means simple, correct, rather colourless speech — not stock phrases.

## The Prohibitions

- **Poetry quoted or original** interlarded into prose or dialogue. Few people quote poetry in ordinary speech.
- **Figures of speech introduced for literary effect.** Not the figurative in general — literature and speech are full of it — but metaphor and simile deployed as ornament. Barrett calls them digressions, excrescences, beautiful in themselves and adding no beauty to the narrative.
- **Foreign words used to show cosmopolitanism.** "The cheapest sort of literary trick." But note his exemption: foreign expressions may be judiciously used **to give local colour, and are indispensable in the speech of certain character types.**
- **Exclamation points.** A row of them is a sign of amateurism. Punctuation draws attention to what already exists; it has no power to create interest.
- **Italics and capitals as emphasis.** They are signs of what has already been expressed, not the expression of a new force. A word needing emphasis should be *placed* so the reader involuntarily stresses it — and a word brought into notice that way far exceeds one owing its prominence to a change of type.

## Sentence Structure

The **periodic** style should be practically tabooed — heavy and retarding. The **very short** sentence is permitted only in moderation; excessive use gives a nervous jerky style, and Barrett names Stephen Crane as the awful example of the "bumpety-bump" method. Exclamatory and interrogative sentences should be totally eschewed.

His prescription: **the simple straightforward declarative sentence, rather loose, of medium length, tending to short at times to avoid monotony and give vividness.**

## Concise Is Not Exhaustive

The final and best distinction. Conciseness means cutting redundancy — often recasting a sentence rather than deleting a word — so the movement of the language matches the action of the plot.

But it must not become exhaustiveness. **It is bad policy to leave nothing to the imagination.** The average reader is fond of reading between the lines and prides himself on it; he is exasperated by a style that leaves him nothing to do, and delighted by expanding the sly hints a knowing writer throws out. He never notices he has fallen into a laid trap — he compliments the author and leaves pleased with them both.

## For Kalemie

**On measurement, both drafts pass Barrett's sentence prescription comfortably.** From [[Kalemie — Standing Diagnosis]]: the master draft averages 14.0 words with 42% of sentences under ten; "Chantal" averages 12.8 with 63% under ten.

But Barrett adds a constraint Cleland does not, and **"Chantal" is closer to failing it**. His warning against the "bumpety-bump" style — nervous, jerky, tiresome — is aimed exactly at a 63% short-sentence rate. Cleland says short sentences are for action and shock; Barrett says a diet of them becomes irritating. Both are right, and the Djamela draft should be read aloud with that specific question in mind.

**Fine writing: two live instances, both already flagged from other directions.**

- *"the bioluminescent stream of pure everything"* — the register spike. Barrett's mechanism explains *why* it fails: reaching for elevated language at the least controlled moment attracts attention to precisely what the prose is trying not to say.
- *désvu* — either a coinage that is not legible as one, or a typo, in italics, at the emotional peak.

**On foreign words, Barrett explicitly exempts Kalemie.** His exemption covers local colour and the speech of character types, which is exactly what the unitalicised Swahili, French and Lingala are. His condemnation is of the writer who sprinkles French for cosmopolitanism — and the draft has one borderline case worth examining: *désvu*, and Djamela's *"they call them anchovy,"* which is *about* that very affectation and therefore earns itself.

**On italics, the draft is a genuine offender.** Barrett's rule is that a word needing emphasis should be *placed* so the reader stresses it involuntarily. The draft uses italics for: internal monologue, Swahili and French dialogue, emphasis, and *désvu*. Four jobs, one typeface. **The dialogue italics in particular contradict the vault's own Writing Constitution**, which says local language is never italicised — yet the draft italicises *"Wamemchukua"* and *"Msichana gani?"* Barrett and the Constitution agree; the draft does not yet comply.

**Concise-not-exhaustive is where the draft is strongest.** The thin envelope leaves the reader everything to do. So does the withheld name. Barrett's account of the reader falling into a laid trap and complimenting the author is the exact mechanism [[The ELIZA Effect and the Vestments of Plausibility|Clark reaches through Weizenbaum]] eighty years later. **Two craft books a century apart describing the same reader.** Where the draft violates it is the places [[The Art of Omission — Unity, Padding, and Proportion|the omission note]] lists — the interpreted watermelon, the stated thesis.

## Questions for AI

1. Read "Chantal" aloud and mark every place the 63% short-sentence rate becomes bumpety-bump. Propose combinations.
2. Audit every use of italics in the master draft and sort by job. Bring dialogue italics in line with the Writing Constitution.
3. Find every metaphor introduced for effect rather than because the character would reach for it. Cut-test each against [[Character by Salient Detail]].
4. Which sentences would gain force from re-placing the emphatic word rather than italicising it?
5. Where does the prose leave the reader nothing to do? Quote the three worst, and say what to remove.
