# BARRETT — INEVITABLE BUT UNFORESEEN

> The ending must appear inevitable — but its inevitableness must not be apparent until the end has come.

## The Mechanism

**Suspense** is the reader's desire to know how it comes out — a phase of curiosity, and the thing that keeps most readers absorbed. But strained attention eventually collapses into indifference, so at some point curiosity must be gratified. That is where **surprise** enters.

Too long a strain is invariably fatal. Knowing when to relieve the tension is the whole skill.

## Where To Put The Relief — By Length

Barrett gives a rule that is unusually concrete:

- **Under 3,000 words** — no room and no need for preliminary surprise. Withhold every hint of the outcome until the climax itself.
- **Approaching or over 10,000 words** — there must be some lessening of tension before the climax.

His long example is James's *The Lesson of the Master* at 25,000 words, in six parts, each ending with a definite advance and a partial relief — and yet, so skilfully done that the whole retains unity of impression.

## The Preparation Rule

The climax must seem the **logical result** of events and characteristics already recited. Too startling, and the reader calls it unnatural; fiction licenses strange people and situations but demands plausibility.

The method: make the hints **progressive and culminative** — each adds to the reader's knowledge, but only when all of them converge at the climax is the mystery solved. And the crucial constraint on those hints:

> They must have influenced him when first he read them and prepared him for what was to come, **without his being conscious of their influence.**

The reader should be able to look *back* and pick out the preparatory touches. Never forward.

## The Two Novice Failures

1. **Giving it away by anticipatory side remarks.** Barrett invents the novice's version of Hawthorne — *Ah! could the unfortunate stranger but have guessed! / Poor girl! had she a premonition of her awful death?* — and notes that these are always irrelevant and obstructive, and that the reader much prefers to learn the outcome for himself. His rule: **silent grief is the keenest.**
2. **Making the outcome obvious from the start**, so there is no story to tell and a surprise is impossible. This one is radical — a conventional plot in conventional treatment. It cannot be repaired in that story, only prevented in the next.

The first fault is easy to fix: most side remarks can be cut bodily, and the necessary ones slurred so they prepare without revealing.

## The Demonstration

Barrett's best piece of practical criticism is his list of every buried warning in *The Ambitious Guest* — the dangerous spot where stones rumble at midnight, the wind that pauses and wails, "whose fate was linked with theirs," the father's wish for a farm "but not where they could tumble on our heads," the grandmother's grave-clothes. Sixteen touches across a short story, not one of them announcing itself, all converging on four words.

## For Kalemie

**Kalemie's suspense architecture is inverted relative to its length, and Barrett's rule identifies it precisely.**

At ~3,900 words the draft sits in his *under 3,000 / withhold everything* band or just above it. It should therefore be holding its outcome to the climax. Instead it **opens with the outcome**: Djamela is dead, Morisho is arrested. The retrospective frame gives away the ending on page one.

That is not automatically wrong — the retrospective frame is a legitimate modern form, and the withheld *name* generates real dread. But it changes what the story's suspense can be about, and the draft has not fully accepted the change. **With the outcome known, the only remaining suspense is: what did Daudi do, and what will he do now.** That question is not posed until very late. The reader spends the middle scenes with no live question in front of them, which is why the draft reads as a sequence of strong scenes rather than a mounting one.

**The fix is not to remove the frame.** It is to pose the real question early — Daudi's complicity — so the reader has something to be in suspense *about* while the flashback runs.

**On progressive and culminative hints, the draft is genuinely good** and better than it knows. Running the *Ambitious Guest* audit on Kalemie's opening yields a real list: the fingernails on and off; the vaccination scar in the wrong place; the cologne he cannot name; the lion fob; the ring at the edge of the frame; the name written in dust and erased. Six touches, none announcing itself, all converging. **That is Hawthorne's method executed correctly**, and it is the strongest evidence in the vault that this writer can do the hard version.

**Where the anticipatory side remark appears.** Barrett's invented bad-Hawthorne lines have two real counterparts:

- *"I had yet to experience how a woman would pull you in like the lake"* — retrospective knowledge, announcing what is coming. The draft's own callout asks whether it belongs here or at the end. Barrett's answer: cut it, or move it past the event.
- *"To me it felt as though it was Djamela's father's desperate attempt to stave off the inevitable rot of the whole situation"* — the narrator interpreting the watermelon for us. **Silent grief is the keenest**; the watermelon preserving itself in sugar already carries this.
- *"If you stare long enough, you can see your own death"* at age six or seven — a genuine prophecy hint. It works, but it is the draft's least deniable one, and the callout's own worry about Djamela reading as prophecy rather than person is exactly right.

**The relationship to "Chantal."** [[2026-07-26 — Chantal (Djamela POV Draft)|The Djamela draft]] solves the suspense problem differently — the narrator's death is known to the reader of Kalemie but never stated in her own text, so her every forward-looking sentence carries dread she cannot feel. **Its hints are progressive and culminative by construction**, because she is laying down evidence she does not know is evidence. That is the technique working at full strength.
