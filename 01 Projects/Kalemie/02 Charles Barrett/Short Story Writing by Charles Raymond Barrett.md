# BARRETT KNOWLEDGE BASE — MAP OF CONTENT

*Charles Raymond Barrett, PhB., **Short Story Writing: A Practical Treatise on the Art of the Short Story** (1900). The oldest book in the vault and the only one written about the short story specifically — McKee is screenwriting, Cleland is suspense across forms, Gardner is fiction in general. Barrett assumes from his first page that the form you are writing is the form you are submitting. Compact, application-focused notes, each with a "For Kalemie" section and AI prompts.*

## Part One — What The Form Is

- [[The Isolation Principle — One Emotion, One Incident]] — a short story is not a novel in a nutshell; it magnifies one thing and excludes everything else *(ch I)*
- [[The Balance Test — Length as a Function of Idea]] — weigh the idea against the words; when the beam rises, stop *(ch I)*
- [[The Unities in Miniature]] — single person, single action, single place, single time; no subplot, ever *(ch III)*

## Part Two — Before You Write

- [[The Plot-Germ and the One-Sentence Test]] — if the plot won't compress to one sentence, something is radically wrong *(ch III)*
- [[The Working Plot — The Skeleton Method]] — one line per important idea, so you can judge the story coldly *(ch III)*
- [[Titles Good and Bad]] — apt, specific, attractive, new, short; the title comes *before* the story *(ch IV)*
- [[Verisimilitude, Not Verity — The Use of Facts]] — facts are crude material; the Willey House case study *(ch V)*

## Part Three — Writing It

- [[Character by Salient Detail]] — the hypernatural; build a whole person on one characteristic *(ch VI)*
- [[Methods of Narration — The Author Must Vanish]] — the case against first person, and the Punch-and-Judy rule *(ch VII)*
- [[The Beginning — Three Paragraphs at Most]] — the editor reads one page of three MSS in four *(ch VIII)*
- [[The Art of Omission — Unity, Padding, and Proportion]] — no chapters, no stars, no moralising, no local colour for its own sake *(ch IX)*
- [[Inevitable but Unforeseen — Suspense and Surprise]] — the ending must be inevitable, and its inevitability invisible until it arrives *(ch IX)*
- [[Climax and Conclusion — The Story in Six Words]] — the climax *is* the story; end as near to it as you can *(ch X)*
- [[Style — Simple, Easy, Concise]] — against fine writing, italics-as-force, and the exclamation point *(ch XI)*

## What This Base Is For

Every note follows the same shape as the McKee, Cleland and Gardner notes: Barrett's principle in compact form → a **For Kalemie** section applying it to Daudi, Djamela, Jean Luc, D'juma and Morisho and the live draft → **Questions for AI** to run against a scene.

**Where Barrett sits among the others.** McKee builds the structure. Cleland wires the scene for the reader inside it. Gardner asks why any of it works. Barrett does something none of them do: he treats the short story as *a thing with edges* — a form defined by what it must exclude. His entire book is an argument from scarcity. You have three to five thousand words; therefore one incident, one place, one span of time, one predominant emotion, two speaking characters at most, no subplot, no chapter breaks, no authorial opinion, and an ending that arrives within a paragraph of the climax. Where McKee tells you what to build, Barrett tells you what you cannot afford.

That makes him the most immediately useful of the four for a story with a **hard 5,000-word ceiling**.

**Three places he is uncannily current.** He states the [[The First-Page Cut|first-page cut]] in 1900 in almost the words the Commonwealth panel uses now — the editor reads no more than the first page of three manuscripts in four. He gives the ideal length as 3,000–5,000 words, which is the prize's window almost exactly. And his account of leaving room for the reader to "expand the sly hints" is the same mechanism Clark reaches through Iser and Weizenbaum eighty years later — see [[The ELIZA Effect and the Vestments of Plausibility]].

**Three places he is a man of 1900, and should be overruled.** He is dismissive of dialect writing in terms that read today as straightforwardly wrong, and that would, applied literally, gut Kalemie's unitalicised Swahili, French and Lingala — ignore him completely here; [[Obfuscated Openness — Detail as Disguise|the code-switching is an asset]]. He has a passage about "the inexperienced writer of the gentler sex" and her weddings that is period sexism and nothing else. And he insists the short story's proper object is "to amuse" and that it "usually treats of the lighter and brighter side of life" — a claim the last century of the form has simply disproved. Take his structural rigour; leave his taste.

Chapters not given their own note: **II — Short Stories Classified** (a taxonomy of story types, of historical interest), **XII — The Labor of Authorship** (revision habits, largely absorbed into [[The Five Passes]]), and **XIII — The Quest of a Market** (1900 magazine submission practice, entirely obsolete — use [[Collection Tracker]] instead).
