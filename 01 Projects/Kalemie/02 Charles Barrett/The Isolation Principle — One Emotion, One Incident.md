# BARRETT — THE ISOLATION PRINCIPLE

> One thing a short story can never be: a novel in a nutshell.

## The Definition

A short prose narrative presenting a bit of real life artistically. It must **progress constantly, arrive somewhere, and enforce some idea** — and leave the reader with a definite impression. Barrett is blunt that the first requisite is having a story to tell at all: word-pictures and pretty scenes are not a story, and "still life" is outside the form's province.

## What Actually Separates It From The Novel

Not length. **Isolation and magnification.**

The novel reproduces a period of real life: many characters, varied scenes, and things present purely because such trivia are part of life. The short story takes a single person or incident out of the weave and treats it as if it were complete in itself.

Barrett's permitted allowance, stated flatly:

- **one scene and incident**
- **one or two real characters** — often only one with a speaking part
- **one predominant emotion**

Everything else is a detriment to the interest and success of the story. Not a weakness — a detriment.

## The Admission That Makes It Honest

He then concedes the form is **artificial and to a considerable degree unnatural**. Isolation is not known to nature; there, everything works on everything else. The short story's central convention is a lie about how life is organised.

The compensation is that if the characters are handled well, the reader still feels they were alive before the story started and will go on after it — we have simply come into contact with them briefly. **The isolation is in the frame, not in the people.**

## The Test

For any element in the draft: *is this here because the story requires it, or because life would contain it?* The second reason is a novel's reason. It is not available to you.

## For Kalemie

**Kalemie fails this on its face, and that is the most useful thing Barrett has to say to it.**

Count what the master draft carries: seven scenes across months, at least four locations (the stop, the dala dala, Daudi's room, the police station, plus the porch and the birthday), and named characters with speaking parts — Daudi, Djamela, Jean Luc, Morisho, D'juma, Djamela's mother, Anitha, two policemen, a woman in a black dress. Barrett's ceiling is *two*.

That is a novel's inventory in a short story's word budget, and it explains a fault [[Kalemie — Standing Diagnosis|the diagnosis]] found from a different direction: the draft is at 3,941 words with no ending, because the material is being asked to cover ground it has no room for.

**The one predominant emotion is the rule to actually apply.** Name Kalemie's, in one word, and then audit each scene against it. Candidates the draft supports: *complicity* (the Controlling Idea), *helplessness*, or — the one the prose keeps reaching for — **the grief of having watched**. They are not the same emotion, and scenes currently serve different ones. Scene 3's ghost-kid class satire serves resentment. Scene 4's watermelon irony serves tenderness-turning-to-dread. Scene 7 serves institutional rage. Each is good; they do not stack into a single impression.

**Where Kalemie already obeys him.** The opening is a textbook isolation: one morning, one location, one incident, the whole of a life implied at its edges. That is exactly the effect Barrett describes when he says a well-handled character feels like they were living before we arrived. The draft's best writing is its most Barrett-compliant writing, which is worth noticing.

**Where "Chantal" does better and worse.** [[2026-07-26 — Chantal (Djamela POV Draft)|The Djamela draft]] has a single predominant emotion throughout — the dread of being counted and coming up short — and that is why it reads as more unified. But it covers *more* ground, not less: a year, six locations, and a narrator who reports on everyone. It buys unity with voice rather than with isolation, which is a modern solution Barrett would not have accepted and which works anyway.

**The concrete instruction:** pick the one emotion, then cut or compress every scene that primarily serves a different one. Given the [[The Balance Test — Length as a Function of Idea|word ceiling]], this is not an aesthetic preference — it is how the ending gets its 400 words.

## Questions for AI

1. State Kalemie's one predominant emotion in a single word. Then, scene by scene, name the emotion that scene actually serves. Report every mismatch.
2. Count the characters with speaking parts in the master draft. For each beyond the first two, state what the story loses if they are silenced and reported instead.
3. Which single scene, if cut entirely, costs the story least? Answer even if the answer is uncomfortable.
4. Test the opening against Barrett's isolation standard — one scene, one incident, one emotion. Does it pass? Does any other scene?
5. Is anything in the draft present because life would contain it rather than because the story requires it? Quote each instance.
