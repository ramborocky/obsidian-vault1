# BARRETT — THE WORKING PLOT

> It enables you to consider your work with a coolness and impartiality which were impossible while it was still in embryo in your brain.

## The Method

Think the story through as conceived, and set down on paper **one line for every important idea**, in logical order.

The lines roughly correspond to paragraphs — imperfectly, since a descriptive paragraph may need more than one line and a dozen paragraphs of dialogue may need only one. Barrett prints the full working plot of *The Ambitious Guest*: forty-four numbered paragraph groups, each a single line, from *the scene is a tavern at the Notch* to *even the death of the ambitious guest is in doubt.*

Critically, **the working plot omits all the artistic touches** — they are ruthlessly shorn away, because they belong to the treatment, not the plot.

## Why It Works

Four claims, and the third is the real one:

1. It crystallises phantasms of the brain into ideas.
2. It arranges thoughts in proper order.
3. **It makes the work objective enough to consider coldly** — impossible while it lives only in your head.
4. It lets you condense or expand with a ready comprehension of the effect on the story's proportions.

And the blunt payoff: *it often reveals the absurdity or impossibility of a plan which had seemed to you most happy.*

## When To Use It

Barrett is honest that many short story writers waste no time on preparation and set the story down complete — and says **that is the ideal method**, more likely to produce something spontaneous and technically correct.

The skeleton is for when the story is *not* well defined in your mind, or when the plot has some complexity. It may serve as a frame to build on, or merely as a reference for the logical order of events. Usually you will forget the scheme in the absorption of composition — but having properly arranged your ideas will assist you materially, if unconsciously.

## For Kalemie

**Kalemie has a working plot already, and it is one of the better ones in this vault — but it is not Barrett's kind, and the difference matters.**

The [[Scenario — Arcs, Beats & Scenes|Scenario file]]'s McKee beat sheet gives, per scene: the beat, the Gap, and a Cleland note. That is a *diagnostic* table — it says how each scene should function. Barrett's skeleton is different and simpler: **one line per important idea, in order, with all artistry stripped.** It says what happens, and nothing about why.

The reason to build the Barrett version as well is his third claim. A beat sheet organised by function still lets you admire your own intentions. A bare list of events does not. **It is the closest thing available to reading your own story as a stranger** — which is why it so often reveals the absurdity of a plan that seemed happy.

**What a Barrett skeleton of Kalemie would immediately expose.** Stripped of the prose, the event list reads roughly:

```
Daudi arrives at the stop. Something is wrong.
Morisho has been arrested for a girl's death.
Daudi lists what he knows about her.
[D'juma once said women resemble the lake.]        ← already stated above
D'juma says women resemble the lake.               ← the double start, visible
The neighbourhood is described.                    ← no event
Djamela is described.                              ← no event
On the dala dala: Morisho, Anitha, the ghost kids.
Djamela turns thirteen. She wishes to be Kim Kardashian.
Jean Luc stops his car. He renames her Chantal.
It rains. Daudi says nothing. She kisses him.
At the station she reveals the pregnancy.
Jean Luc arrives with an envelope. It leaves thinner.
[nothing]                                          ← the missing ending
```

Every structural fault [[Kalemie — Standing Diagnosis|the diagnosis]] took measurement to find is visible in fourteen lines, unaided. The repeated lake line. Two consecutive entries with no event in them. A blank where the climax should be. **That is the method working exactly as advertised**, and it costs twenty minutes.

**The instruction:** before the next revision session, write the skeleton by hand — one line per event, no adjectives, no craft annotations, no Gap column. Then read it as a list of things that happen to a girl and a boy. Any line you cannot justify as an *event* is a scene that has not yet earned its words. See [[The Balance Test — Length as a Function of Idea]].

**One caution.** Barrett prefers writing the story straight through when it is clear in your mind, and warns the skeleton is a tool for when it is not. Kalemie's problem has never been that the writing is unclear — the prose is the strongest thing about it. **Use the skeleton for structure only, and do not let it become another document to maintain instead of writing the ending.** The vault currently holds a Dashboard, a Scenario, a Craft Log, a Collection Tracker and five knowledge bases, and one unwritten conclusion.

## Questions for AI

1. Produce a Barrett skeleton of the master draft — one line per event, no craft notes, no adjectives. Number the lines.
2. Mark every line that is not an event. Those are the scenes to merge or cut.
3. Do the same for "Chantal" and compare the two skeletons side by side. Which reads as a story and which as a sequence?
4. Write the skeleton the story *should* have, then diff it against the one it does have.
5. From the skeleton alone, with no prose visible, would you want to read this story? Answer as a stranger.
