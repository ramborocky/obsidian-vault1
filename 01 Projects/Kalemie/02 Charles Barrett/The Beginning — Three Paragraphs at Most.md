# BARRETT — THE BEGINNING

> Of three-fourths of the manuscripts submitted to him, the editor seldom reads more than the first page.

## The Rule

**Two or three paragraphs. Often one.**

And the diagnostic that follows it, which is the valuable half: if the story cannot reach the story proper in that space, *something is radically wrong — probably in the plot.* A sprawling opening is rarely an opening problem. It is a conception problem surfacing at the only place it can.

## Foundation Facts

Every story has facts the reader must hold to follow it: time, scene, names, relationships, prior events that bear on what follows. Barrett's rules for them:

- **Select ruthlessly.** Only facts with a definite influence that requires knowing them.
- **Any of them may be deferred** — introduced later when their need appears, or withheld entirely to build suspense or mystery.
- **They must never be listed.** Not "ticketed like the dramatis personae of a play bill." They must be introduced so deftly the reader takes them in involuntarily, as part of the warp and woof.

His illustration is the whole lesson in two lines. Not:

> This family was happy, and comprised father, mother, grandmother, daughter of seventeen, and younger children.

But Hawthorne:

> The faces of the father and mother had a sober gladness; the children laughed. The eldest daughter was the image of Happiness at seventeen, and the aged grandmother, who was knitting in the warmest place, was the image of Happiness grown old.

Same information. One is a manifest; the other is a room.

## The Failure Modes

- **The glittering generality** — opening with a paragraph of philosophising about love or fate before anything happens. Barrett concedes Poe and Kipling do it and says plainly that in nearly every case the story would improve if it were cut.
- **Beginning in the middle and going both ways at once** — the moment the writer says *but first let me explain how all this came about*, the reader begins to yawn, and the charm of the opening is lost in the dreariness of the explanation.
- **The dilatory opening** — Irving spends on an introduction what a modern writer would spend on a whole story. Barrett doesn't call it a fault in Irving; he calls it a fault of Irving's period, and says do not imitate it.
- **The catalogue of charms** — the heroine's hair, eyes, lips and teeth inventoried before she has done anything.

## Setting

**Background is the last thing to choose and the first to consider when writing out.** A scene earns its place only if it influences the action or is needed to understand what follows — "no matter how great may be its beauties or how artistic your description of them."

The method: suggest the scene by the few specific features that distinguish it from other similar scenes, and let the reader's imagination supply the rest. Barrett notes that from Hawthorne's description no two readers would draw the same picture, yet all would possess the same essentials — loneliness, bleakness, haunting terror.

And: **specificness in minor details is a great aid to vividness.** Never write "the village of Z—, in the year 18—."

## For Kalemie

**The good news first: Kalemie's opening buys its page, and buys it in Barrett's own currency.** *The founder of dead things.* The crowd with its back turned. The boy who will not meet Daudi's eyes. Foundation facts delivered involuntarily — we learn the town, the boy's reputation, the social order and the fact of a death without a single sentence of exposition. This is the Hawthorne method executed correctly, and it should not be touched.

**Then it breaks three of Barrett's rules in the next two pages.**

**1. It is longer than three paragraphs, and the overrun is diagnostic.** The opening runs to roughly twenty paragraphs before Scene 1 begins, and Scene 1 then restarts the story on D'juma's lake speech — the double start [[Kalemie — Standing Diagnosis|the diagnosis]] identifies. Barrett's reading of an overlong opening is that the trouble is *probably in the plot*, and here he is right: the draft opens twice because it has not decided whether the story is the investigation or the memory.

**2. The first sentence is a glittering generality.** *"Port de Kalemie. The air thickly filled with an infusion of colonial coffee and rotten carcasses hidden under the guise of mineral mines, full of corpses aged twelve to seventeen, yet not much younger than me."* This asks the reader to hold a thesis about extraction before meeting a person. Barrett on Poe applies exactly: in nearly every case the beginning could be cut and the story improved. **The story's real first line is its second**, and cutting to it costs nothing.

**3. It contains the catalogue Barrett warns against** — though in a form he would have admired. The list of clues (fingernails, vaccination scar, cologne, fob, selfie) is a catalogue of a person, but it is *doing character work*: it shows Daudi managing grief by inventory. That is a legitimate modern subversion. What is not legitimate is that six of the seven items are evidentiary — see [[All the Small Things — Grief Through the Minor Object]].

**On setting, Kalemie is exemplary and should be left alone.** Ave de Industrielle, Rue Saint Michel, Mihogoni, the Ecole Secondaire de Kitumaini, St. Augustine Parish along Ave Kabanyo Mute. Barrett's rule about never writing "the village of Z—" is the rule this draft obeys most confidently, and it is a large part of why the world reads as real. See [[Verisimilitude, Not Verity — The Use of Facts]].

**The one-line instruction:** delete the first sentence, delete Scene 1's restatement of the lake speech, and the opening becomes Barrett-compliant at no cost in words — in fact at a gain of roughly 250, which is most of the [[The Balance Test — Length as a Function of Idea|ending's budget]].

## Questions for AI

1. Count the paragraphs before the story proper begins. Where would Barrett say the beginning ends, and where does it actually end?
2. Test the draft opening with its first sentence deleted, so it begins on *"In our neighbourhood they called me the founder of dead things."* What is genuinely lost?
3. List every foundation fact the opening delivers. Mark each as involuntary (woven in) or ticketed (stated). Rewrite the ticketed ones.
4. Which settings in the draft influence the action, and which are only described? Cut-test the second group.
5. Apply the same audit to "Chantal" — does its opening reach the story proper within three paragraphs?
