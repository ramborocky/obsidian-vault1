# BARRETT — METHODS OF NARRATION

> The author may stand behind the scenes and work the puppets, but his hand must be unseen, his voice disguised, his personality imperceptible. No one cares for the man who makes the Punch and Judy show.

## The Governing Rule

Suitability, and suitability means **naturalness**. Story writing is a modern phase of the world-old custom of story *telling*; the printed page should sit as easily on the eye as a voice on the ear. Barrett's model is a grandmother telling children a story at twilight — no striving after effect.

## The Novice's Core Difficulty

Subordinating his own personality. He knows he must individualise the story by putting something of himself into it, and does not understand that **only his spirit is wanted; his body is in the way.**

Hence: no side remarks, no criticisms, no moralising, no *dear reader*. In the short story no personal confidences, moralising comments, or confessions are allowed at all. If you must express opinions, write essays.

## The Case Against First Person

Barrett does not forbid it — he notes masters have used it — but lists four real costs:

1. **The narrator gets between the story and the reader.** The one who "I's" his way through tends to become an important fellow who intrudes on private scenes and prefers philosophising to advancing the plot.
2. **Knowledge strain.** The reader wonders how one man knew so much. We grant omniscience to the invisible third person without question; we do not grant it to a participant.
3. **The modesty trap.** If the narrator honestly professes limited knowledge, he becomes a dull fellow relating a story whose essential parts he missed.
4. **The survival problem — the sharpest one.** *However harrowing the experiences recounted, the narrator has come safely through.* The narrative therefore lacks the anxiety for the hero's welfare that is a large part of the pleasure of fiction.

He also quotes a critic's verdict that first person delivers a flattering full-length portrait of the hero with no inconvenient shadows, plus an idealised pastel of the woman he admired and a few heads-and-shoulders of everyone else.

Diary and epistolary forms carry all these costs and add their own. Narration-within-narration — the framing device where a captain is invited to tell his tale — is worse: *why not give the tale direct, in the character of the old farmer? There is no law against that.*

## His Recommendation

> Tell the story in the third person, as a passive observer; make the characters active and conversational; permit nothing, not even your own personality, to get between the reader and the story.

## For Kalemie

**Both drafts are first person, so this is the note that most directly challenges the project — and one of Barrett's four objections lands hard while another is defeated outright.**

**Objection 4 lands on Kalemie.** Daudi survives. He is telling this in retrospect, safely, from after. By Barrett's account that drains the anxiety — and it is a real diagnosis of a real symptom, because it is exactly why [[Kalemie — Standing Diagnosis|the diagnosis]] finds the tension sitting in the *past* rather than the present. The reader is never worried about Daudi. The compensating move the draft makes is to transfer the anxiety to Djamela, who does not survive — which works, but means the narrator is structurally a spectator, which loops back to the passive-protagonist problem the Dashboard has flagged since July.

**Objection 4 is defeated by "Chantal."** [[2026-07-26 — Chantal (Djamela POV Draft)|The Djamela draft]] puts the story in the mouth of the person who dies. Barrett's objection assumes the first-person narrator came safely through; here she did not, and the reader knows it, and every sentence is therefore loaded with a dread the Daudi version cannot generate. **That is a genuine formal advantage, not a stylistic preference** — and it is the strongest structural argument for the Djamela draft that exists anywhere in this vault.

**Objection 1 is a live risk in "Chantal" specifically.** Djamela philosophises. *It was not shame. It was arithmetic.* *I had the mathematics right, I had only got the sign wrong.* Barrett's warning about the narrator who prefers moralising to advancing the plot is precisely the fault [[The Funhouse Mirror — AI Output as Cliché Detector|the funhouse mirror]] flags in that draft from a different direction. Two independent tests, same sentences.

**Objection 2 is live in Kalemie.** Daudi reports Djamela's interiority, the policemen's motives, and Jean Luc's calculations. He was present for the police station, but not for what Djamela thought on the porch. Audit for knowledge he cannot have.

**Where Barrett should be overruled.** His blanket preference for third person is a convention of 1900, and the modern short story — including most Commonwealth-shortlisted work — is overwhelmingly first person. Do not switch POV on his authority. **Use his four objections as a checklist, not his conclusion as an instruction.**

**One thing to adopt without reservation.** *Permit nothing, not even your own personality, to get between the reader and the story.* Neither draft has a *dear reader* problem — but "Chantal" does have a narrator who occasionally addresses an implied tribunal (*I want that written down somewhere*). That is a strong effect used twice and a mannerism used five times. Count them.
