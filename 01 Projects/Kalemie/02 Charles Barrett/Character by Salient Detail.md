# BARRETT — CHARACTER BY SALIENT DETAIL

> A character must be exaggerated to appear natural.

## The Paradox

A real person has too much of the commonplace about him to serve in fiction. A character on the page is "a blur of hieroglyphics" — so description, action, speech and thought must be made **unnaturally striking** in order to produce, through sight alone, the effect that meeting the actual person would produce.

The word Barrett borrows for the result is **hypernatural**. Dickens intensified to the verge of the impossible and we never feel Micawber is unnatural. Art is so inferior to nature that to make the same impression it must deepen colours, heighten contrasts, omit here and exaggerate there.

The most convincing characters are usually **composites** — the striking traits of several real people, filled out by imagination. Which is why readers so often recognise "someone they know" in an invented figure.

## Against The Catalogue

The novice's error is telling everything and leaving nothing. The result is a multiplicity of detail that verges on baldness, arranged with no regard for relative value — "a mere catalogue of personal charms." Barrett prints three examples, and his point is that in every one, nothing distinguishes the person described from scores of others with the same general traits.

The method instead:

> Select and present only those salient details which body forth a vague image possessing a definite personality — to which the reader may give such distinctness as his imagination imparts.

**Build a complete character upon a single characteristic.** His examples do not really describe anyone: Irving's Ichabod is a scarecrow eloped from a cornfield; Dickens's Scrooge is a catalogue not of features but of *temperature*, a man who iced his office in the dog days and didn't thaw one degree at Christmas. The author gives the **sensations the character produced on observers**, and so produces the same sensation in the reader.

## Once Introduced, Let Them Work

Characteristics must not be listed like invoices. Characters must display their psychology through speech and action; their words must seem the result of mental processes. The better of his two methods is not mind-reading but **showing the person actuated by their dominant quality** — Poe builds an entire story on a man temporarily mastered by revenge.

## The Numbers

Speaking parts: **in general only two, frequently one.** Others are supernumeraries without form or influence. And the characters must be **unnaturally isolated — little past, less future, strangely lacking in relatives** — because a few thousand words cannot carry ancestry and family. Any history that bears on the story should arrive by subtle touches, preferably inside dialogue, so the reader takes it without noticing.

## On Dialogue

The short story's dramatic trend means action should be advanced by speech, and a short story should be full of talk of the proper kind. But: **people do not talk by book.** Reproduce colloquial speech — poor grammar, slang, incomplete sentences — then *edit* it, choosing the characteristic idioms.

Two failures he illustrates at length: the **bookish** (painfully correct, laboriously profound, exquisite only as burlesque), and the **commonplace** (dialogue interesting to the speakers and to nobody else). And the worst — characters who suffer dreadful afflictions and go on speaking in platitudes "like the poor sawdust dolls that they are."

**Ignore his chapter on dialect entirely.** His claim that dialect writing is inartistic, degenerate and a passing fad is period prejudice, wrong then and wrong now.

## For Kalemie

**The two-speaking-parts rule is broken and probably should be** — but the *isolation* rule is being broken accidentally, and that is fixable.

Kalemie carries Daudi, Djamela, Jean Luc, Morisho, D'juma, Anitha, Djamela's mother and two policemen with speech. Barrett's ceiling is two. Modern practice is looser, so do not cut on his authority — but note which characters currently have **past and relatives** attached: D'juma has a trade, a second trade, a wife and a cosmology; Morisho has a dropout history and a moral position. Both are supernumeraries by function and principals by treatment, and that mismatch is a real part of why the draft is over-full. See [[The Isolation Principle — One Emotion, One Incident]].

**The single-characteristic test, applied honestly:**

| Character | The one characteristic | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daudi | He keeps broken things | **Strong** — every scene expresses it, including his silences |
| Jean Luc | A window that goes down with a swish | **Strong** — sound, cologne, envelope; all one gesture of frictionless money |
| Djamela | She counts | **Strong in "Chantal", weak in Kalemie** — from outside she is mostly want |
| D'juma | He speaks in parables to avoid saying he is afraid | **Strong in the Scenario, absent from the draft** |
| Morisho | Small kindnesses that balance nothing | **Present but unexploited** |

**The catalogue warning has one live instance.** Djamela's first physical description — *tall and beautiful just like her mother* — is exactly the failure Barrett prints three examples of: nothing distinguishing her from scores of others. The draft's own callout already asks what Daudi is actually seeing. Barrett's answer: give the *sensation she produces*, not her features. The draft proves it can do this — the polished-Friday, removed-Monday fingernails do more characterising than any adjective in the story.

**On dialogue, the draft is in good shape and one scene is not.** Morisho's mia tano mia tano, Jean Luc's mixed French and Lingala, Djamela's "they call them anchovy" — all characteristic idiom, edited, doing class work. The exception is the police station's older officer, who recites the penal code "as though reading from a pre-written script." That is deliberate and it works. But the *younger* officer's "if you understand me" is the story's finest piece of dialogue, and it is worth noticing why: he never says the thing. Barrett's rule about letting characters be actuated by a dominant quality, executed perfectly.

**Where the sawdust-doll warning applies.** Daudi does not react to Djamela's pregnancy disclosure. The draft's own question asks what he feels and does not say. Barrett's version is harsher: a character who receives devastating news and produces no interior movement reads as a doll. **This is a missing beat, not a restraint.**

## Questions for AI

1. Name the single characteristic each character is built on. Flag any character where you cannot find one — that is a supernumerary being treated as a principal.
2. Rewrite Djamela's first description so it gives the sensation she produces rather than her features. Under 25 words.
3. Which characters carry past and relatives they do not need? List what could be cut without loss.
4. Find every line of dialogue that could be spoken by any character in the story. Those are the sawdust lines.
5. Draft Daudi's unspoken reaction to the pregnancy disclosure — physical only, no interior statement, under 40 words.
