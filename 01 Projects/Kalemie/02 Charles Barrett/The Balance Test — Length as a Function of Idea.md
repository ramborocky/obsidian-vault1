# BARRETT — THE BALANCE TEST

> Put the idea in one pan of a balance. Deal words into the other — five hundred, a thousand, two thousand — and when the beam carrying the idea rises, the story is finished. A word more or less is false work.

## The Principle

Length is not a target you hit. It is a **property of the idea**, discovered by weighing.

Barrett gives working numbers — a short story should rarely exceed 10,000 words or fall below 1,000, and **3,000 to 5,000 is the most usual length** — but immediately subordinates them: length is determined by the theme, not by an arbitrary limit. Every plot requires a certain number of words for proper elaboration, and neither more nor less will do.

The phrase to keep is **false work**. Padding is not merely waste; it is a lie about how much the idea weighs.

## The Corollary Nobody Likes

If a story keeps overrunning its budget, the fault is usually not in the prose. **It is in the idea.** An idea that cannot be elaborated inside five thousand words is a different idea — a novella's or a novel's — and no amount of tightening converts it. Barrett applies this diagnostically throughout: when a beginning sprawls, he says the trouble is "probably in the plot"; when a story needs chapter divisions, he says the plot belongs to a larger form.

Overlength is a symptom. Treat it as one.

## The Editor's Version

Barrett quotes a letter a young writer received, and it is the whole principle stated in money:

> We will pay $100 for your story as it is. If you can reduce it a third, $150; if a half, $200.

## For Kalemie

**This is the note that turns the prize ceiling from a constraint into an instrument.**

The Commonwealth Short Story Prize accepts **2,000–5,000 words**. Barrett's own ideal band — 3,000–5,000 — sits inside it almost exactly, which is a genuinely useful coincidence: a writer in 1900 and a prize panel in 2026 independently arrived at the same window as the natural size of the form.

The live numbers, from [[Kalemie — Standing Diagnosis]]:

```
Master draft prose        3,941 words   (no ending)
"Chantal"                 4,099 words   (complete)
Ceiling                   5,000 words
```

**Run the balance test on both.** The master draft has an idea in one pan and 3,941 words in the other, and *the beam has not risen* — the story has not arrived anywhere, because the ending does not exist. That is not a story that is nearly finished. By Barrett's test it is a story whose weight is still unknown.

"Chantal" at 4,099 words *has* arrived. The beam is up. Which means the honest reading is: **the Djamela draft is a finished object and the Daudi draft is not**, and the difference is roughly 400 words of ending, not 400 words of polish.

**The diagnostic use.** [[Dashboard|The Dashboard]] schedules seven scenes at 400+600+500+600+500+700+400 ≈ 3,700 words, plus the [[Scenario — Arcs, Beats & Scenes|Scenario]]'s new Djamela fragment at ~350 and its ending at ~140. That totals **~4,190** — which fits. But it fits only if every scene lands on target, and the Scenario's own budget note reaches ~3,790 by a different route. Two estimates, 400 words apart, neither tracked against the actual file.

**Barrett's instruction is to stop estimating and start weighing.** Re-measure after every session, and when a scene overruns its allocation, do not first try to tighten the prose — first ask whether that scene's *idea* is the right size for this story. Scene 2, which [[Dashboard|the Dashboard]] already wants merged, is the test case: it overran because it has no incident in it, not because it is verbose.

**One caution against Barrett.** His numbers are a floor and ceiling for the form, not a target for a submission. Do not pad toward 5,000 because there is room. A 4,100-word story that has arrived beats a 4,900-word story that is still travelling — and judges read the second as the first with padding.

1. Weigh the master draft: has the beam risen? Name precisely what the story still owes the reader before it can be said to have arrived.
2. For each scene, state its current word count and the idea it carries. Flag any scene where the words materially exceed the idea's weight.
3. Reconcile the Dashboard's budget (~4,190) with the Scenario's (~3,790). Which scenes are estimated differently, and which estimate is right?
4. Apply the editor's letter to "Chantal": what would a one-third cut remove, and would the story be better for it?
5. If the ending is written and the total exceeds 5,000, name the scene to cut — in advance, now, before the pressure is on.
