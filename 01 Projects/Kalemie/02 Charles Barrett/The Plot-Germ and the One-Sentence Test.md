# BARRETT — THE PLOT-GERM AND THE ONE-SENTENCE TEST

> The plot of a short story should allow of expression in a single short, fairly simple sentence; if it cannot be so compressed there is something radically wrong with it.

## The Three Levels

Barrett separates what most writers blur:

| Level | What it is | Kalemie example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Theme** | The primal truth the story illustrates, in the most general terms | *Inaction is complicity* |
| **True plot** | One short sentence. Trend and outcome fixed; treatment open | *A boy who collects dead things watches a girl be bought, and keeps her memory instead of justice* |
| **Working plot** | One line per important idea — see [[The Working Plot — The Skeleton Method]] | the scene-by-scene skeleton |

The one-sentence test operates on the middle row. If the true plot will not compress, the fault is **radical** — not in the writing, in the conception.

## What A Plot Is

Action. Something must happen. **At the end the characters must be differently situated, and usually differently related to one another, than at the start.** Not necessarily tragic or even serious, but of enough importance, novelty and interest to justify being told.

Usually a short story takes a *minor* crisis rather than the supreme crisis of a life, because the supreme crisis needs more preparation than the form allows. Stories that do take the supreme crisis — Barrett names Stevenson's *Markheim*, Hawthorne's *The Birthmark* — are exceptional, and work because the whole life comes to a focus in the single crisis that constitutes the story.

## The Plot-Germ Method

The advice is startlingly modern:

**Take two or three characters, strongly individualised morally and mentally, place them in a strong situation, and let them develop.**

Barrett's insistence is that plot follows from character with mathematical sensitivity. *Change any vital part in any character and the plot must be different.* Change the country and the atmosphere changes, which changes the incidents, which changes the plot. Two plots grown from the same germ can end up with no more resemblance than two shells on a beach.

Against this he sets **"building the pyramid from the apex downward"** — starting from a clever climax and reasoning backwards. He names it as the novice's characteristic error and traces it to an exaggerated conception of the importance of plot. His counterweight is the sentence that ought to be over the desk:

> It is not so much what the characters do that interests us, but how they do it.

## Plot Is Only Relatively Important

It must be present or there is no story; once present it takes second place. The short story exists to give a glimpse of real life, and plot is the means. It must not force itself through the fabric of the story **like the protruding ribs of a half-starved horse** — it should give form to the flesh, and be invisible under it.

## On Originality

There are no new plots. All stories trace to a dozen elemental ones. What passes for an original story is an old one seen from an unexpected angle — and Barrett proves it by synopsising eight magazine stories from March 1900, all built on *two men in love with one woman*, and showing that each is fresh only through one **vivifying element**: a new character, a new scene, a new mental bias in the writer.

## For Kalemie

**Run the one-sentence test now, because the draft may fail it.**

The [[Scenario — Arcs, Beats & Scenes|Scenario file]] already contains a One Sentence line, and it is 58 words long:

> *In a lake town where wealth arrives in tinted glass and justice arrives in an envelope, a boy who collects dead things has to decide whether remembering a girl is its own kind of justice, now that every other kind has failed her.*

That is a good sentence and it is not Barrett's test. Barrett wants *short* and *fairly simple*, and the reason is diagnostic: a plot that needs subordinate clauses to hold together may be two plots. Test whether Kalemie's compresses to something like *a boy watches a girl be bought and chooses memory over revenge* — and if the compression loses something essential, that lost thing is the second plot, and it is what is pushing the draft over budget. See [[The Balance Test — Length as a Function of Idea]].

**Kalemie's true plot and theme are currently competing for the same slot.** *Inaction is complicity* is a theme — a primal truth. The Dashboard treats it as the thing to be defined and clarified, which is correct, but a theme cannot be *plotted*. What can be plotted is Daudi's changed situation, and here the draft has a real problem Barrett names precisely: at the end of the current draft **the characters are not differently situated**. Djamela is dead before page one. Morisho is arrested before page one. Daudi ends the extant text watching an envelope get thinner. The [[Scenario — Arcs, Beats & Scenes|Scenario's ending]] fixes this — he takes the fob, he nails the motherboard — which is exactly Barrett's requirement arrived at independently.

**The pyramid warning applies directly.** *"Daudi finds the envelope, nails it, smiles"* has been a fixed ending on the Dashboard since 22 July, with the story built backwards toward it. Barrett would call that apex-downward construction, and the symptom is visible: the middle scenes are strong as observation and weak as consequence. Cross-read with Gardner's [[Working Backward from the Climax — the Victim-Story Trap|victim-story trap]] — two craft books, eighty years apart, warning about the same move.

**The plot-germ test is worth running on the ages.** Barrett: *change any vital part in any character and the plot must be different.* Djamela at 13 and Djamela at 16 are different germs and must produce different plots. The draft currently has both and has not chosen — see [[Kalemie — Standing Diagnosis]].

**The vivifying element.** Kalemie's elemental plot — an older man takes a young girl, a boy who loves her can do nothing — is genuinely ancient. Barrett's standard is that freshness comes from one new element. Kalemie's is not the setting, which is close to expected; it is **Daudi's physics vocabulary against Djamela's arithmetic**, two people measuring the same events in incompatible units. That is the vivifying element. Protect it.
