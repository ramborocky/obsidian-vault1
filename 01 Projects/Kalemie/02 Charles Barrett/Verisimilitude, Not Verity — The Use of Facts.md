# BARRETT — VERISIMILITUDE, NOT VERITY

> Verisimilitude, not verity, is wanted in fiction. The inwardness of facts makes fiction.

## The Principle

All fiction is founded on fact — the boldest imagination needs a point to launch from — but **the ungarnished truth is seldom literature.** Facts are crude material.

The amateur's error is thinking that because a thing happened, it belongs. Barrett's rebuttal is bracing: reality is very seldom strange; it is usually tame and flat, and when it *is* strange it is apt to be grotesque. Most of daily life supplies material only for a chronicle of dullness. He notes that even the reporter selects, suppresses, emphasises and arranges for effect — the "true story" is already an artefact.

He puts the operative distinction as: **the observer notes the facts, then the artist seizes the ideas behind them** — the types they represent, the substance they embody. The result is as lifelike as life and is a copy of nothing that ever lived.

The sub-title *A True Story*, which beginners prize, he calls the trademark of an unreadable mess of conventional people and commonplace conversation.

## Write What You Actually Know

Go to the world for material, not to other people's books — reading a good story and going and doing likewise produces unconscious plagiarism, and takes all the conventional parts while omitting the essential individuality.

His examples of the failure are all failures of *reach*: the writer who sets his tale in an England he has never seen; who writes the "best society" of a metropolis he knows only from the society column; who retreats into a dim past he has not researched. In each case the result "hopelessly lacks tone; it might pass below stairs, but not in the drawing-room — to those who know."

Against this he sets Mary E. Wilkins, who looked at the bleak hills and commonplace people around her — which other local writers despised as unpoetic — and won fame by seeing them.

## What Art May Not Depict

A limit Barrett states carefully and worth holding: nature can take liberties with facts that art dare not. Art may fill us with anger, fear, terror, awe — "but the moment it condescends to excite disgust, it passes out of the realm of art." Readers will not tolerate what feels like too gross an invasion of the privacy of a home or the sanctity of a soul; they must retain the sense that the suffering characters are made things, **or their pity for the characters turns into anger at the author.**

## The Case Study — The Willey House

The value of the chapter is that Barrett prints the newspaper account Hawthorne worked from, then lists every liberty taken in *The Ambitious Guest*:

- **Kept** the location — even he could not improve it
- **Changed** the month, to make the rain plausible and the wind seasonable
- **Omitted** all names, to add an air of unsolved mystery
- **Added** the guest and the grandmother
- **Aged up** the daughter
- **Removed** the hired men, as surplus to the story's needs
- **Removed** the real warning, but **kept** the shelter they had prepared — to heighten the climax
- **Suppressed** the finding of the bodies, for plausibility and pathos

Barrett's verdict: the newspaper account is a true story; Hawthorne's is literature.

## For Kalemie

**Kalemie's factual foundation is its greatest single asset, and this note exists mostly to stop it being over-trusted.**

The draft obeys Barrett's central instruction — write what you know — with unusual authority. Mia tano mia tano. The 16-seater Toyota Hiace. Ugali dagaa against the ghost kids' imagined baguettes. Nails on Friday, off Monday. The vaccination scar low on the arm rather than the upper. PK bubble gum. These are not researched details; they are *seen* ones, and Barrett's Wilkins example is the exact model: a writer looking at what surrounds him, which others would call unpoetic, and finding the story in it.

**Where the warning bites.** Barrett's rule is that facts are crude material and must be *selected against the story's needs*, not included because true. Two places the draft may be including because true:

- **The neighbourhood paragraph in Scene 2** — the fishermen, the passport-free lake, the observation that origin does not matter in Kalemie. Genuinely interesting, geopolitically sharp, and it does not influence the action. Barrett's test for setting is influence-or-cut, and this is the clearest fail in the draft. [[Dashboard|The Dashboard]] already wants Scene 2 merged; this is the reason why.
- **The "corpses aged twelve to seventeen" opening** — a true fact about mineral extraction, deployed as a thesis before any person appears. See [[The Beginning — Three Paragraphs at Most]].

**The Willey House method is the right tool for the age problem.** Hawthorne aged the daughter up because the story needed it. Kalemie has a live contradiction — Djamela 13 in the draft, 16 in her character file — and it has been treated as a continuity error to be reconciled. Barrett reframes it as a *choice to be made in the story's interest*: which age does this story need? At 13 the police-station penal-code beat has full force and Jean Luc is unambiguous. At 16 the story becomes about a girl's compromised agency and loses the legal engine. **Decide it the way Hawthorne decided the month — by what the climax requires.**

**The disgust limit is worth taking seriously, without taking his taste.** Barrett's period would have refused this subject matter entirely, and he should be overruled on that. But his mechanism is sound and is the same craft note [[Kalemie — Standing Diagnosis|the diagnosis]] reaches about Scene 6: when the reader stops feeling pity for the character and starts feeling something directed at the *author*, the story has lost them. That is a question about handling, not about subject — and the answer is the restraint both [[All the Small Things — Grief Through the Minor Object|the small-things method]] and [[Obfuscated Openness — Detail as Disguise|obfuscated openness]] describe.
