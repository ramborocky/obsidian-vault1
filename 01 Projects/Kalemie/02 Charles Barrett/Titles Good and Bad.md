# BARRETT — TITLES GOOD AND BAD

> A poor title usually means a poor story — and the editor knows it.

## The Claim

The title is not a handle screwed on afterwards. It is **the text of the story**, logically deduced from the plot — and because it is deduced from the plot, a weak title is usually evidence of a weak plot.

Barrett goes further than any modern craft book dares: **the title should be fixed long before the story is finished, and if anything should precede the plot**, the way Poe said he built *The Raven* around the word *nevermore*. His practical corollary — if you cannot find a title, examine your plot first; and if you can think of a dozen equally good ones, your plot may lack the definiteness of impression the form requires.

## The Five Tests

**Apt** — grows out of the plot and hints at what is coming, without anticipating the ending. The best form is *suspended aptness*, where the relevance only becomes clear after the story has been read. James's *The Lesson of the Master* is his model; he calls the effect almost ideal.

**Specific** — distinguishes this story from every other built on the same elemental plot. This is where the novice fails most: he takes the title from the *theme* rather than from the particular phase he is telling. Hence "Moral Vision," "Sorrow and Joy," "The Straight Path" — titles for essays in moral philosophy, not stories.

**Attractive** — must arouse curiosity at a glance. Commonplaceness is the usual failure and is usually traceable to the plot.

**New** — avoid the borrowed tag. "All's Well that Ends Well," "The Irony of Fate" and their kind are favourites with beginners and will cover a multitude of sins while constituting a large one themselves.

**Short** — modern readers object to being told in advance what is going to happen. No "Why Tom Changed His Opinion of Me."

## On Names As Titles

Permitted, with a condition: **if the name hints at character, or is unusual enough to attract attention, it may stand alone.** Otherwise it will be stronger in combination. He cites Stevenson's *Markheim*, Poe's *Ligeia*, Hawthorne's *Feathertop*. And a caution — if a name is to hint at character it must do so subtly enough to escape all but the quick-witted.

He also bans the double title joined by *or*, as the mark of penny-dreadful writing.

## For Kalemie

**"Kalemie" fails three of Barrett's five tests, and this is the most immediately actionable finding in the whole base.**

| Test | "Kalemie" | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Apt | A place name. Grows from the setting, not the plot | **Fail** |
| Specific | Distinguishes the story only geographically | **Weak** |
| Attractive | Arouses no curiosity in a reader who does not know the town | **Fail** |
| New | Not borrowed | Pass |
| Short | One word | Pass |

Barrett's own list of vague titles — "A Wedding in a Texas Jail," "A Lynching Incident" — fails for exactly this reason: they name the *subject matter*, not the story. "Kalemie" tells a judge where, and nothing else. On a longlist of 200 it does no work at all.

And by his diagnostic — *a poor title usually indicates a poor plot* — the flatness of the title is worth reading as evidence. It is the title you choose when the story has not yet decided whether it is about a place, an investigation, or a boy's silence.

**"Chantal" passes, and passes well.** It is a name-title, which Barrett permits when the name hints at character — and Chantal is not a character, it is *the name imposed on one*. The aptness is suspended exactly as he prescribes: on first sight it is a woman's name; after reading it is the collar with the nice buckle. That is the *Lesson of the Master* structure, arrived at by accident. **On Barrett's five tests, "Chantal" is the better title of the two by a wide margin.**

**Alternatives worth testing against the five tests** — each deduced from the plot's *phase* rather than its theme, per his specific-not-general rule:

- **The Founder of Dead Things** — apt, specific, attractive, suspended. Currently the draft's best line, doing nothing but sitting in paragraph two.
- **The Thin Envelope** — names the principal object, which is one of his three sanctioned title forms; specific; aptness fully suspended.
- **Some Women Resemble the Lake** — apt and attractive, but it is the *theme*, which is the error he names most often. Also it hands the reader the metaphor before the story earns it.
- **Twenty Seconds** — specific, curious, suspended, short. Risky given [[Kalemie — Standing Diagnosis|what Scene 6 has not yet resolved]].

**One caution against Barrett.** His demand that the title precede the plot is a 1900 working method, not a law. But the weaker claim holds and is worth adopting: **fix the title before the final draft**, because a title chosen last tends to be chosen from the theme, and theme-titles are the flattest kind.
