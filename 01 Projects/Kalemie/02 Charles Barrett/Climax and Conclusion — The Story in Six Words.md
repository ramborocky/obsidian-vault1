# BARRETT — CLIMAX AND CONCLUSION

> The climax is the apex of interest and emotion; it is the point of the story; **it is really the story.**

## The Editor's Second Test

If an overworked editor finds an opening that promises well, he turns immediately **to the conclusion**, to see whether the author kept the promise. Only then does the manuscript go aside for careful reading.

So the two places a story is actually judged are its first page and its last. Everything between is read on the strength of those two.

## The Two Parts

- **Climax** — the chief surprise, the relief of suspense, the point. It ends and eliminates suspense, and it must decide the destinies of all the characters and the fate of all their schemes.
- **Conclusion** — solving what remains, and the artistic severing of relations between narrator and reader.

## The Proportion Nobody Expects

The climax is felt throughout the story — the whole narrative is one long preparation for it — but **in itself it occupies a single ordinary paragraph**, and the climax proper, the point of the point, is **about half a dozen words.**

Barrett's example is Hawthorne's landslide. The build occupies a paragraph; the climax proper is four words:

> "The slide! The slide!"

## The False Climax

The novice's characteristic move, and Barrett says he is *too* skillful at it: **breaking off the narrative abruptly the moment the suspense terminates.** It produces a jump in the reader's mind by suddenness rather than by concentrated force. It is an abrupt conclusion, not a climax at all — frequently propped up with italics or capitals.

The distinction is exact and worth holding: a real climax makes the reader jump because everything preceding it has been loaded; a false one makes them jump because the floor ended.

## Three More Warnings

- **Climax ≠ tragedy.** The amateur, schooled by melodrama, believes he must end with a violent death. A climax may be as strong if it is only the *yes* with which the heroine answers. Often the catastrophe in a climax is merely a cause or a result of the real climax.
- **The climax must not come too soon.** Amateurs go into needless detail early, then sight the finish and rush — skeletonising the most important part, so the climax arrives feeling forced and illogical when proper proportion would have made it inevitable.
- **Preliminary climaxes must stay subordinate.** Few, distant, and never strong enough that one of them is mistaken for the real one.

## End As Near The Climax As You Can

> When the climax has come the story has reached its end, and the quicker you terminate it the better your reader will be pleased.

The conclusion averages one paragraph. **Climax and conclusion should be as nearly simultaneous as possible** — Barrett goes so far as to say that if Hawthorne were writing today he would probably end on *"The slide! The slide!"* itself, and be stronger for it.

Forbidden in the conclusion: padding, pointed morals, lamentation over the sins portrayed, and ending on a catch-word that refers back to the title. Above all, the conventional ending — which he says is fatal, and which follows inevitably from a conventional plot.

## For Kalemie

**Kalemie currently has no ending, which means it fails the editor's second test absolutely.** A judge who admires the opening turns to the last page and finds the draft stopping mid-scene on a thin envelope with no resolution. On [[The Judging Rubric — Nine Axes|the rubric]] this caps axis 8 at 1 and caps the story.

**The good news is that the raw material for a Barrett-grade climax is already on the page.** The envelope going in fat and coming out thin is precisely the structure he describes: the whole story loaded behind an image, the point delivered without commentary, in a handful of words. If the climax proper is *six words*, Kalemie's candidate already exists — **"Making it look thinner."**

**But it arrives too early, and Barrett names the symptom exactly.** The envelope lands at the end of Scene 7 with the story then stopping. That makes it a **false climax** by his definition: the narrative breaks off the moment suspense terminates. The jump the reader feels is the floor ending, not force concentrating.
