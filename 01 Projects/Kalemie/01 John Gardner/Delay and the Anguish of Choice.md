# GARDNER — DELAY AND THE ANGUISH OF CHOICE

> In serious fiction, the highest kind of suspense involves the Sartrian anguish of choice — our concern is not just with what will happen but with the moral implications of action.

## Relevant vs. Irrelevant Distraction

- The lead-in to a climax matters more than the climax itself. Every beat of delay should be a "relevant distraction" — something that heightens anticipation *and* enriches meaning by recalling an earlier planted detail (a sound the reader has already learned to fear, an object that contrasts with this moment). Irrelevant distraction (Gardner's example: musing about a mockingbird for no planted reason) feels manipulative — "we are being led, a little like donkeys" — even when it technically works.
- The classic mechanism: a frightening sound recurs, but it's one we (or the character) have already learned is harmless — until, this time, it might not be.

## Plot Suspense vs. Moral Suspense

- Ordinary suspense asks *what happens next*. The rarer, higher kind asks *what will this person choose*, when the choice is genuinely hard because both options rest on values the reader can respect. This is the suspense literary fiction can do that thrillers generally can't — it requires the character to have real, dramatized free will (see [[Interest, Free Will, and the Active Protagonist]]).

## For Kalemie

- The planned Crisis Decision — confront Jean Luc or do nothing — is precisely the "Sartrian anguish of choice" Gardner names as fiction's highest form of suspense, and it's the reason Kalemie's climax doesn't need a thriller mechanism (a chase, a discovery under time pressure) to be gripping. The delay leading into that choice should be built from relevant distractions specifically: earlier-planted details (the motherboard, a sound, a physical space) recurring at the moment of decision, not new incidental business.
- The police-station scene is the natural home for the recurring-sound technique: if a specific sound or object has already been established as harmless or routine earlier in the story (paperwork shuffling, a particular door, a fan), its return at the moment the envelope is handed over could do exactly the work Gardner describes — dread built from the reader's own prior experience of the story, not from anything new.
- Watch the BMW scene for the "irrelevant distraction" trap: if the Gap needs to be bigger there, the temptation will be to add new business (a stalling detail, an unrelated observation) to stretch out the moment before Djamela doesn't look back. Gardner's principle says that stretch has to be built from something *already planted* — an earlier gesture, sound, or object recalled — or it will read as padding rather than suspense.
