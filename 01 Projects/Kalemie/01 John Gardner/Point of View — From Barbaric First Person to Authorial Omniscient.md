# GARDNER — POINT OF VIEW: FROM "BARBARIC" FIRST PERSON TO AUTHORIAL OMNISCIENT

> First person locks us in one character's mind, locks us to one kind of diction throughout, locks out the possibility of going deeply into other minds. Henry James called its use in long fiction "barbaric."

## The Five Options, Ranked by What They Cost

- **First person** — not actually the "natural" choice it's assumed to be (no fairy tales or jokes are told in first person); good for a voice with real oral-tradition texture, risky because it can produce writing that's merely "less noticeably bad" rather than good, since it can't compensate for the loss of gesture and facial expression the way spoken storytelling does.
- **Third-person subjective** ("deep POV") — America's dominant contemporary mode. Locks the reader inside one mind even more thoroughly than first person's honesty about its own limits; when the character's judgment is wrong, the reader's correction can only come from ironic distance. "It peeks through a keyhole, never walks through an open field."
- **Third-person objective** — pure camera-eye, no interiority at all; works well in short bursts, obviously limited over length.
- **Authorial omniscient** — "the writer speaks as, in effect, God": moves between minds, judges sparingly, gives the reader the security of "the cards are on the table." Fell out of fashion with the loss of religious/metaphysical certainty historically, but is available to any contemporary writer purely as technique, "as much a fiction... as anything else."
- **Essayist omniscient** — has the reach of authorial omniscient but a distinct personal voice of its own (Jane Austen, not the neutral Tolstoyan register) — the narrator is a character in miniature, with opinions, without being a character *in* the story.

## For Kalemie

- Kalemie's actual choice — first-person, retrospective, single-POV — is the one Gardner is most skeptical of for anything beyond short-story length, and the story genuinely is one: this is exactly the form where his caveat is weakest and Cleland's [[Structure is King]] endorsement is strongest ("the reader needs to be trapped inside Daudi's retrospective knowledge and his blind spots"). Both can be right at once — Gardner's warning is a length-and-genre-dependent caution, not an absolute law (see [[Aesthetic Law and Artistic Mystery]]), and a tight short story is where first person's cost (locked diction, one consciousness) is cheapest and its gain (intimacy, the retrospective dread of the "that morning I knew" opening) is most valuable.
- The real risk Gardner flags — that first person can produce writing that's merely "less noticeably bad," because it doesn't force the writer to compensate for lost gesture and facial expression — is a genuinely useful diagnostic for a currently blank-charactered Daudi. If his first-person voice isn't doing extra work (physics-vocabulary metaphor, retrospective irony, a distinct diction), the story is paying first person's full cost without collecting its benefit.
- Retrospective first person gives Daudi something close to authorial-omniscient's authority without leaving the form: because he's narrating from after Djamela's death, he already knows what young-Daudi didn't, and can judge — sparingly, per Gardner's warning about heavy-handed moral intrusion (Tolstoy's *Resurrection* as the cautionary example) — in a way a strictly in-scene first-person narrator couldn't. That's the tool for making the "cyclical ending" land: the adult Daudi's voice bookending the story is functionally borrowing the security of omniscience inside a first-person frame.

## Questions for AI

1. Where does Daudi's retrospective narration currently *use* its hindsight — commenting, however lightly, on what young-Daudi couldn't see — versus where does it just report events as they happened, wasting the vantage point the form provides?
2. Is there a moment where the story needs information from inside Jean Luc's or Djamela's mind that first-person Daudi structurally cannot supply — and if so, is the workaround (someone tells him, he infers it, he never learns it) itself doing thematic work, or is it a limitation the reader will feel as a gap?
3. Per Gardner's caution about heavy authorial moral intrusion (Tolstoy's *Resurrection*) — does adult Daudi's retrospective voice ever tip from implication into telling the reader what to conclude about Jean Luc or the system, undercutting the Controlling Idea's earned quality?
