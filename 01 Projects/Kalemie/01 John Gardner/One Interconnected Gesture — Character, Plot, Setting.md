# GARDNER — ONE INTERCONNECTED GESTURE: CHARACTER, PLOT, AND SETTING

> To change a character's name from Jane to Cynthia is to make the fictional ground shudder under her feet.

## Everything Changes Everything

- Characters, plot, and setting aren't separately authored and then combined — every element affects every other, the way every atom in the universe pulls, however slightly, on every other atom. Coleridge's phrase: the writer must "copy, with his finite mind, the process of the infinite 'I AM'" — making the whole world in one coherent gesture, like a potter shaping a pot, not assembling parts.
- Practical consequence: a writer cannot fully authenticate a character by giving them the right props and backstory facts. He has to shape character, plot, and setting *together*, discovering how each constrains the others as he goes.

## The Smoke Game — Characterization by Symbolic Association

- In the parlor game "Smoke" (or "Essences"), a player identifies a hidden famous person only through metaphor — "what kind of weather are you," "what kind of smoke." The metaphors, taken together, converge on a startlingly precise portrait, even though no single answer is literal. Fiction does this deliberately and more precisely: place a character in the weather, object, or gesture that metaphorically expresses their nature, and the reader assembles the person without being told who they are.

## Two-Stage Writing: White Heat, Then Brooding

- First draft: write by feel, trusting instinct, "sometimes writing in a thoughtless white heat of inspiration," not stopping to intellectualize every detail's meaning.
- Second stage: read the draft over and over, patiently, letting the mind wander, watching for the unconscious's accidental repetitions (the same phrase used, unplanned, for two different objects) — these are the seeds of the story's real, discovered theme. Gardner's own example: noticing that he'd used the same phrase for Helen's thrown brooch and a sealed message, and following that accident until it revealed that Helen's independence was partly self-delusion.
