# GARDNER — THREE FAULTS OF SOUL: SENTIMENTALITY, FRIGIDITY, MANNERISM

> Faults of soul, like faults of technique, can be corrected. The main work a writing teacher does is help the writer become that "true Poet" without whom there can be no true Poem.

## Sentimentality — Effect Without Earned Cause

- Emotion the story hasn't dramatically earned: stock response (love of country, pity for the downtrodden, cute animals), cheap melodrama (innocent victim vs. cartoon villain), or rhetorical tricks (breathless one-sentence paragraphs — "Then she saw the gun") standing in for actually-built feeling. The cure isn't less emotion — "without sentiment, fiction is worthless" — it's earning it through character-in-action first.

## Frigidity — Insufficient Seriousness Toward the Material

- Longinus's term: the writer reveals, by some slip, that he's less concerned about his characters than any decent observer would naturally be. Gardner's example: a father-son fistfight rooted in real, established love and misunderstanding, undercut when the narrator reaches for a cheap, trivializing image ("crying like a baby... with his diapers full") instead of honoring the scene's real weight. Frigidity is also the writer who lets a sentence he knows is bad stand in the final draft — a failure to take the work seriously enough to fix it.

## Mannerism — Style in Service of the Writer's Ego, Not the Material

- Stylistic tics that make the reader sense "some ulterior purpose on the writer's part" — the style calling attention to the author rather than serving character and action. Distinguished carefully from justified idiosyncratic style (Sherwood Anderson's plain, stiff prose, which *expresses* his plain, earnest narrator rather than showing off) — the test is whether the story would lose something real if the device were removed, or whether it's there to prove the writer different from everyone else.

## For Kalemie

- D'juma's lake speech is squarely in sentimentality's danger zone as currently written (a paragraph of poetic wisdom) and the Dashboard's own fix — cut to one line — is a frigidity/sentimentality balancing act: cut too far into aphorism without earning it through her specific, dramatized grief, and it becomes frigid (a slogan standing in for real feeling); leave too much unearned poetry in, and it stays sentimental. The one line has to be earned by something concrete around it, per [[The Barn Exercise — Indirection and Symbolic Description]].
- The police-station scene carries real frigidity risk in the other direction: a scene about institutional indifference to a dead girl can tip into the writer matching the system's coldness rather than dramatizing it — Gardner's distinction is that the *characters* can be frigid (the officer, the system) but the *prose* must not be, or the reader can't tell whether the flatness is intentional irony or authorial disengagement.
- Daudi's physics vocabulary is this story's single biggest mannerism risk and its single biggest asset, and Gardner's test is the exact one to apply: does "entropy," "refraction," "gravity" ever appear because it's clever rather than because it's the only way *this specific boy* could actually be perceiving the moment? If the vocabulary would survive being replaced by a different clever motif without changing the story's meaning, it's decoration; if it's load-bearing to how Daudi processes grief, it's earned style, closer to Sherwood Anderson's plainness than to showing off.
