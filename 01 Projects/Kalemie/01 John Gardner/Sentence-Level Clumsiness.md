# GARDNER — SENTENCE-LEVEL CLUMSINESS

> In good fiction, the reader never has to go back over a sentence just to find out what it says.

## The Checklist

- **Passive voice** — nearly always weaker ("Your parrot bit me" beats "I was bitten by your parrot"); acceptable only for characterization (a timid character) or comic/institutional mimicry.
- **Infinite-verb-phrase openers** ("Looking up slowly from her sewing, Martha said…") — guilty until proven innocent. Overused as a lazy fix for repetitive subject-verb rhythm; correct fix is compound predicates, appositional phrases, or subordinate clauses instead. Can also produce outright illogic ("Firing the hired man and burning down his shack, Eloise drove into town" implies simultaneity that isn't possible).
- **Diction shifts** — elevated ("held a healthy natural red color") crashing into colloquial ("most of the time") in the same passage reads as amateurish, not as texture.
- **Sentence overload** — cramming all three syntactic slots of a sentence with modifiers destroys focus; load one slot, leave the others alone.
- **"That/which" trailing clauses** — tend to produce anticlimax, the sentence's energy leaking out the end instead of building toward it (the periodic sentence — important thing last — is generally stronger).
- **Faulty rhythm and accidental rhyme** — unintentional "sprung verse" (jammed stresses) or rhymes landing on stressed syllables distract exactly like a typo; sound should serve sense, not fight it.
- **Events out of order / cloddish detail insertion** — forcing the reader to reconstruct sequence, or visibly struggling to slip in a physical description (the character passing a mirror), breaks the illusion of ease.

Every item on this list is a different mechanism for the same failure: breaking [[The Vivid and Continuous Fictional Dream]].

## For Kalemie

- This note is the granular, sentence-level enforcement layer under the Dashboard's own grammar rule — "Minimize 'to be' verbs (was/were/is) — they train passive voice" — and the 20-word sentence rule. Gardner supplies the *why*: passive voice and long "that/which"-stretched sentences don't just violate a house style, they specifically produce anticlimax and distraction, which is why they're banned rather than merely discouraged.
- Diction-shift risk is unusually high in Kalemie because of its deliberate register mixing: Daudi's physics vocabulary, French/Swahili/Lingala left un-italicized as his native register (per the Dashboard), and a retrospective adult narrator's more considered voice all sit in the same sentences. Gardner's test isn't "keep diction uniform" — it's "does any shift feel like a slip rather than a choice." A sudden drop into elevated English-teacher diction around a French phrase would be the failure mode to watch for, not the code-switching itself.
- The 20-second rain scene (Scene 6) is the highest-risk zone for accidental infinite-verb-phrase pileup, since action sequences tempt writers toward exactly that fix for repetitive subject-verb rhythm. Gardner's alternative — compound predicates and appositional phrases — keeps the prose forward-moving instead of freezing into a string of "-ing" openers, which matters most exactly when the scene needs speed.

## Questions for AI

1. Scan the full draft for every sentence that opens with an "-ing" phrase — for each one, does it slow the moment on purpose (acceptable, per Gardner, for a weighted or considered beat) or is it filler dressing up a flat subject-verb sentence?
2. Find every passive-voice construction — for each, is there a specific characterization reason it should stay passive, or is it simply weaker than the active alternative?
3. Read the rain scene (Scene 6) aloud — are there any accidental rhymes or jammed-stress clusters that trip the reading rhythm at the exact moment the prose should be accelerating?
