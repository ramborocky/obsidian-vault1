# GARDNER — WORKING BACKWARD FROM THE CLIMAX: THE VICTIM-STORY TRAP

> A convincing imitation of reality is not enough. The writer must "mine deeper" — dig out the fundamental meaning of events by organizing the imitation of reality around some primary question or theme.

## The Method

- Start from the climactic event and ask what must be dramatized earlier to make it both *meaningful* and *convincing*. Gardner's two worked examples (a farmer's truck destroyed by a semi; a stripper who runs over a flagman) both hinge on the same fork: if the antagonist/accident hits the protagonist *for a reason we understand*, the protagonist can be the story's true center; if it happens for no dramatized reason, the protagonist has been demoted to a **victim**, and "the introduction of a malevolent [force] that simply happens into the story... has become such a cliché in modern fiction as to be almost unusable."
- Even when a story is well-plotted and well-observed, it can still be merely "a persuasive imitation of reality" with no real theme underneath — Gardner's example is Eudora Welty's *Losing Battles*: accurate, convincing, and thematically thin. The second, harder job is finding what the events are actually *about*, discovered through what genuinely interests the writer in the material, then letting every subsequent detail (setting, minor characters, imagery) get chosen in service of that theme.

## For Kalemie

- This is the sharpest available lens on the Dashboard's most structurally dangerous open question: if Djamela's death is ultimately random or accidental rather than caused by a dramatized, comprehensible chain of Jean Luc's choices, the story risks becoming exactly the "malevolent force that simply happens in" pattern Gardner calls a cliché — and Djamela becomes a victim-object rather than a character, which the Dashboard's own social-drama ambitions (systemic corruption, not random tragedy) argue against anyway. Working backward from her death, the question is: what, dramatized on the page, makes it *inevitable*, not just possible?
- The same backward-working method applies to Daudi's climax at the police station: the thin-envelope Gap only lands if the story has already dramatized, earlier, exactly why the system would fail this specific case in this specific way — not corruption-in-general, but a chain the reader can trace, the way Gardner insists Pigtoe's semi-truck death needs the earlier scenes establishing exactly why the driver would (or wouldn't) have hit him on purpose.
- "Mine deeper" is the direct instruction for finding Kalemie's theme underneath its Controlling Idea. The Controlling Idea is currently stated ("inaction is complicity"); Gardner's method says to find out what genuinely interests the writer in the *specific* material — Daudi's physics vocabulary, the lake, the motherboard — and let those choices reveal what the story is really about, rather than treating the Controlling Idea as separately imposed. If entropy is the governing image, is the deeper theme closer to "systems run down when no one intervenes" than to "inaction is complicity" as currently phrased — and if so, which formulation should organize the remaining revision choices?
