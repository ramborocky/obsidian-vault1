# GARDNER — PSYCHIC DISTANCE

> It was winter of the year 1853. A large man stepped out of a doorway. … Snow. Under your collar, down inside your shoes, freezing and plugging up your miserable soul…

## The Ladder

Gardner's five-rung example, from farthest to closest:

1. *It was winter of the year 1853. A large man stepped out of a doorway.* — tale-distance, formal, remote.
2. *Henry J. Warburton had never much cared for snowstorms.* — named, but still reported from outside.
3. *Henry hated snowstorms.* — closer; plain interior statement.
4. *God how he hated these damn snowstorms.* — inside his diction now, close to free indirect style.
5. *Snow. Under your collar, down inside your shoes, freezing and plugging up your miserable soul…* — psychic distance near zero; the prose *is* his sensorium.

## The Rule Is Control, Not Closeness

- No rung is inherently better. What ruins fiction is an *uncontrolled, unmotivated* jump between rungs — the amateur tell is "Mary Borden hated woodpeckers. Lord, she thought, they'll drive me crazy! The young woman had never known any personally..." — rung 4 collapsing back to rung 2 inside two sentences, with no reason.
- Convention: open at long/medium distance, move closer for high-intensity scenes, draw back for transitions, move closest at the climax. Deliberately holding one distance for a whole story (icy if held far, at risk of sentimentality if held near) is a legitimate, high-difficulty choice — but it must be a choice, sustained on purpose.
