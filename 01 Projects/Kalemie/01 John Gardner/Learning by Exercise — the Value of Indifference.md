# GARDNER — LEARNING BY EXERCISE: THE VALUE OF INDIFFERENCE

> Writing an exercise, the beginning writer is doing exactly what the professional does most of the time.

## Indifference as an Advantage

- Writing about real, autobiographical material locks every psychological censor on — the result is either safe-but-untrue emotion (an old friend's promiscuity becomes "sensitive and warmly sensual") or bold-but-distorted emotion (she becomes a slut). An assigned exercise, with no autobiographical stake, paradoxically produces *truer* feeling, because the writer is serious and playful at once, without the ego-protection reflex autobiography triggers.
- Most of what goes into real fiction isn't there because the writer desperately wanted it there — it's there because the story needs it: a minor character exists to sell the clock for the time-bomb; a thunderstorm gets built vividly not because the writer loves weather but because Martha's midnight phone call needs to be believed.

## Fiction Is Built of Structural Units, Worked One at a Time

- A story is a passage of description, then dialogue, then an action, then more description — discrete units, not one continuous rush. The professional treats each unit with full, patient attention on its own terms (perfecting Uncle Fyodor's store before the hold-up men even enter it) rather than rushing toward the "important" parts. Working unit by unit, with the larger plan held only in the back of the mind, is what produces a story with no dead spots.

## For Kalemie

- This gives direct, low-stakes permission for the exact revision method the Dashboard already prescribes — one scene a day, worked as its own unit ("Move Inciting Incident onscreen," "Compress per Rewrite Checklist," etc.) rather than attempting the whole draft's problems simultaneously. Gardner's structural-units principle is the theoretical justification for why the 7-Day Scene Schedule works better than trying to fix Daudi's arc, the Gap, and the ending all at once.
- The indifference principle suggests a specific unblocking technique for Daudi's blank character file: rather than sitting down to write "who Daudi really is" as an earnest, high-stakes act (which risks the same censor-lock Gardner describes for autobiography), draft him through a small, low-stakes exercise first — describe his room, or one object he owns, with no plan for how it will be used — and let the character facts surface as a byproduct, the way Gardner's students discover unplanned feeling in an assigned barn description.
- D'juma's lake speech and the motherboard are both, structurally, minor-character/minor-object units that exist because the story needs them (to carry theme, to carry the plot's central clue) rather than because the writer has an autobiographical stake in lakes or electronics — which is exactly the kind of "in service of the story" detail Gardner says gets built best: worked patiently as its own small unit, given full attention, then folded back into the larger plan.

## Questions for AI

2. Which current scene in the draft feels like it was written in a rush toward "the important part," skipping the patient unit-by-unit attention Gardner describes — and what would slowing down on its first fifty words alone produce?
3. Is there a minor object or character (beyond the motherboard) that the story needs but hasn't yet been built with full, independent attention as its own structural unit?
