# GARDNER — SHOW, DON'T TELL: NEEDLESS EXPLANATION

> Set beside the complex thought achieved by drama, explanation is thin gruel, hence boring.

## The Principle

- Amateur fiction states what should be dramatized: "Mrs. Wu is a crabby old woman" with a sentence explaining her sciatica, instead of showing her kick the cat, yell at the neighbor, complain on the phone. Experienced writers make the same mistake for a subtler reason — "too great a fondness on the writer's part for the mellifluous tones of his own voice."
- Gardner's paired example is the sharpest version of this rule in the whole book: a drunk detective, explained —

  > *"Detective Gerald B. Craine was very drunk... his sense of responsibility, his courage, his nobility of heart... were as keen as ever; but his eye for mundane truth was not what it might have been."*

  — versus dramatized:

  > *"Where the snake came from he did not see... With a yelp, without thinking, Craine threw down the bottle... He drew his pistol as he ran."*

  The second version conveys everything the first stated, and more, without a single abstract noun.
- Especially avoid authorial comment on what characters are *feeling*. A woman's whole morning of gesture and mutterings can make her decision to leave, at the end, self-evident "in a way almost too subtle for words" — at which point stating the reason makes the reader yawn and set the book down.

## For Kalemie

- This is the theory behind the Dashboard's banned-word list in its most literal form: Gardner's Craine example is a direct model for rewriting any Kalemie sentence that currently explains a feeling instead of dramatizing its cause. The exercise is mechanical and immediately usable — take any sentence with *felt, realized, understood, knew* and ask what Craine's snake-and-pistol paragraph would look like for that same beat.
- The BMW scene (Scene 5) is the place this rule bites hardest. "Make the Gap bigger — Daudi expects her to stay, she doesn't look back" is not a note about adding an explanatory sentence ("Daudi felt betrayed that she didn't look back") — it's a note that the *not-looking-back* has to be staged with enough concrete, Craine-level physical specificity (what exactly does Daudi see, in what order, in how much time) that the betrayal is inescapable without being named.
- The thin-envelope ending is Gardner's principle at its highest stakes: the Controlling Idea ("inaction is complicity") must never be stated by the narrator at that moment. If Daudi's retrospective voice explains what the envelope means instead of rendering the physical fact of it — its weight, what's missing from it, the motion of his hands — the ending collapses from tragedy into thesis statement.

## Questions for AI

1. Find every sentence in the draft that names an emotion directly (afraid, betrayed, ashamed, angry) — for each, what is the Craine-style dramatized action that could replace it?
2. In the BMW scene, list the physical, filmable beats of Djamela not looking back, in the exact order Daudi would perceive them — is that sequence currently on the page, or is the moment currently summarized?
3. Read the final paragraph with the envelope — does any sentence explain what the moment means, and if so, what concrete physical detail could carry that meaning instead?
