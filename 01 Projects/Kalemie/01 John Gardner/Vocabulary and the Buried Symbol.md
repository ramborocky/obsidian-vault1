# GARDNER — VOCABULARY AND THE BURIED SYMBOL

> In effective writing — normally — the writer slips in symbols and allegorical emblems with the cunning of a flim-flam man gulling his country victim.

## Ordinary Words Carry as Much Weight as Rare Ones

- A huge vocabulary isn't automatically an asset — fashionable words ("serendipity," "ubiquitous," "fictional strategy") and unearned ornate diction read as insecurity or dishonesty, especially when they violate a character's own register. But a *limited* vocabulary of ordinary words is a real, common, near-crippling problem too: the specific noun (lintel, corbelling, pinch-clippers) creates textural precision that vague nouns can't. Brand names characterize with particular sharpness — "the people who drive Toyotas are not the same people who drive BMWs."

## Symbols Must Be Slipped In, Not Announced

- The symbol that "stands out too sharply from its matrix distracts the reader's eye... with the unpleasing effect of making the writer seem frigid and his story disingenuous — a work in which the reader feels manipulated." Melville's model in "Bartleby": the narrator's elevated, orbicular diction lets a plain description of two office windows (a white wall, a blackened brick wall) carry a full second layer of meaning — Bartleby's death, the narrator's spiritual position — without the language ever breaking from ordinary description into announced symbolism.

## For Kalemie

- The BMW in Scene 5 is, unusually, both a literal plot object and a Gardner-style brand-name characterization device already built into the material: it doesn't need added symbolic dressing, because "BMW" already does the class-and-power work Gardner describes brand names doing. The risk is the opposite of most fiction's — over-explaining what the car represents, when the noun alone, placed correctly, already carries it.
- The motherboard is this story's Bartleby-wall test case exactly: its first appearance has to be described in the same plain, unremarkable diction as everything else around it in that scene (per Jean Luc's file, "buried mid-list among other unremarkable background details") for the later reveal to land as discovery rather than manipulation. Any elevated or lingering word choice at that first touch is the "symbol standing out too sharply from its matrix" Gardner warns against.
- Daudi's physics vocabulary is the place to apply the ordinary-vs-rare-word test most carefully: "entropy" risks being this story's "serendipity" — a word reached for because it sounds literary rather than because it's the precise, load-bearing term a physics-minded boy would actually use. The fix Gardner suggests isn't avoiding the vocabulary but widening it — the ordinary, specific vocabulary of Daudi's actual interests (a particular tool, a particular unit of measurement, a specific piece of scavenged electronics) will do more symbolic work, more invisibly, than repeating the same few showcase words.

## Questions for AI

1. At the motherboard's first appearance, does any word choice make it stand out from the surrounding background details in register, rhythm, or specificity? It should read as inert as everything around it.
2. List every instance of Daudi's physics vocabulary in the draft — how many distinct, specific terms are actually in rotation versus how often the same two or three words (entropy, gravity) recur? A wider, more ordinary technical vocabulary would likely carry more weight with less visible effort.
3. Is there a plain, ordinary noun anywhere in the setting — a tool, a fixture, a brand — that could be doing unannounced symbolic work the way Melville's brick wall does, currently sitting undeveloped in the draft?
