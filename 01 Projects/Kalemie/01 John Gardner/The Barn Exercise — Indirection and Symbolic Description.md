# GARDNER — THE BARN EXERCISE: INDIRECTION AND SYMBOLIC DESCRIPTION

> One does not simply describe a barn. One describes a barn as seen by someone in some particular mood, because only in that way can the writer's experience of barns be tricked into mumbling its secrets.

## Description as a Probe, Not Scenery

- Description isn't there to orient the reader or supply props. Its real job is reaching into the writer's unconscious and forcing half-buried symbols up where he can eventually work with them. Good description is symbolic *because it's built right*, not because the writer planted symbols in it on purpose.
- Gardner's model exercise: describe a barn as seen by a man whose son has just been killed in war — without mentioning the son, the war, the death, or the man doing the seeing. Done well, the result is a "faithful description of some apparently real barn" that nonetheless carries the father's grief, even though the reader can't always name what emotion they're picking up.
- The trap: "the hack mind leaps instantly to images of darkness, heaviness, decay." The images that actually work are rarely the first, most obvious ones — they come from emptying the mind of everything but the desire to tell the truth in concrete details.

## For Kalemie

- This is the theoretical backbone of "Show entropy — don't explain it (Daudi's room falling apart)," already a Medium Priority fix on the Dashboard. Gardner's exercise gives a sharper version of the instruction: don't describe Daudi's room *as* entropy (that's explaining), describe the room as seen by a boy who has just understood something is wrong, without naming what — and let the disorder be what it is, concrete and specific, until the reader supplies the dread. If the description reaches for the hack-mind images (dust, decay, shadows) it will read as authorial telegraphing rather than discovery.
- The same exercise, run directly on the manuscript: describe the motherboard as seen by Daudi *before* he knows it was Djamela's, without mentioning Djamela, mentioning that it's evidence, or naming what Daudi feels. That is exactly the "buried mid-list" first touch Jean Luc's file calls for — Gardner's method is a way to actually draft that paragraph rather than just plan it.
- D'juma's lake — "some women resemble the lake… it remembers everything" — already works the way Gardner describes: the governing metaphor didn't get planted as a stated theme, it accrued meaning through the physical, sensory fact of a lake that is deep, cold, and holds bodies. The risk in cutting the speech to one line is losing the concrete sensory grounding and keeping only the abstraction — Gardner's exercise argues for finding one physical, sensed detail (temperature, a color, a smell) to carry the line rather than trusting the aphorism alone.
