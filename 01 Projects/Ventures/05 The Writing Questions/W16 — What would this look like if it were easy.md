# 16 · What would this look like if it were easy?

The mind makes the plan proportional to the goal's importance. The question interrupts with an absurd premise: suppose this were easy.

**Winning something.** Elaborate: a nine-axis rubric, six craft bases, an empirical campaign, a cross-index. Easy: **finish the two paragraphs that are owed, submit, move on.** Both briefs are already written and marked inline in Draft 4.

**Making the writing pay.** Elaborate: win a prize against 7,806 entries and convert it into an agent and a book. Easy: **one story a month to a venue that pays $200–480 on acceptance**, from a cycle that generates material anyway.

**Getting better.** Elaborate: build a calibrated instrument. Easy: **three honest readers, briefed with cold-read prompts that already exist, given a deadline.**

**The Morland application.** Elaborate: a fourth pivot on the subject. Easy: the subject is fixed, the extract is in revision, the proposal exists, the biography is drafted — **finish those three and send them.**

**Settling the repo question.** Elaborate: a policy on public repositories and prize risk. Easy: **one email, asked neutrally, this week.**

Look at what the easy versions share. They are **smaller, sooner, and more exposed.** The elaborate versions are bigger, later, and safer — and that is not a coincidence. Complexity is frequently avoidance with a project plan attached, and it is especially convincing when the complexity is genuinely skilled work.

> **The question:** if this were easy, what would it be? Then ask what it costs to just do that version first. Not because easy is always right — because **the elaborate version should have to win against it, and usually it can't.**
