# 9 · What can't be copied in my prose?

Kelly's argument applies to fiction more brutally than to anything else. **Competent prose is now free.** A machine will produce a clean, publishable-looking 4,000-word literary short story about a lake town in seconds, and it will be fine.

So the question is not whether your prose is good. It is what in it **cannot be generated.**

Your own Learning Curve base already holds the sharpest answer, from Lynda Clark's research: **whatever a model generates by default is the cliché.** That is a working definition of the enemy, and it is testable — anything a model reaches for first is, by construction, the average of everything written before. The gap between that average and your page is the only thing you are actually selling.

The second finding in that same base is the practical one: **grief arrives through the minor object.** All the small things. That is not a stylistic preference, it is a description of what generated text is worst at — the specific, useless, true detail that no aggregate would produce because no aggregate contains it.

Applied through Kelly's eight generatives, four survive for fiction:

- **Authenticity** — it is yours, from that town, by that lake, and nobody else could have stood where you stood.
- **Embodiment** — a printed thing, a reading, a room. The music is free; the performance is expensive.
- **Immediacy** — readers who get it first, which is a thing to sell rather than a courtesy.
- **Patronage** — people pay writers because they want them to keep going. Kelly's own *1,000 True Fans* is the model.

Every one of those is built by **publishing**, not by perfecting. None of them can be earned by a story that no one has read.

> **The question:** what is in this that a model could not have produced — and have I cut it, in a revision pass, for being strange?
