# 1 · Who is the payer, and is it a reader?

Three routes out of the same 4,000 words, and they are not variations of one business. They are three different customers.

| Route | Who pays | Expected value | Shape |
| --- | --- | --: | --- |
| Commonwealth | A panel | **£1.92** per entry | Lottery ticket |
| Morland | A foundation | **£90** per application | **A monthly wage** |
| Paid markets | An editor | $200–480 on acceptance | An invoice |

**Morland is roughly 47× the Commonwealth per application**, and that understates it, because the Commonwealth pays one lump sum to one person while Morland pays **£1,500 a month for twelve months** — about $1,900 against a household-and-debt figure of $1,228.

Notice what none of the three is. **None of them is a reader.** A reader pays for a book, and no route here reaches one for years. That is not an argument against any of them; it is the reason the writing cannot be the income plan, and it stays true however good the prose gets.

The vault already found that selling stories beats prizes and KDP ([[Paid Markets]]). What it had not done is put all three payers in one table, which makes the ranking obvious and uncomfortable: **the campaign with the September deadline and 47× the expectation is the one being run in parallel, and the lottery ticket is the one being run as the campaign.**

> **The question:** for each thing I am about to spend a month on — who signs the cheque, how many of them are there, and what are the odds they sign mine?
