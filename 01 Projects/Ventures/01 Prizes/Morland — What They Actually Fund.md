## 🔑 THE SIX THINGS EVERY FUNDED PROJECT HAS

**1 · A dated wound, not a mood.**
1967. The 1800s. 1950s Algeria. Shakahola. The AIDS epidemic. **Not one of these is "a novel about a family."** Every single one attaches its people to a specific, nameable historical event — and the event does the work of making the book necessary.

**2 · Half of it is non-fiction, and that half looks winnable.**
Five of the twelve are non-fiction, in a field where nearly everyone applying is a fiction writer. Non-fiction also gets **18 months instead of 12** at the Foundation's discretion. **The non-fiction that wins is memoir-with-a-journey or reportage-of-an-atrocity** — never the essay collection, never the study.

**3 · A hook you can say in one clause.**
*Salsa-obsessed assassin. Resurrected dictator. 7,500 km on foot. First contact, but they already know.* Every funded project survives compression to about fifteen words with its strangeness intact. **If your idea goes flat when compressed, it is not ready.**

**4 · A named formal device.**
AfroSurrealist fable. Oral storytelling technique. Walking memoir. Climate fiction. These writers did not just say what the book is about — **they said what kind of thing it is**, and gave the judges a shelf to put it on.

**5 · Ferocious geographic specificity.**
The Kenyan highlands. Windhoek. Shakahola. Cameroon to the US and back at thirteen. **Nobody has ever been funded to write about "Africa."**

**6 · The writer is visibly the only person who could write it.**
Kwachou's own return at thirteen. Saio's own healing. Baraka's own East Africa. **The proposal answers "why you" without ever asking the question.**

## 💡 FOUR IDEAS

**These are angles cut from your own material, measured against the six patterns above.** They are places to stand, not proposals — the book has to be one you would still want to write in month nine, and the 400–1,000 words are yours to write. **Two fiction, two non-fiction, deliberately.**

---

### 1 · 🚂 THE VILLAGES THAT WERE MOVED — *fiction*

> **A lake-town family carried off by lorry during Operation Vijiji, and the forty years they spend failing to get back.**

Between 1973 and 1976 the Tanzanian state moved millions of people into planned Ujamaa villages — houses left standing, graves left behind, whole settlements emptied by lorry. **It is one of the largest forced resettlements in African history and it is almost unwritten in English fiction.**

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Dated wound** | Operation Vijiji, 1973–76 ✅ |
| **One clause** | *The state moved the village; the family spent forty years walking back* |
| **Why you** | It is your lake, your region, your language, and the generation that remembers is still alive to be interviewed |
| **The device** | Told across three houses — the one they left, the one they were given, the one they built |
| **Sample chapter** | The morning of the move. The objects chosen and the objects left |
| **The risk** | Ujamaa is politically charged, and the Foundation excludes *works of special interest such as religious or political writings.* **This must be a family's story that politics happens to, never a verdict on Nyerere** |

---

### 2 · 🧾 A DEBT OF HONOUR — *non-fiction*

> **What money does to a Tanzanian household, written from inside by the accountant who keeps the books.**

Microloans at 4% a month. School fees with hard dates. Mobile money. The unfinished house. **You are a qualified accountant carrying real debt in a country where nobody has written this from the practitioner's chair** — the literature is all policy papers from outside, and none of it has a kitchen in it.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Dated wound** | The mobile-money and microcredit decade, and what 48% a year does to a family |
| **One clause** | *An accountant audits his own household, and the numbers indict everyone* |
| **Why you** | ACPA, an employer, a ledger, a building site and a lender — **you are the only person on the applicant list who can do the arithmetic and write the scene** |
| **The device** | Structured as an audit. Each chapter a line item that turns out to be a person |
| **Sample chapter** | One month's expenses, followed line by line to where the money actually went |
| **The risk** | It must stay memoir and reportage. **The moment it reads as an economics study or a policy argument, it is excluded** |

---

### 3 · 🥁 ELEVEN BEATS FOR THE DEAD — *fiction*

> **A funeral drummer in a lake town knows nine of the eleven beats, and spends a life discovering why the last two are withheld.**

The trade of grief as a job. A man paid to feel nothing at the exact moment everyone else is feeling everything, in a town where the funeral economy is one of the few reliable ones.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **One clause** | *He knows nine of the eleven beats for the dead. The tenth is the one you hunt a reason to play* |
| **The device** | **Eleven parts, one of them withheld from the reader** — structure as inheritance |
| **Why you** | The world, the register and the trade are already in your hand |
| **Sample chapter** | A funeral where the wrong beat is played, and nobody in the family notices but one person |
| **⚠️ The risk, and it is real** | **The Foundation requires a new work, not a work in progress.** A published short story exists in exactly this world. A novel is not the story — but you would be proposing into territory you have already written, and a judge who finds the Kindle listing may read it as continuation. **Of the four, this is the one most likely to be ruled out on a technicality.** Ask before choosing it |

---

### 4 · 🧱 THE HOUSE THAT IS NOT FINISHED — *non-fiction*

> **Every road in East Africa is lined with half-built houses. This is one of them, room by room, and where the money went instead.**

The concrete skeleton with rebar still reaching up out of the pillars, waiting for a floor that may never come. **Everyone across the continent knows this building and nobody has written its book.** It is remittance, inflation, school fees, a funeral, a hospital bill — a national condition standing by the road in plain sight.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **One clause** | *A man builds a house for twelve years and it is still not finished* |
| **The device** | **One chapter per unfinished room** — each room is the thing the money was spent on instead |
| **Why you** | You are building it. The Ventures folder tracks it as a live and stalled track |
| **Sample chapter** | The roof — the single decision the whole build waits on |
| **Shape precedent** | Closest to Carlo Saio's walking memoir, which won: **one physical structure carrying a personal reckoning** |
| **The risk** | It needs a strong second subject or it becomes a builder's diary. The house is the frame, not the content |

## 🔥 IDEA 5, ASSESSED HARSHLY — the neo-colonialism essays

**Proposed:** essays on neo-colonialism — democracy and tech exported into Africa, the information diet, hyper-consumerism, existentialism, post-humanism.

**Verdict: as framed, this is the only idea on this page that could get you thrown out before anyone reads a sentence. And underneath it is possibly the best book here.**

### Six reasons it fails as stated

**1 · It runs straight into the one explicit exclusion.**
*"It is not the purpose of the Scholarships to support academic or scientific research, or works of special interest such as religious or political writings. Submissions or proposals of this nature do not qualify."* **A book of essays about democracy being exported to Africa is a political work by the plainest possible reading.** Not borderline. Centre of the target.

**2 · It is a collection, and they do not fund collections.**
They fund **a full-length book**, and they say so twice. A gathering of essays on six themes is a collection with a spine glued on. The rule names short stories; the reasoning does not stop there.

**3 · Six subjects is not a book, it is a syllabus.**
Neo-colonialism *and* tech *and* information diet *and* consumerism *and* existentialism *and* post-humanism. Every funded project on this page compresses to fifteen words and stays strange. **This one compresses to "thoughts about the modern world," which is what everybody's unwritten book is about.**

**4 · The field is not just crowded, it is finished.**
Nkrumah named neo-colonialism in **1965**. Then Fanon, Ngũgĩ, Achebe, Mbembe, Wainaina, Táíwò. **The judges have read all of it, from writers with chairs at universities.** Entering that room to say colonialism continues by other means is entering it to say something they have known for sixty years.

**5 · There is no "why you," and that is fatal here.**
Pattern 6 says the writer must visibly be the only person who could have written the book. **Anyone with an internet connection can write about hyper-consumerism.** Nothing in that list requires a Tanzanian accountant in a lake town — and everything you actually have is being left on the table.

**6 · The irony would sink it even if nothing else did.**
A book protesting that Africa is bombarded with imported frameworks, **written in imported frameworks** — post-humanism, existentialism, the whole seminar vocabulary. A sharp judge sees that in one paragraph. The form contradicts the argument.

> **Blunt version: this is the proposal of a man who has read a great deal and is arguing with the radio. The other four are proposals from a man who has lived somewhere.**

### But the instinct is right — and here is the version that wins

**Stop arguing and start reporting.** Every theme survives; not one of them gets named. Your own Constitution already says it: *we write scenes, not explanations; objects carry emotion.*

> ### 🧥 **THE BALE** — *non-fiction*
> **Follow one bale of dead men's clothes from a donation bin in Europe to a market stall on the lake, and every argument you wanted to make arrives without being made.**

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Dated wound** | The **East African Community's attempt to ban secondhand clothing imports** — and the trade retaliation that killed it. A specific fight, with dates, that Africa lost |
| **One clause** | *The West sends its dead clothes to Tanzania, and got angry when Tanzania said no* |
| **Why you** | It is **an import, a market and a balance of payments** — you are an accountant. You can price the bale and then stand in the market where it is opened |
| **The device** | **One object, followed the whole way.** Bin → sorting warehouse → container → port → auction → stall → the body wearing it |
| **What it carries, unnamed** | Neo-colonialism, consumerism, dependency, dignity, what it means to wear a stranger's name — **all of it as scene, none of it as argument** |
| **Sample chapter** | The bale opened at the market. Who buys first, what they pay, what is inside |
| **Why it clears the exclusion** | It is **reportage about a trade**, not a political thesis. The politics is in the facts, where it cannot be excluded |

**This is the same book you asked for.** It has a date, a place, an object, a chain and a "why you" — and it never once says *post-humanism*. → sits beside idea **2 · A Debt of Honour**, and the two could be one book.

## HOW TO CHOOSE — three questions, in this order

**1 · Which one survives compression?** Say each in fifteen words to someone who does not read. The one that makes them look up is the one.

**2 · Could you write 10,000 words a month of it for twelve months?** That is the actual undertaking, every month, and it is a lot of book. **The idea that excites you least at month nine is the wrong idea, whatever it looks like now.**

**3 · Fiction or non-fiction?** Non-fiction is the thinner field, may buy 18 months, and ideas 2 and 4 are both there. **But a non-fiction proposal has to survive the exclusion of academic and political writing, and both of them run close to it.**

> **My reading, and it is only that: 4 has the cleanest run at the six patterns** — an image everyone recognises, an obvious structure, no political exposure, and a "why you" that needs no explaining. **1 is the biggest book**, and if you have the appetite for the research it is the one that could matter for thirty years. **3 is the one to be careful with.**
