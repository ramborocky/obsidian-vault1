# PART B · THE PROPOSAL

## 1 · What kind of book is it?

**Not essays.** Say the word "essays" and two things happen: they read *collection*, which they do not fund, and they read *argument*, which the exclusion on political writing is aimed at.

> ### It is **first-person literary reportage** — a single continuous narrative, following one object down one chain.

The named tradition is the **commodity book**: one thing, followed from origin to end, with the world arriving as a by-product. Kurlansky's *Cod*. Rivoli's *The Travels of a T-Shirt in the Global Economy*. Minter's *Secondhand*.

⚠️ **Know your neighbours before a judge names them.** *Secondhand* (2019) and *T-Shirt* (2005) cover this exact trade. **Both are written from the outside, by Americans, following the goods outward from the donation bin.** Nobody has written it **from the receiving end, in the market, by someone who grew up wearing it.** That is not a gap in the literature by accident, and it is your entire claim.

## 2 · The dated wound — and it is stronger than I first thought

| Year | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| **2016** | Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Burundi and Rwanda jointly move to ban secondhand clothing imports — phase-out by 2019, source textiles regionally |
| **March 2017** | The US recycled-textiles lobby (SMART) petitions Washington for action |
| **2017–18** | The US threatens AGOA expulsion. **Kenya, Uganda and Tanzania back down** |
| **March 2018** | Rwanda given 60 days' notice |
| **2018** | **Rwanda holds. The US puts a 30% tariff on Rwandan clothing exports** |

> **Five countries decided together. Four folded. One stood and paid for it. Yours folded.**

That is the book's spine and it is not an argument — **it is a thing that happened, with dates.** Every theme you wanted is inside it: who decides what Africa wears, who is allowed to build an industry, what happens to a country that says no.

## 3 · The structure — stations, not themes

**Chapters are places on the chain, not topics.** This is what keeps it out of the essay trap.

```
the donation bin        →  the sorting warehouse   →  the grader
the container            →  the port                →  the auction
the wholesaler           →  the bale, unopened      →  THE CUT
the stall                →  the buyer               →  the body
                         →  what nobody buys
```

**The bale is opened once, at the centre of the book.** Everything before it is anticipation; everything after is consequence. The politics rides underneath and is never announced.

## 4 · What the 400–1,000 words must do

**Seven things. In your words, not mine.**

1. **The hook, in one clause** — say it before you explain anything
2. **Name the form** — first-person literary reportage, roughly 85,000 words, a single narrative
3. **The dated event** — 2016 to 2018, the ban, the threat, the folding, Rwanda's tariff
4. **The chain** — the stations, so they can see the book's shape in five seconds
5. **Why you** — *shown, never claimed.* An accountant who can price a bale and then stand in the market when it is cut open
6. **What the year buys** — the travel, the access, who you will talk to, why twelve months is the right length
7. **One paragraph that is simply good prose** — the bale being opened. **They are judging literary merit. Give them a sentence to judge**

> **Write it in several sittings, out loud, in your own register.** They are reading for a writer.

## 5 · Two things that could still sink it

**It must stay reportage.** The moment a paragraph starts explaining what the trade *means*, it becomes the political essay the rules exclude. **The facts carry the argument. You never state it.**

**It must not read as economics.** You are an accountant and that is your advantage — but the numbers belong in scenes: the price of a bale, what a stallholder pays, what she gets back. **A table is fatal. A price in a mouth is not.**
