# Zigula in Four Weeks

> [!danger] Read this before anything else
> **Claude does not know Zigula and must never be asked to supply a Zigula word, ending or sentence.** There is no reference grammar of the language in existence; anything a model produces will be invention dressed as fact, and you would carry it to Chogo and use it on elders while researching a book about them.
> **Every Zigula form in your notes must be traceable to a source below or to a named human.** Claude's job in this course is structure, Bantu grammar via Swahili, drilling you on what you have collected, and catching your inconsistencies.

## What "mid-beginner" means in four weeks

At 40 minutes a day, honestly:

| Reachable | Not reachable |
| --- | --- |
| ~300–400 words you can recall | Conversation at speed |
| The noun class system, recognised and mostly agreed correctly | Fluent production |
| Present, past and future frames on ~20 verbs | Narrative telling |
| Greetings, kinship and courtesies used correctly with elders | Understanding fast speech between two natives |
| Reading a verse of the Zigua New Testament against Swahili and parsing it | Reading unaided |
| **Running a recorded interview with an elicitation sheet** | — |

The last line is the one that matters for the book.

## Your unfair advantage

Zigula is Bantu **G31**, Seuta group — beside Bondei, Shambaa and Ngulu. Swahili is **G42**. You are not learning a language from zero; you are learning the distance between two close cousins. What transfers almost wholesale: noun classes with agreement, the verb template `SUBJECT–TENSE–OBJECT–ROOT–EXTENSION–FINAL VOWEL`, verb extensions (applicative, causative, passive, reciprocal), question formation.

What you actually have to acquire: **the lexicon**, the **sound correspondences** between Swahili and Zigula, a **different set of tense markers**, and the places where the class system diverges.

That is a much smaller job than "learn a language", and it is why four weeks is not absurd.

## The daily 40 minutes

| Minutes | What | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | **Listen and shadow** — audio, repeat aloud, no reading | The ear must lead. Reading first installs a Swahili accent over Zigula |
| 15 | **New material** — the week's block, from the sources | The only place new forms enter |
| 10 | **Drill** — yesterday's and last week's cards | Spacing is what makes it stick |
| 5 | **Produce** — write three sentences, out of your head, in your wordlist file | Production reveals what recognition hides |

## Week 1 · Sound and the sitting

**The goal is not words. It is an ear and a method.**

- Get every source above open and working. Audio on the phone
- **The sound map.** Listen for the regular correspondences between Swahili words you know and their Zigula cousins. Write down every pair you find. You are building your own conversion rule, and it will let you guess hundreds of words later
- **Greetings and courtesies to elders** — from a human, not a book. Register matters more than vocabulary and books get it wrong
- **Kinship terms.** The whole grid: mother, father, grandmother, uncle both sides, in-law, descendant. This is a book about descent; the kinship vocabulary is the technical vocabulary of your subject
- **Build the elicitation sheet** (see below)

**End of week:** 60 words. One page of sound correspondences. One recorded 10-minute sitting with a speaker.

## Week 2 · The classes

- The **noun class system**: what each class holds, what its prefix is, what it forces on adjectives, numbers and verbs. Ask Claude to teach you this in Swahili first — that part is safe — then fill the Zigula column yourself from Webonary
- Lexicon blocks: **body · house · food · farm · water · trade**. Concrete nouns first, always
- **Agreement drills.** One noun from each class, made plural, counted, described. Get them checked by a speaker

**End of week:** 160 words. Classes recognised on sight.

## Week 3 · The verb

- **Subject markers** for all persons. **Tense markers** for present, past, future — the Zigula set differs from Swahili's `-na- -li- -ta-` and finding out how is the week's work
- **Negation.** In Bantu it is often a different shape entirely, not a bolted-on "not"
- **20 high-frequency verbs**: come, go, eat, drink, sleep, speak, hear, see, give, take, want, know, work, farm, cook, sell, buy, return, remember, die. *Return* and *remember* are your book's verbs — learn them first
- Start reading **one verse a day** of the Zigua NT against the Swahili. Parse every word. This is where grammar stops being abstract

**End of week:** 260 words. Simple sentences you built yourself, checked by a human.

## Week 4 · Questions, and the real task

- **Question words** — who, what, where, when, how, how many, why. A researcher's core tool
- **Discourse glue**: and, but, then, because, after that. What lets you follow a story rather than a sentence
- **The interview.** Run a recorded conversation using your elicitation sheet. You will not understand most of it live — that is fine and expected. Record it, transcribe slowly afterwards with the dictionary, and that transcript is both your exam and your first piece of fieldwork
- **The question from [[Letter to the Editor — The Zigula Survival Argument]]:** ask three households which language the children answer in. Record the answers verbatim

**End of week:** ~350 words. One transcribed interview. One field answer nobody else has.

## The elicitation sheet — build it in week 1

For a language with no textbook, you do not "study", you **collect**. The sheet is a fixed list of prompts you run with any speaker, so that every sitting produces comparable data:

1. **Point and name** — 100 concrete objects, whatever is in the room and the yard
2. **The kinship grid** — every relation, both sides of the family
3. **The verb frame** — one verb, run through: I do it, you do it, he does it, we did it, they will do it, I do not do it
4. **The count frame** — one thing, two things, many things, across every noun class
5. **The story prompt** — "tell me about the journey" — recorded, not transcribed live. This is where real language lives, and where the book's material is

Run the same sheet with each speaker. The differences between speakers are data, not noise.
