## What this pass did, and what it refused to do

**Deletion-only. No word of mine is in any poem.** Every change below is a cut. Where a cut
would leave a hole that needs filling, the hole is marked `[?]` with the question only you can
answer.

The reason is the skill's own governing rule:

> A tell is a symptom of a missing fact. If you can supply the fact, supply it. If you can't,
> cut the sentence. If you can neither supply it nor cut it, mark it and tell the user. **Do
> not invent it, and do not blur it into something vaguer that no longer trips the pattern.**

In poetry every missing fact is lived material — Kariakoo, Chagula, Myuzi, Rahma, the ropes at
your mother's grave. I don't have any of it. If I wrote replacement lines you would have a
manuscript that scores clean on a detector and is *more* machine-written than the one you
started with. That is laundering, and the playbook names it as the failure mode.

**So: I cut, you fill.**

### ✂ "The Morning Breeze" (#1, prose version, 387 words) — score 72.4/1k

Five negative definitions in 387 words, and the structure itself is a label set:
*The First Verse · The Second Verse · The Third Verse · The Fourth Verse · The Final Inversion.*

> arrives **not as** a thing, **but as** a solvent
> offers **not** hope, **but** a blankness
> He listens **not for** cries, **but for** silences

Plus *a catalogue of densities*, *the grammar of stems*, *the vessel of the hours*.

**This cut solves two problems at once.** You have a second poem also called "The Morning

title in your contents disappears with it.

### ✂ "The King's Last Course: A Gastronomic Autopsy" (170 words) — score 32.4/1k

Invented Latin binomials and a thesaurus register that no ear produces. 170 words, and after

If the image you wanted was a man eating a last meal while something predatory watches him,

### ✂ "Shadows in the Moonlight" (630 words) — score 48.4/1k

Ten numbered sections. **Every single one closes on a portable aphorism:**

> what we release is what saves us
> to hold a forest, you must first be willing to be buried
> even your shadow has roots
> I am both the source and the delta, the beginning and the homecoming
> sorrow and joy are just different names for the same tide

Six negative definitions: *not a cage, but a vast sky* · *not an answer, but a deeper, more

as a different kind of presence*.

Ten sections, one shape, ten times. Deleting the closers removes 40% of the poem and leaves
nine openings with nothing after them. This is a rebuild, not an edit.

## "The Word of the Ghawth" (1,564 words, 20 sections) — 31.0/1k

The longest piece in the book and the most uniform. But there is genuinely strong material
buried in it.

**First, a thing you should see.** Sections VI and XI are the same two lines twice:

> **VI.** Because the mountains were stoic, I could not mark them with my bitterness.
> Because the heavens were named by our fathers in the great darkness, I could not raise my fist and claim them.
>
> **XI.** Because the mountains were stoic, I could not bruise them.
> Because the heavens were named in darkness, I could not claim them.

That is one passage printed twice, lightly varied. It is not a refrain — nothing else in the
poem repeats, and the variation is synonym-swapping, not development.

**Keep these six sections** — they carry images with weight and specificity:

- **I. The River of Bone** — *a thicket of white calabashes, their jaws still set in the grimace of a final, defiant shout*
- **VII. The Cage He Forged** — *great bales of ghost-cotton, a thicket of ropes dangling like the neckties of clerks* / **"The leg irons, he said, were the only truth that needed no poetry."**
- **XII. The Yokes** — *We laid out the wooden yokes on the bank, the ones our fathers carried.*
- **XIV. The Window He Carved** — the sage rubbed into a tilapia head, the window cut into red clay. Odd, specific, unguessable. Nobody generates this.
- **XV. The Coffle** — *he dragged all the yokes back to the water's edge and laid them in a coffle. / Each skull remembered its name, the patient, splintering weight of its yoke.*
- **XX. The Last Breath** — **"I cocked my ears and listened, a man who loved a man."**

That last line is the truest thing in the poem and it is sitting at the bottom of 1,500 words
of scaffolding.

**Cut these eight sections:** VI, VIII, XI, XIII, XVI, XVII, XVIII, XIX.

They are the anaphora block (`Because…` × 7), the aphorism block (*"Nothing with horns — be it
buffalo or truth — can be bound forever"*), and the two `I must forgive you for not knowing
the language of which I speak` sections, which appear at IX and XVIII in near-identical form.

**Net:** 1,564 → roughly 600 words. That is a 60% cut, which the playbook's checklist would
normally flag as "you cut instead of replacing." Here it's the point: what's left is the poem,
and it's a better one at a third the length.
