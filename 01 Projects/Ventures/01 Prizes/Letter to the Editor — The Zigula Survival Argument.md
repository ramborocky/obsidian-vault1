# Letter to the Editor — The Zigula Survival Argument

> [!abstract] What this is
> A skeleton for a letter to an East African paper on the survival of Zigula–Mushunguli. **Structure, evidence and traps only — every sentence of the letter is the author's.**
> Companion to [[Our Descendants Came Back Home — Marked Up]] and [[The Mushunguli — Structure and Proposal Draft]].

## The thesis — B

**Dispersal did not kill this language. Assimilation at home might.**

It survived slavery, a thousand miles, a century and a half, and a language from another family. What it may not survive is being the second language in its own district. The threat changed shape: for 150 years it was distance, and distance lost. Now it is prestige, and prestige is winning.

## The beats

### 1 · The peg — 1–2 sentences
A letter needs a reason to exist this week. Candidates, in order of strength:
- A Kiswahili-promotion or language-in-education story the paper has just run
- The 2003 resettlement anniversary, or any Somali Bantu return coverage
- A heritage, archives or "vanishing culture" feature to argue against

**Name the piece in the first line.** Editors cut letters that float free of the news.

### 2 · The fact, told fast — ~80 words
Sequence only, no adjectives. The sequence is the argument:
- Taken out of Handeni in the 1800s
- Escaped their owners into the Juba forest, around 1840
- By 1900, spoken in fifteen villages a thousand miles north, surrounded by Somali — a Cushitic language, not a Bantu one
- Scattered again by the civil war; camps in Kenya, resettlement to the United States from 2003
- Some returned to Chogo, Handeni — the district they were taken from

### 3 · The turn — 1 sentence
The hinge. *None of that killed it. What is killing it is ordinary life in Tanga.*
If a reader stops here they still have the point.

### 4 · What follows — 2 claims, ~150 words
Two only, each with something concrete under it:
- **Transmission, not numbers, is the measure.** Adults speak it; not all the young do. A language dies at the moment parents stop passing it on, not when the last speaker dies — that moment arrives decades earlier and is invisible
- **It has been under pressure at both ends, and the question is why it held in one place and not the other.** Maay Maay progressively replaced Swahili and the Bantu languages across the Gosha villages; Mushunguli survives in Jilib and Jamaame districts, not everywhere. So the diaspora was never a sealed jar. **The real question — and it is a better one — is why a language holds where it is the marker of a distinct people, and slips where it is merely the old way of saying things**

### 5 · The ask — 2 sentences
Letters that name an action get printed:
- Record the last fully fluent generation, now, at Chogo and in Handeni
- A place for Zigula in Handeni's own schools — alongside Kiswahili, not against it
- Treat the returnees as a research asset, not a welfare case

### 6 · The sign-off — 1 line
Name · Dar es Salaam · **Zigua, writing a book on the Mushunguli.**
That standing is why they print you and not the next letter.

## The trap — read this twice

> [!danger] Do not let this read as an argument against Kiswahili
> In Tanzania, Kiswahili is a national achievement and a unifier, and any letter that seems to resent it will be dismissed as ethnic particularism before the argument is heard.
> **The letter must be pro-Kiswahili and pro-Zigula in the same breath.** The line to hold: Kiswahili is what lets Tanzanians speak to each other; Zigula is what lets a family speak to its own dead. A country can afford both, and the second costs almost nothing.

## Keep out

- ❌ *"Second most spoken language after Swahili"* — false by a factor of ten. Sukuma alone has ~8.1M
- ❌ *"Most geographically diverse language in East Africa"* — Somali, Oromo, Tigrinya, Nuer and Dinka are all more dispersed
- ❌ The full naming history — Zigula, Mushunguli, Gosha, Jareer, Somali Bantu. **Use one name, gloss it in three words, move on.** There is no room, and the tangle reads as throat-clearing

## Consider putting in

**That your own Zigula is not fluent.** In a funding proposal it is a liability. In a letter it is the strongest line available: the descendant who must be translated to his own relatives *is* the argument. One sentence, unhedged, then never again.

> [!warning] Corrected 2 September 2026
> An earlier version of this note claimed the language survived in the Juba valley because no prestige language was available to the household. **That is wrong.** Maay Maay — Cushitic, not Somali proper — progressively replaced Swahili and the Bantu languages across Goshaland, and Mushunguli now survives in Jilib and Jamaame districts rather than throughout. The language was held against pressure on both sides, and lost ground on both.
> **Do not write the sealed-jar version.** The defensible engine is the comparison: why it held in Jilib and Jamaame, and why it is slipping in Handeni.

> [!warning] One claim to confirm before it is printed
> That transmission is breaking **faster in Handeni than in the diaspora** remains the letter's engine, and it still rests on inference rather than a source. Ethnologue's "not all young people" covers the language as a whole. **Settle it at Chogo:** ask three households which language the children answer in. That single answer, reported plainly, is worth more than any figure in the table above.
