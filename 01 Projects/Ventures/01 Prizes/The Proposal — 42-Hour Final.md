## THE TEXT — 665 words

In April 1983 the residents of Oysterbay and Upanga began putting their televisions in the street. Not selling them. Putting them out at night, along with bundles of banknotes, into gutters that had never been asked to hold anything of value. In the morning the police collected it and delivered it to the Bank of Tanzania, which counted it. Nobody had been charged with anything. A law had been passed, and the objects understood it before the people did.

The law was the Economic Sabotage Act. The man running it was Edward Sokoine, the Prime Minister. It reversed the burden of proof: a citizen was now required to explain his own possessions, in special courts, without a lawyer. Fourteen months later Sokoine was dead on the Dodoma road at Dakawa, and a South African named Dumisani Dube was convicted of killing him.

This book is an investigation of those fourteen months and of the seventeen years that made them possible. It is narrative non-fiction, roughly eighty-five thousand words, in three parts, organised not by chronology but by the three spaces the Tanzanian state let itself into: the village, the house, and the road.

The village came first. In February 1967 the Arusha Declaration announced that the nation would be built out of its own people. Between 1973 and 1976 the state moved millions of them into planned villages, mostly by lorry. Houses were left standing. Graves were left where they were. The policy was given an operational name, Operation Vijiji, and the name is the most honest document the period produced.

The house came sixteen years later, in April 1983, and it required a man to account for his own furniture. It was popular. It was popular because everybody knew somebody it should have been aimed at.

The road came last, on 12 April 1984, where a Land Cruiser met the Prime Minister's car on the Dodoma–Dar es Salaam highway at a speed nobody has ever established. Dube served the sentence. Forty years on the country has not accepted the verdict, and the interesting thing is not whether the verdict is right. The interesting thing is that a state which had spent seventeen years asking its citizens to account for everything could not, in the end, account for this.

I was born in 1977, the year the ruling party was founded, and at home I was called Chama after it. My father had served near Nyerere and was appointed ambassador to the United Kingdom in 1986. In 1990 he took me to a hospital in London to see Mwalimu, who asked my name; my father explained the nickname, and Mwalimu laughed. I have thought about that laugh for thirty-six years. It was the sound of a man entirely at ease with having named a generation after an institution.

I am also a qualified accountant. I can read a ledger, a seizure inventory and a tribunal record, which is a duller qualification than the one above and a more useful one. The archive is in Kiswahili.

The scholarship year buys the fieldwork: Iringa and the resettled villages, the Dodoma road at Dakawa, the Tanzania National Archives, the special-tribunal records, Dube's trial file, and the runs of *Daily News* and *Uhuru* between April 1983 and April 1984. All of it is inside Tanzania. There is no visa in this book and no fixer.

It also buys the interviews, and that is the part with a clock on it. The people who were put on the lorries in 1974 are in their seventies and eighties. The people arrested in 1983 are not much younger. This is the last decade in which the book can be reported rather than researched. After that it becomes somebody's thesis.

I am not going to solve the accident. The country has been not-solving it since 1984 and has become practised at it. What I want to write is the seventeen years that made a road a plausible place for a government to end.
