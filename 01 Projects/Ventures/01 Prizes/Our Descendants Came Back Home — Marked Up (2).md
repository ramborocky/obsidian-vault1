# 🚪 THE MUSHUNGULI — STRUCTURE AND PROPOSAL DRAFT
### *The Two Doors* · **~790 words** · one continuous piece of prose

→ [[The Mushunguli — Chogo, Saadani, and the Language They Kept]] · [[The Proposal — Drafting Session]] · [[Oduah Read Closely — The Method Behind the Funded Book]]

> [!danger] 🔴 THE FINDING THAT CHANGES THE BOOK — **April 2003**
> **The Chogo transfer and the first Somali Bantu arrivals in New York happened in the same month.**
>
> | | |
> | --- | --- |
> | **Chogo, April 2003** | 21 km² in Handeni, Tanga. Over 3,000 people from Mkuyu camp. **2.5 acres a family.** Citizenship in 2009 |
> | **New York, April 2003** | The first arrivals of **~12,000** — what US authorities called *the most ambitious resettlement programme ever from Africa*. **35 states.** Columbus, Ohio is now the largest community; Lewiston, Maine the most written-about |
>
> **One people. One month. Two doors.** Some walked back onto the land their ancestors were taken from. Some went to Ohio.
>
> **And there was a third door that closed.** Mozambique accepted them first, on the same ancestral-link reasoning Tanzania used — **then withdrew after the floods**, and the United States stepped in.
>
> **This is what makes it a book rather than a local history.** It is not *what happened at Chogo*. It is: **when a people finally get to choose where home is, what do they choose — the place they came from, or the place that will have them?**

## 📚 THE STRUCTURE — four movements

> [!warning] ⚠️ On the "table of contents" you asked for
> **Morland takes no contents page, no chapter list, no numbered sections.** The description is **400–1,000 words of continuous prose.** The 2025 application failed partly on exactly this — it had *2. · 3. Why Me? · 4. Why the Morland Scholarship? · 5. Conclusion.*
>
> **The structure below is for you and for the eventual publisher.** In the proposal it appears only as Move 4 — **four things that happened, not four things you will write.**

| | Part | When | The move |
| :-: | --- | :-: | --- |
| **I** | **THE FOREST** | 1800s–1900 | Taken out of Tanga to the Juba and Shebelle plantations. Escape into the forests as the **Gosha**. **By 1900, Kizigula is the language of fifteen villages a thousand miles north** |
| **II** | **THE PORT** | 1870s–1890 | **Bwana Heri bin Juma** holds Saadani against the Sultan, then the Germans. Bombarded June 1889, forts inland, surrender April 1890. **And Saadani was a slave harbour** |
| **III** | **THE CAMPS** | 1991–2003 | Barre falls. No clan, no protection. Dadaab, then **900 miles across Kenya to Kakuma**. Mkuyu. **Mozambique says yes, then no.** The choice arrives |
| **IV** | **THE TWO DOORS** | April 2003– | **Chogo and Columbus, in the same month.** Citizenship 2009. And at Chogo the language begins to go |

**Parts I and II overlap in time on purpose** — the same decade from both ends of one coast. **The book is not chronological, and [[Oduah Read Closely — The Method Behind the Funded Book|neither is Oduah's piece]]**, which opens in 2019 and reaches back to 1989.

**Narrative order:** open in **Part IV**, at Chogo, now. Then go back.

## THE DRAFT

> **The spine, in fifteen words — write it on a card first:**
> ### *Taken out of Tanga as slaves in the 1800s. In 2003 they walked back.*

---

At Chogo, in Handeni, there are old people who have never been to Tanzania before and who speak the language of the place they are standing in. They learned it from their grandmothers, who learned it in Somalia, from women who learned it on plantations on the Juba river from women who had been carried north out of this district in chains. It is my language. When they arrived in 2003 the officials did not need an interpreter.

Their great-great-grandparents were Zigua, taken to the Somali coast in the nineteenth century, when between twenty-five and fifty thousand people were sold from Zanzibar to work the Juba and the Shebelle. Some escaped into the forest and were called the Gosha, the people of the forest, and by 1900 Kizigula was being spoken in fifteen villages a thousand miles from Tanga. They kept it for six generations. At Chogo, now, their grandchildren speak Kiswahili.

`[What you heard at Chogo — one sentence, one person, one thing they said. Put it here.]`

**THE TWO DOORS** is the story of a people who were finally asked where home was, and did not all give the same answer.

In April 2003 the government of Tanzania gave them twenty-one square kilometres at Chogo and two and a half acres a family, and six years later it gave them citizenship. That same month the first of about twelve thousand of them landed in New York, in what the Americans called the most ambitious resettlement ever attempted out of Africa. They are now in thirty-five states. The largest community is in Columbus, Ohio. Before either of those doors opened, Mozambique had agreed to take them on the same ancestral reasoning Tanzania used, and then the floods came and Mozambique said no.

The book is investigative narrative non-fiction, roughly eighty-five thousand words, in four parts, and one of them goes backwards. While their ancestors were being shipped north out of this coast, a Zigua named Bwana Heri bin Juma was holding the port of Saadani against the Sultan of Zanzibar and then against the Germans. He was shelled out in June 1889, built forts inland, and did not surrender until April 1890. Saadani was a slave harbour. He is remembered here as a man who fought the Germans.

I am not going to resolve that, and I am not going to establish how he died — one account has him hanged, another has the Germans calling him an honourable enemy and telling him to rebuild the town. What can be established is what the port did, what the man did, and that both are true. The distance between those two facts is the subject.

I am Zigua, from Tanga. The people at Chogo are what my family would have been if a raid had gone the other way, and that is the whole of my claim to this. `[Your own connection — the village, the name, anything specific.]` Chogo is `[X]` hours from where I live. I have been, and I have started with `[number]` of them.

The people who made the crossing in 2003 as adults are now in their sixties and seventies. The ones who remember the Juba villages are older than that. The language they carried for a hundred and fifty years is being lost in one generation, at home, in peace, and nobody is doing anything wrong.

## HOW IT IS BUILT

| Move | Where | Doing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1 · The image** | *officials did not need an interpreter* | True, strange, and it is the whole book in one clause |
| **2 · The spine** | *finally asked where home was, and did not all give the same answer* | The reader assembles the question; the proposal never asks it |
| **3 · The form** | *narrative non-fiction, eighty-five thousand words, four parts* | Named, sized, shelved. **No adjective** |
| **4 · The movements** | forest · port · camps · two doors | **Four things that happened.** The Mozambique detail does the work of a whole paragraph |
| **6½ · The refusal** ⭐ | *I am not going to resolve that* | **Bwana Heri and the slave harbour.** Converts the exclusion risk into visible judgement |
| **5 · Why you** | *what my family would have been if a raid had gone the other way* | **The strongest why-you available to any applicant anywhere.** Never claimed, just stated |
| **6 · The clock** | *lost in one generation… nobody is doing anything wrong* | Urgency with no villain in it |
| **7 · The close** | same sentence | **Ends on the loss, not on a promise** |

**The controlling idea is never stated:** *a people can survive being stolen and not survive being safe.* It arrives in the last clause and every paragraph above is evidence for it.
