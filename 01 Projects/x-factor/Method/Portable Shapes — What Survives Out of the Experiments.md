# PORTABLE SHAPES
### What survives out of the experiments — **shapes only, no sentences**

> [!success] This file is safe to keep open while you write
> **There is no prose from [[mihogoni-3f]], [[mihogoni-3g]], [[MABANGA (New Story — AI Draft, Not Canon)]] or [[mihogoni-2]] anywhere on this page.** No quoted lines, no images, no phrasings. Every device below is described as a *shape* — what it does and what it needs — so that whatever fills it has to be invented at the keyboard.
>
> **That is the point.** A device is a form. The words that go in it are the work, and the work is yours. Keep the experiment files closed.

## The seven shapes

### 1 · An epigraph series over one object
A short block of found text sits above each section. All the blocks describe **the same object** — but from different hands, in different eras, in different languages or registers. The reader assembles a history from the sequence alone.

**What it needs:** one object that has genuinely passed through several regimes. The blocks must sound like real documents, not like the story. Nothing in the prose may comment on them.

**Why it works:** the sequence carries the argument, so no sentence has to.

---

### 2 · A clause that survives every register and quietly changes category
Inside that series, **one recurring provision** appears in every block. It says the same thing each time. But the terms it uses migrate — from one kind of category to a completely different kind — so that by the last block the thing being described has been silently reclassified.

**What it needs:** the provision must be boring. If it is interesting, the reader will watch it instead of absorbing it.

**The detonator:** the protagonist ends up *inside* the category, in an ordinary scene, and does not connect it to anything.

**This is the strongest device in any of the experiments and it costs nothing but placement.**

---

### 3 · A bureaucratic joke, delivered aloud, with a delayed laugh
An official explains a piece of the system to someone it is about to be used against — accurately, and as a joke. The people who benefit laugh. Then **one young person laughs late**, because they understood it was a joke before they understood what kind.

**What it needs:** the explanation must be true and checkable. The delay must be short — a beat, not a paragraph — and nobody may remark on it.

---

### 4 · A closing list of true things that serve nothing
Near the end, a short paragraph of unrelated facts about the place: things that happened, people who did something odd, small histories the story never picks up. It ends on **a flat statement that none of it is relevant.**

**What it needs:** every item must be cut-testable — remove it and the plot notices nothing. At least one should be a person whose life the story does not follow.

**Why it works:** it is the world proving it existed before the story started. → the Atelier's Day 18.

---

### 5 · An untranslated word, held back and spent once, last
A word from the narrator's own language appears **nowhere in the story until the final line**, and is never glossed. Its meaning arrives entirely from how the moment is staged — who said it, in front of whom, and what it cost.

**What it needs:** the staging must deliver two things without translating either — roughly, the register (formal? intimate? public?) and the relation it claims. If a reader needs the dictionary, the staging is not doing enough yet.

---

### 6 · One object, two economies, never named together
A single physical object appears in two completely different transactions in the story — one intimate, one commercial. **The same word is used for both.** No sentence, and no character, ever puts the two uses in the same breath.

**What it needs:** the two scenes should be far apart, and the second should be told plainly, as ordinary business.

---

### 7 · A skill the narrator has that the reader does not
The narrator is **the most competent person in the story at one specific thing**, and the reader cannot do it. Their expertise is real, demonstrated, and unimpressive to everyone around them.

**Why it works:** it inverts the usual position of a young narrator — they stop being the one who has things explained to them. It also buys any amount of unrelated appetite and vanity for free.

---

## The two that do **not** transfer

These were tested and they failed. → [[19 The Third-Person Test — What Close Third Bought and Cost]]

**✗ A passage explaining how the languages of the town work.** It reads as intelligence in close third and as implausible lecturing in first person, and everything in it can be dramatised instead — one room where three languages do three different jobs will do all of it in a paragraph of action.

**✗ A long scene the narrator is excluded from.** If she is far enough away for the exclusion to hurt, she is too far away to have heard it. Either she is close and it is not exclusion, or it shrinks to what carries across the distance. **Third person hides this bill; first person itemises it.**

## The one rule while drafting

> **Work from this page, not from the experiment files.**

If you need to check what a device does, it is described above. If you find yourself wanting to reread [[mihogoni-3f]] or [[mihogoni-3g]] for *how a line went*, that is the moment to stop and write it wrong instead — a worse sentence that is yours beats a better one that is not, and under rule 3e it is not close.

Google Docs keeps revision history at keystroke level. That history is the best authorship evidence available to you, and it is only worth anything if the drafting genuinely happened there.
