# PLOT AS YEARNING, NOT PYRAMID

> [!danger] This is the note where the sixth base attacks the first
> **[[Story structure by robert mackee.md|McKee]] is the vault's foundation for structure, and Faulkner's chapter 8 is a direct assault on the tradition McKee sits in.** Read both. Do not resolve it by picking a side — resolve it per story, and know which one you chose.

## The principle

> **"A short story, in its brevity, may not have a fully developed plot, but it must have the essence of a plot, yearning."** — Robert Olen Butler, quoted by Faulkner

Yearning. **Not a sequence of points that map to a diagram — a restlessness that carries the reader from one moment to the next with uncertainty running through it.**

Faulkner's charge against Freytag's pyramid is blunt: it is *"the most tyrannical of all craft elements"* and it *"kidnaps young writers each year."* Megan Giddings, quoted at length, makes the sharper version — a stringent requirement of continuously rising tension *"means that quieter moments, humorous moments, moments where time slows and allows room for thought, might feel like they need to be cut."*

**That is not an aesthetic complaint. It is a description of how good material gets deleted.**

He goes after Campbell's monomyth on the same ground and adds a cultural argument via Matthew Salesses: Western narrative descends from oral performance recounting heroic deeds; Chinese narrative, per Zheng Zhenduo, descends from **gossip and street talk** — less adventure, more day-to-day. Ming Dong Gu: organised on a principle *"different from the time-based, direction-oriented, and logically coherent principle of the Western narrative."*

> **"Plot is a statement about reality, a commentary on how life is lived."**

His example is unanswerable: *The Odyssey* is Odysseus's story, and **Penelope — twenty years of waiting, and 108 suitors managed by strategy — is left out.** Same house, same decades, no pyramid, and it is a story.

### What replaces it

**Tropisms.** Nathalie Sarraute's term, borrowed from biology: the almost imperceptible movements organisms make toward or away from what impinges on them. Her stories trace *"interior movements that precede and prepare our words and actions, at the limits of our consciousness."* Lidia Yuknavitch, on discovering her, started writing stories as **"a series of intensities."**

**Momentum, not arc.** Faulkner concedes every work needs momentum — but the author chooses its source. Direct action, or David Shields's *"subtle, progressive buildup of thematic resonances."* Image, mood, voice.

**Mystery as a plot in itself.** Maud Casey: *"If mystery, the genre, is about finding the answers, then mystery, that elusive yet essential element of fiction, is about finding the questions."*

## Where it shows in the gallery

**This chapter explains why [[The Serpent in the Grove — ANNOTATED|Nazir]] won while failing your architecture instrument.**

The Nine Axes score it **31/45**, losing on economy, close and controlling idea — all structural. Its climax lands at the midpoint and **45% of it is aftermath**, which is Freytag inverted. By the pyramid it is malformed.

By Faulkner's terms it is nothing of the kind. Sita learning to write her name — *the A came out like a little house with a crooked door, but it stood* — is a tropism. So is the shelf she builds inside herself for decisions she cannot afford to call freedom. So is Puttie learning to hear his mother *by the weight of her good foot and the mercy of her bad.* **The back half is not falling action. It is a series of intensities**, and it is what the chair called *deeply moving.*

**[[Dite — ANNOTATED]] has no pyramid at all.** Five sections, non-chronological, an object-narrator, and a climax that is anatomical rather than eventful — *the pleasure in my groin moves upward and explodes on my tongue, suddenly unsilenced.*

**[[Mothers Not Appearing in Search — ANNOTATED]] is the exception that keeps McKee honest.** It has a clean escalation — compound, compound again, stadium — a real climax at ~87%, and a genuine turn. **Two of three gallery stories ignore the pyramid; one obeys it beautifully.**

## Against the other bases

| | Position |
| --- | --- |
| **[[Story structure by robert mackee.md\|McKee]]** | Spine, acts, turning points, the gap, controlling idea. **Direct conflict** |
| **[[The Fichtean Curve\|Gardner]]** | Rising crises. Conflict, but Gardner's *[[Profluence — Resolution, Logical Exhaustion, and Emphasis vs Function\|profluence]]* — the sense of going somewhere — is compatible with yearning |
| **[[The Working Plot — The Skeleton Method\|Barrett]]** | Skeleton method, one incident. Conflict on method, **agreement on scarcity** |
| **[[Writing That Learns, Not Teaches\|Kiteley]]** | Ally. Learning beats teaching, and a pyramid is a story that already knows |
| **[[Illuminate Fear and Dread\|Cleland]]** | Ally on mystery-as-engine, opponent on continuous escalation |

> [!important] How to actually hold both
> **McKee is a diagnostic; Faulkner is a permission.** When something is broken, McKee finds it — the vault's own record shows the Nine Axes catching real faults. When something is *working but unshaped*, McKee will tell you to break it into acts and Faulkner will tell you not to.
>
> **The decision rule: does the story have yearning?** If yes and it lacks structure, leave it. If no, no amount of structure will supply it — and that is the failure the pyramid cannot detect, which is precisely Chantal's situation at 40/45.

## The exercise

Faulkner's is the honest one: take a fragmentary piece and **rewrite it into seven sentences, one per Freytag stage.** Then ask what it lost — mystery, oddness, room to think — and what it gained.

**Run it in both directions.** Take something architecturally sound and strip the connective tissue until only the intensities remain. Whichever version you keep, you will have learned which one the material wanted.
