# THE FULLNESS OF OMISSION

## The principle

> **"Omission is a form of creation."** — David Mamet, quoted by Faulkner

Not *cutting*. **Creating.** The distinction is the whole chapter. Mamet's genealogy: Chekhov removed the plot; Pinter removed the history and the narration; Beckett removed the characterisation. **"We hear it anyway."**

Faulkner's own framing is pruning, and it is better than it sounds: a pruner has to *inhabit* the tree — feel its wood, follow its energy, decide what is healthy — so subtraction is an act of intimacy with the thing, not violence against it. **You cannot prune a story you do not know the shape of.** Mary Robison rejected "minimalist" for **"subtractionist"** on exactly this ground.

Hemingway's iceberg is quoted in full, and the operative clause is the conditional: *if a writer knows enough about what he is writing about* he may omit it and the reader will feel it **as strongly as though it had been stated.** Omission only works on material the writer actually has. **Cutting what you never knew is not omission, it is absence**, and readers can tell the difference.

### The keyword: *ellipsis*

Faulkner's candidate for the single word describing the whole aesthetic. Etymologically **"to hide behind silence."** Also, in grammar, the omission of words *recoverable from context* — which makes it the exact technical name for the thing this vault keeps calling withholding.

His practical formulation, and it is the usable one:

> **"Mind the gaps"** — the gaps between words, between sentences, between paragraphs, and the gap around the story itself.

> [!important] The test that comes out of this chapter
> **Not *"what can I cut?"* but *"what does the reader assemble in the space I leave?"***
>
> If the answer is *nothing*, the cut was a deletion. If the answer is *something specific and wrong*, the surrounding material is underbuilt. If the answer is *something specific and right*, you have made a thing.

## Where it shows in the gallery

**[[Mothers Not Appearing in Search — ANNOTATED]] is the purest instance in the three.** Lubwama never states that Fatima sells sex, never states that Coach is coercing her, never states that the mother's rage is shame. Every one of those is assembled by the reader out of accurate reporting by a child who does not understand what he is reporting.

The load-bearing example is the list of men — *waited like I had when it was Dan, like I had when it was Hassan… when it was the electrician* — followed by **the exception**: *she hadn't told me to wait when it was Sumaiyah.* Six words that convert a list into a tally. **The information is entirely in the gap.**

**[[The Serpent in the Grove — ANNOTATED]]** withholds the one thing that would settle its moral question: what moved in Vishnu at the well. *Something moved in him* — and the story never says what, for the remaining half of its length.

**[[Dite — ANNOTATED]]** withholds Creole almost entirely for 70% and then spends it three times.

## Against the other bases

**[[The Art of Omission — Unity, Padding, and Proportion|Barrett, 1900]] said this first and said it harder.** Barrett's is a rule of proportion — cut what does not serve the single incident. Faulkner's is a rule of *effect* — cut what the reader can build better than you can state. **They are not the same instruction and both are right.**

**[[Reveal Answers Slowly|Cleland]]** is the tactical version: withholding as suspense, controlling *when* the reader learns. Faulkner is arguing something else — that some things should never be delivered at all.

**Tension with [[The Vivid and Continuous Fictional Dream|Gardner]].** Gardner wants the dream unbroken and continuous. Ellipsis *interrupts* it on purpose. Faulkner's answer is that the interruption is where the reader enters — but Gardner's warning stands: **an ellipsis the reader cannot bridge is not mystery, it is a hole.** The difference between the two is entirely in whether the surrounding material is sufficient.

## The exercise

Faulkner's, and all three are worth doing at any length:

1. **A story that is only a list.** (Cf. Chantal's inventory, which is already this.)
2. **An erasure.** Take any text, black out most of it, read what survives.
3. **A story only in dialogue.** *Hills Like White Elephants.*

Then the question he attaches: **did the story gain by subtraction, and what exactly did it gain?** If you cannot name the gain, restore the cut.
