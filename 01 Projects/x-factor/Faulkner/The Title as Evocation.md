# THE TITLE AS EVOCATION

## The principle

> **"Think of the title as an element of evocation, not description. Suggest, don't name."**

Faulkner's central test, and it is a real one:

> **A good title is one that is redefined by the story, and gains a layer of meaning every time you reread it.**

Not a label on a container. **A thing the story acts upon.** He notes this matters more the shorter the work, because *"there is more of an opportunity for the ending to reach back to the beginning"* — the two ends are close enough to touch.

The hazard he names is the one almost everyone has:

> **"Many a writer titles a story early in the process and then doesn't truly question the title. The working title unfortunately becomes the title not because it's good but because of inattention."**

Michael Martone's lovely framing: the title is a *"duty-free zone of existence, the realm of untaxable perfume and spirits"* — outside the word count, therefore free. In a 100-word story writers legitimately use it to buy room.

And the closing instruction, which is the whole chapter: **"Make your title unforgettable."** Faulkner adds that he almost never forgets a novel's title and has forgotten most short story titles he has read — which is the actual competitive problem.

> [!important] The three-question check
> 1. **Does the story redefine it?** If the title means the same thing on page 1 and on the last page, it is a label.
> 2. **Would you read a story with this title?** Asked cold, of a title in a list of twenty-five.
> 3. **Does it describe, or does it evoke?** If you can point at the thing in the story that the title names, it is probably describing.

## Where it shows in the gallery

**[[Mothers Not Appearing in Search — ANNOTATED|Lubwama's is the best title of the three and possibly the best in the campaign.]]**

*Mothers Not Appearing in Search.* It does everything at once:

- **It is the story's only statement of its own subject.** The prose never says the story is about a mother; the title does, once, from outside.
- **It is redefined by the ending** — a Facebook search returning *a thousand Fatimas, most of them white, none of them polytechnic.* The search that fails is literal, and the failure retroactively makes the title's *mothers* mean the woman he did not search for.
- **It requires the reader to do the substitution the story refuses to state.** The title says *mothers*; the last line searches for *Fatima*. **The reader has to close that gap, and closing it is the story.**
- It sounds like a systems error, which is a register nothing else in the story uses.

**[[The Serpent in the Grove — ANNOTATED|Nazir]] earns his title and then spends it.** *Serpent* arrives three times: as a simile for Vishnu's patience (28%), as one of the folk names for what he cannot name (*jumbie, duppy, serpent*, 51%), and inverted in Sita's mouth in church — *here the tree had kept truth and a man had lied* (76%). **That is the title being redefined three times.** Then the coda explains it — *the serpent in the grove was never only a snake* — which is the one move the chapter warns against.

**[[Dite — ANNOTATED|*Dite*]] withholds its own title until the last word of the story.** A Creole word, unglossed, spoken by a professor of French to a child who asked in Creole first. **The title is the ending**, and until you reach it the title is only a sound.

## Against the other bases

**[[Titles Good and Bad|Barrett]] is the direct ancestor** and the two agree almost completely — Barrett from 1900 warning against the flat descriptive title. Faulkner adds the **retroactive test**: not just *is it good*, but *does the story change it.*

**[[The Plot-Germ and the One-Sentence Test|Barrett's plot-germ]]** is the useful pairing. If you can state the story in one sentence, the title should be a *slant* on that sentence — never a summary of it.

**[[The Resonant Close|Gardner]]** requires every closing image to drag an earlier one behind it. **In all three gallery stories the title is one of the things dragged.** Treat the title as the earliest plant in the story.

**[[The First-Page Cut]]** — a panel reads twenty-five titles before it reads a first page. This is the only craft element that is *always* read.

## The exercise

Faulkner's is deliberately extreme: **write a title longer than its story, or a title that is the story.**

The version for work at this length:

1. **List ten alternative titles for a finished story**, without deleting the working one, and force at least three to come from the last 10% of the text rather than the first.
2. **For each, ask whether the story redefines it.**
3. Then set the list aside for a week and pick cold.

**A title chosen on day one and never re-examined is the default failure**, and it costs nothing to fix.
