# END TWO PARAGRAPHS EARLIER

## The principle

> **"I was once told that the best way to end a story is to end it two paragraphs earlier, so I always look at my endings and see how they read if a substantial chunk of them is taken away. It's interesting how in the attempt to write a good ending, we keep going further, writing more. I find it's as true as any writing advice I've ever received."**

The diagnosis inside that is the useful part: **the overlong ending is not a failure of judgement, it is a symptom of trying.** You write more *because* you are reaching for the ending. The reaching is what produces the excess.

The rest of the chapter supplies the standard:

**What an ending is for.** David Shields: **"retrospective redefinition"** — like the couplet closing a sonnet, it should turn the reader back on what they have just read and make them hold it differently.

**What it is not for.** Faulkner is severe about the twist: *"a supposedly clever paradox, an odd coincidence that fails to illuminate."* A story built toward a punch line *"functions like someone flicking off your cap as you walk down the hallway."* The belief that an ending must contain a dramatic reversal **"has always seemed overrated to me"** — a subtle reversal, a nuanced recognition, is preferable. Charles Baxter's formulation, borrowed: pursue a discovery that is **"an emergent precious thing."**

**And the definition worth memorising**, from Jayne Anne Phillips:

> **The last lines "should create a silence, a white space in which the reader breathes. The story enters that breath, and continues."**

Valeria Luiselli, quoted at the close: *"The end of things, the real end, is never a neat turn of the screw, never a door that is suddenly shut, but more like an atmospheric change… more a whimper than a bang."*

> [!important] The test, and it is mechanical
> **Delete the last two paragraphs. Read it. Then delete two more.**
>
> Do not decide. Just read all three versions cold, ideally aloud, ideally a week apart. Faulkner's claim is that the shortest one usually wins, and the reason it wins is that the material you added was **you searching for the ending in public.**

## The adjudication this note exists for

> [!danger] The Nazir coda is the campaign's most consequential open question, and three sources now agree
> [[The Serpent in the Grove — ANNOTATED|*The Serpent in the Grove*]] reaches a **5-scoring close** at ~95%:
>
> *"Because this place know things. And when a place know things, you talk soft so it can talk soft back."*
>
> **And then it continues for twenty more lines** — *Pass there soft… The serpent in the grove was never only a snake… A story is a well…* — abandoning narration for incantation and stating the controlling idea outright.
>
> | Source | Verdict |
> | --- | --- |
> | **[[Climax and Conclusion — The Story in Six Words\|Barrett, 1900]]** | End within a paragraph of the climax. **Broken, and inverted — 45% of the story is aftermath** |
> | **[[The Judging Rubric — Nine Axes]]** | Economy **2**, close **3**, controlling idea **3.** Three axes lost, all here |
> | **Faulkner, 2023** | End two paragraphs earlier. **The coda is the writer reaching in public** |
> | **[[Writing That Learns, Not Teaches\|Kiteley]]** | Teaching, not learning |
>
> **And it won, from 7,806 entries**, praised as *original, poetic and deeply moving.*
>
> **Hold both facts, and note which way the evidence runs.** [[14 The Judges — Six Years of Chair Statements|Six years of chair statements]] show no panel has ever praised a story for economy or for its close — **2 of 6, both glancing.** So the coda cost it three axes on an instrument the panel does not use, and cost it nothing with the panel.
>
> **Conclusion for anyone learning from this gallery: the two-paragraph rule is right about the prose and wrong about the prize.** Cutting Nazir's coda would produce a better story and would not have produced a different result. Know which of those you are optimising for.

## Where else it shows in the gallery

**[[Mothers Not Appearing in Search — ANNOTATED|Lubwama]] obeys the rule exactly.** The story ends on the failed search — *none of them polytechnic* — and **stops.** No commentary, no coda, no explanation of what the title means. Shields's *retrospective redefinition* delivered in a single clause.

**[[Dite — ANNOTATED|*Dite*]] ends on one word.** *Dite.* Everything the story has built — the throat, *tall*, the ledger, the altar, the four registers — is behind it, and none of it is named. **This is Phillips's definition met precisely: the last line creates a silence the reader breathes into.**

**Two of three stop on the image. One explains it. The one that explains it is the one that won** — which is why this note ends in a question rather than a rule.

## Against the other bases

**[[Climax and Conclusion — The Story in Six Words|Barrett]] and Faulkner are the same rule 123 years apart**, and that is the strongest cross-base agreement in the vault. Two writers who share no tradition, no era and no form arriving at *stop immediately after the climax.*

**[[The Resonant Close|Gardner]]** supplies what the ending must *do* — every closing image dragging an earlier one behind it. **Faulkner supplies where it must stop.** Use Gardner to build it and Faulkner to trim it.

**[[Internal Structure — Shapes for an Ending|Kiteley]]** offers alternative shapes; Faulkner offers the length test that applies to all of them.

**[[Closed Vs Opening Endings.md|McKee]]** frames it as a choice between resolution and irresolution. Faulkner's whole aesthetic leans irresolute — *"works of brevity often seek irresolution… stories that hang in the air, unfinished."*

## The exercise

Faulkner's, verbatim in effect: **take a finished story, end it two paragraphs earlier, and see what the resonance does.** Ask what you gained *by suggestion and by indeterminacy*.

Then the harder second pass: **find the sentence where you started reaching.** It is usually the first one after the true ending, and it is usually the one you are proudest of.
