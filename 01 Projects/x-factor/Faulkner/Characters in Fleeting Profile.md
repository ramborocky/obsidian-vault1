# CHARACTERS IN FLEETING PROFILE

## The principle

> **A character need only "fill the whole space of the situation."** — Milan Kundera, quoted by Faulkner

Not a person. **A presence sufficient to its moment.** Irving Howe's phrase gives the chapter its title: in short forms we see people only in *fleeting profile* — *"we hear a line of their flute solo but not the orchestra they play in."*

Faulkner's argument runs against a century of received wisdom, and it is more useful than it first looks:

**1 · Flatness is a technique, not a failure.** Via Kate Bernheimer on fairy tales: characters are *"silhouettes, mentioned simply because they are there,"* given perhaps one emotion, in no psychological conflict — and this flatness is desirable because it **"allows depth of response in the reader."** The vacuum is the point. Carmen Maria Machado's version: flash *"possesses missing details (the right missing details) and flatness (the right kind of flatness) that creates a vacuum that begs to be filled."*

**2 · Backstory is the enemy.** *"A detailed backstory is anathema to flash because you'll be tempted to stuff all of the more you've conjured into the story."* The dossier is not the problem; the temptation to spend it is.

**3 · The route in is difference, not description.** Not what they look like or how they walk — **the piercing detail that cuts against expectation.** His examples are deliberately silly and exactly right: the vampire who bites their fingernails; the superhero who mumbles and slumps.

**4 · And the sentence to keep:**

> **"Nurture a disgust of banality."** A complacent author creates complacent characters — *"content to exist in generalities, clichés, and stereotypes."*
>
> His test: **does anyone in the world experience their own existence as a stereotype?**

> [!important] The operational question at the end of the chapter
> **"If story is character and character is story, ask yourself what is the most vital character trait to tell the story?"**
>
> Singular. Not the list — the one.

## Where it shows in the gallery

**[[Mothers Not Appearing in Search — ANNOTATED|Lubwama]] runs the whole cast on single traits and every one of them lands.** The cobbler: *unkempt white beard*, greets a child like an agemate, **knows Jabulanis**. Three details, two scenes, completely alive. Nabongho exists to ask one question. Pinto exists to be run over and never return.

**And Fatima is the chapter's best possible exhibit.** We learn almost nothing about her — a polytechnic, a DVD rack, a scent, some braids, a laugh at a child asking whether coins are her savings. **She refuses to be either victim or predator**, and the refusal is achieved by *withholding*, not by rounding her out.

**[[Dite — ANNOTATED]] gives Dadi four sentences** — started picking tea at nine, picked twice as fast as her husband, earned more, *"twice as likely to lose her temper"* — and she is the most vivid person in the story. Nigel, by contrast, gets far more space and stays a type. **Length is not the variable.**

**[[The Serpent in the Grove — ANNOTATED|Nazir]] gives Sita a container image** — *she learned to make herself small, to take the shape of whatever container held her* — and then drops her down a well. One image, doing characterisation and foreshadowing at once.

## Against the other bases

**[[Character Dimension and Cast Design|McKee]] wants dimension** — contradiction, depth, capacity to surprise. **Faulkner says a single piercing trait can substitute.** Both true at different lengths, and the crossover is roughly where a character has to *change* rather than be *revealed*.

**[[Character by Salient Detail|Barrett]] is the direct ancestor** and Faulkner adds one thing to him: the detail should **cut against expectation**, not merely be characteristic.

**Real conflict with [[Interest, Free Will, and the Active Protagonist|Gardner]] and [[Active Vs Passive Protagonists.md|McKee on passive protagonists]].** Gardner's warning that a blank protagonist breaks a story is one of this vault's load-bearing rules. Faulkner's fairy-tale flatness looks like exactly the thing Gardner forbids.

> **The resolution is that flatness is legitimate for everyone except the person whose choice the story turns on.** Sita is flat in outline and *decisive* in action — she sends the child away, and that single act is why the story is not a victim-story. **Flatten the cast. Never flatten the chooser.**

**[[Show, Don't Tell — Needless Explanation|Gardner]] vs the fairy tale.** Bernheimer points out *"The Rosebud"* says *beautiful* and *lovely* without showing either, and works. **The exemption is narrow: it holds where the register is already folk or fabular.** In realist close narration it does not, and Gardner's rule stands.

## Where this feeds the instrument

**[[15 The Chair Test — Scoring the Hidden Factor]] axis 4 — The Person** — is the operational descendant of this chapter, with one addition Faulkner does not make:

> **A 5 requires that at least one character refuse the category the story could have put them in.** The villain who is not a villain. The woman the plot blames, given her own morning.

Chantal scores **3** here. Djamela is real; the mother is furniture, Jean Luc has no object, Morisho is a function. **Not one character refuses their category** — and Faulkner's chapter says the fix costs one detail per person, not a rewrite.

## The exercise

**"You're the kind of person who ___."** Seven sentences, each beginning that way, each revealing something *outside* expectation. Faulkner's claim is that if each escalates on the last you may end up with a story rather than a sketch.

Run it on the characters who are currently functions, not on the protagonist.
