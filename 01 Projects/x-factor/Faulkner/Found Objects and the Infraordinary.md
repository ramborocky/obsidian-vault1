# FOUND OBJECTS AND THE INFRAORDINARY

## The principle

> **"Anyone may 'find' a text; the poet is he who names it, 'Text.'"** — John Hollander, quoted by Faulkner

**The frame makes the story.** Faulkner's lineage runs Duchamp's urinal → Joseph Cornell's shadow boxes → Rauschenberg → the found-text story, and the point is constant: the transformation is done by *presentation*, not by alteration.

Cornell is the chapter's centre and the best model in it — a recluse in his mother's house in Queens who walked New York collecting *"cork balls, metal springs, old photographs, dime-store trinkets,"* filed them in labelled boxes, and assembled them into miniature theatres. A box marked *tinfoil* shelved beside one marked *Caravaggio, etc.* **He attached the highest value to objects of no intrinsic worth.** His own line: *"My boxes are life's experiences aesthetically expressed."*

### The infraordinary

Georges Perec's term, via Blanchot, and it is the most useful idea in the chapter. The everyday is *"what is most difficult to discover"* — it belongs to insignificance, and is therefore *"perhaps the site of all possible signification."* Perec's definition of the infraordinary:

> **"What's really going on, what we're experiencing, the rest, all the rest."**

**The rest, all the rest.** That is the leakage principle stated forty years before this vault measured it.

### Story as document

The chapter's practical half. A story can take the form of any text that exists in the world:

- a **recipe** (Leesa Cross-Smith's ingredients of girlhood)
- an **outline** (Czyzniejewski's divorce)
- a **dictionary entry** (Kathy Fish, *Collective Nouns for Humans in the Wild*)
- a **flow chart** (McElroy, *The Death of Your Son*)
- a **paint catalogue** (Ploetz — life told in Whites and Reds, from *Hospital Light* to *Empty Pillow*)
- a **404 error page**, a customer review, a B&B guest log, a Mad Lib

Lydia Davis builds from found emails, valuing *"awkward nonprofessional writing"* for combinations a practised writer would never produce. Annie Dillard's formulation of what happens when you frame found text: **"The original meaning remains intact, but now it swings between two poles."**

## Where it shows in the gallery

**[[Dite — ANNOTATED]]** — the four epigraphs, and the payoff that makes it more than decoration: the *boutikier sinwa*'s credit notebook at 13% returns at 92% as the **indenture ledger**, *"all columns and lists… taken from our old grocer's ledger,"* with *coolies borrowed on colonial contract* where the flour used to be. **A found document at both ends of a life.**

**[[Mothers Not Appearing in Search — ANNOTATED]]** — a Facebook search, as the last image and the title.

**[[The Serpent in the Grove — ANNOTATED]]** — Sita's cheap copybook with *a red line that was less a margin than a joke*, in which she writes three things a night and **never the well.** A found form used to record an omission.

## Against the other bases

**[[The Text|McKee's image systems]]** is the nearest relative — objects recurring with accreting meaning. Faulkner's difference: **the object can be the container of the story, not just a motif inside it.**

**[[All the Small Things — Grief Through the Minor Object]]** is this vault's existing statement of the smaller version — grief arriving through the minor object. **Faulkner and Perec give it a name and a politics:** the infraordinary is not a technique for delivering emotion, it is *"what's really going on."*

**[[Verisimilitude, Not Verity — The Use of Facts|Barrett]]** wants facts that persuade. Faulkner wants **facts that were already lying around**, and argues the not-invented quality is itself the effect.

**The Writing Constitution — *objects carry emotion*** — is the house rule this chapter extends. The extension: **an object can carry structure too.**

## The exercise

Faulkner's, and it takes twenty minutes: **go through your spam folder, your junk mail, an old newspaper.** He finds three usable things in one pass — an email opening *"Firstly let me apologise to you…"*, a sender called *Mr. Info*, a subject line about a plant mix that melts fat cells.

The better version for work at this length: **find the document your story's world would actually produce** — a tariff, a charge sheet, a school rule, a price list, a clinic leaflet, a ledger — and set it above a scene without comment. Then check whether it can be made to *know something the narrator does not.*
