**What I did differently, in one paragraph, and then I'll get out of the way.** The first pass was assembled — I mapped devices out of the benchmarks into a table and executed them, and it came out 2,551 words with a thin world and the worst long-sentence figure on the board. This time I wrote it and let the instruments stay in the drawer until afterwards. The one thing I took deliberately from *Dite* is how it handles a language: it withholds Creole almost entirely and then spends the same four words three times, in three mouths, meaning three different things, and never remarks on it. So there is one Lingala phrase in here that does that, and I am not going to say which or where.

# MABANGA

### *Claude, second pass, 2026-07-31. Free invention.*

> **LIBANGA SIMPLE — 50 $.** Le nom une fois, dans le sebene, sans adjectif. On paie avant le studio.

There is a place in a song where everything is happening at once and I can hear one thing inside it.

The guitar is going and it does not stop, that is the whole point of a sebene, the guitar goes until the man decides. Under it the drum. Over it three men shouting, and over them a crowd shouting that was recorded somewhere else on a different day. And somewhere in all of that, for less than a second, the atalaku throws a name.

Most people hear noise.

I hear Ferdinand Mukendi.

I have been able to do it since I was nine, and I did not know it was strange until Tonton laughed and made me do it again in front of four adults. Now people test me. At weddings they put a phone in my face and say Merveille, what did he say there, and I tell them, and they look at each other, and I am pleased with myself for about two hours.

Josué cannot do it and he is seventeen. He cannot even find the drum. He claps on one and three like a Protestant.

---

> **LIBANGA AVEC TITRE — 100 $.** Le nom avec *grand* devant. Deux fois. Le grand doit être content.

We are at Ngaba, past the roundabout, third parcelle after the tap.

The tap has not worked since before Josué was born, which does not stop Maman Bijoux from standing beside it most mornings explaining the schedule to people who have not asked. There is a schedule. Nobody has seen it. Maman Bijoux has seen it.

Our courtyard is cement that somebody poured badly a long time ago, so there is a low place near the wall where the water sits for two days after rain and the mosquitoes come and make their arrangements. My mother has asked the landlord about it twice in six years. She does not ask a third time about anything.

At six the charcoal smoke comes up the street. At seven the SNEL takes our side and leaves the other, and the Église du Réveil up the road has a generator, so on Wednesdays we get Pasteur Nkulu whether we are going or not. He shouts about the spirit of poverty. He shouts it into a microphone that cost more than everything in our house except the speaker.

My mother is called Gloire. Everyone says Maman Gloire, even people older than her, which is a thing I did not notice until Grâce pointed it out and then I could not stop noticing it.

She plaits hair in the courtyard from seven in the morning. She is the fastest on this street and she is not the cheapest, and she will explain the connection between those two facts to anyone who has twenty minutes. Women come from Kalamu for her.

Here is what they are actually paying for and none of them knows it. **She does not talk while she works.** Every other woman on this road talks. My mother puts a client in the chair and takes her head in both hands and says nothing for two hours, and the client sits there and something comes out of her that she did not bring with her, and at the end she pays and says ah maman, and comes back in three weeks.

She keeps the money in her bra and counts it twice, once when it goes in and once at night, and both times her lips move.

Papa is in Lubumbashi. He has been in Lubumbashi four years. He sends something in July and something in December and Maman puts it against the écolage and does not discuss it, and July was thin this year, and I know that because I count things.

---

> **LIBANGA CHAUD — 200 $.** Trois fois. Et l'atalaku doit avoir l'air de te connaître.

Tonton Chicago is my mother's younger brother and he is an atalaku.

His name is Bernard. Nobody has called him Bernard since before I was born, including Maman, who calls him Chicago while she is insulting him, which I think should count for something.

He is a small man with a very large voice and one good shirt. He has been in four orchestres and left three of them in a way that means he cannot go back. He invented a cri that went round Kinshasa for a whole season — you have heard it, everyone has heard it — and he was not paid for it, and he will tell you about that, and you should let him, because he tells it well and it gets better each year.

He never brings money. He brings sugar. Once he brought a fan that did not work and stood there while my mother thanked him for it.

Maman says he is thirty-six and has never held on to a thing for one week in his life. She says it in front of him. He laughs and agrees and eats her madesu with his fingers and asks for more.

Now. The trade, since you do not know it.

A big man wants his name inside a song. Not on the cover — inside, in the sebene, shouted, so the whole quartier hears it in the taxi and in the nganda and at the wedding of somebody they have never met. He pays. The atalaku throws it in.

That is a libanga. It means a stone. You throw a stone into the song.

Nobody is ashamed of it. Tonton says it is the only honest thing in Congolese music because the price is agreed before, which is more than you can say for the record company, and then he says the name of the record company, and then my mother tells him to lower his voice because the neighbours have not asked for his career.

The men who buy are transporteurs, and men with depots at Limete, and men who have come back from Dubai with a watch, and men who want one particular woman to hear their name in a song and understand what kind of man has his name in a song.

I have known the tariff since I was eleven. It is not written anywhere. Everybody has it.

---

> **LIBANGA DÉDICACE — 350 $.** Le nom du client **et** un deuxième nom, l'un après l'autre, pour que les gens comprennent.

The first time my name went into a song I was thirteen and a half and I did not stop talking for two days.

Tonton did it as a joke. The studio is a room above the pharmacy at Matonge with a mattress against the window for the sound and a fan that they turn off when they record, so after twenty minutes it is a room with four men in it and no air. He put me in the corner and told me not to breathe. Then in the sebene he threw *Merveille* twice.

Nothing else. No adjective. My name going past in a song like a taxi.

I made him play it back eleven times. I counted. He said if I asked for twelve he would throw Josué instead.

When we came down onto the avenue it was that hour when the light goes orange and the whole of Victoire is shouting, and I remember thinking that every person walking past me was going to hear my name that week and not one of them would know it was me, and that this was the best thing that had ever happened to anybody.

I told Grâce at school. Grâce said prove it. I said I cannot prove it, it is a song. She said then it did not happen. Grâce is my best friend and she is like this and I have decided to keep her.

---

> **LIBANGA CADEAU — gratuit.** L'atalaku jette un nom qu'on ne lui a pas payé. Pour la famille. Pour fâcher quelqu'un. **Ou pour faire connaître un nom.**

After that it kept happening.

*Merveille* in the sebene of a thing that played at the nganda on Lumumba all through the dry season. *Merveille* in one that a taxi driver had so loud that Grâce and I heard it from the road, and I hit her arm, and she did not say anything about proving it that time.

I asked Tonton why and he said because the client pays.

I said which client. He said a grand. I said which grand, and he did a thing with his hand that means the conversation is over, and then he ate.

I want to be fair, because I have not always been. I did not push it. I did not push it because I did not want the answer to be that it was a mistake, or that it was a different Merveille — there are three Merveilles in my class alone, and one of them is a boy. I wanted it to be mine.

So I stopped asking. That was a decision and I made it and I would like it written down somewhere that I made it.

---

At the tap that week, Maman Bijoux was explaining the schedule, and I said the water came Tuesday now, not Thursday, because I had counted, and a man behind her with two jerricans said, *aza mwana*, and everybody laughed and went back to talking about the schedule.

I was right about Tuesday. Nobody has ever mentioned it.

---

> **LIBANGA MOKONZI — 500 $.** Le nom seul, sans musique. Le solo s'arrête. Tout s'arrête. Puis ça repart.

The grand had a Prado, green, always clean, which in this city is a whole sentence about a man.

I saw him twice and both times he stayed in the car and Tonton went down. The second time he put his arm out of the window while they talked and I saw the arm and the watch and part of a blue shirt, and I did not see his face, and it did not occur to me until much later that a car window is exactly the height of a face.

He is from Limete. He has a depot. Tonton calls him Mopao.

That second time I was up in the room with the mattress and the door was open onto the stairs, and their voices came up, and I was not listening because I was working the headphones — they are too big for my head, they have always been too big, you have to hold one side on with your hand.

Then Tonton said something in a voice I had not heard him use. Three words. He said them quietly and then he said them again, not quietly.

The grand did not answer. Or he answered so low it did not come up the stairs.

They talked for a while after that about something else, and then I heard the car door, and then Tonton came up the stairs counting, and he put a hundred dollars folded small into my hand and said buy something for your mother, and his ears were red, which happens to him, and I thought it was the heat.

I bought a phone. It was ninety-two dollars, it was a Tecno, and I told my mother I had found the money, which is the stupidest sentence I have said in my life so far.

She took it and turned it over and looked at the back of it. Not the screen. The back.

Then she gave it to me and went and finished the head she was working on and did not speak at dinner, and Josué asked what was wrong with everybody and nobody answered him, and he ate two plates.

---

> **LIBANGA SÉRIE.** On place plusieurs noms dans le même sebene, l'un après l'autre, à la file. Prix à discuter.

At the beginning of the rains my mother took the wire out of the speaker.

We have a speaker in the courtyard for when she works, the big one with the handle, and it plays from a phone, and it has been on at seven in the morning every day of my life. She pulled the wire out and put it in the cupboard where she keeps the pagnes, under everything.

I said Maman, the ladies like it.

She said the ladies can talk.

I said everybody has music. Even the church has music.

She said this is my parcelle.

For three weeks she plaited in silence and the women asked and she said the speaker was broken, and one of them said her son could fix it, and my mother said thank you, no.

I was so angry I could not look at her. I had a whole explanation worked out. Her brother was doing something in the world and she was in a courtyard with a comb, and she could not stand my name being in a place where hers was not.

I said something close enough to that for her to hear it.

She put the comb down, which she does not do, and went inside, and stayed there a while, and came out and picked it up and carried on with the head.

I have gone over that afternoon a great many times.

---

Bénédiction lived two parcelles down. She was sixteen. She had a laugh you could hear from the roundabout and she was the one who taught me the ndombolo step that Grâce still cannot do, the one where you have to be lazy in the shoulders.

In August she went to Dubai.

That is what was said. *Elle est partie à Dubaï.* Her aunt said it, and the women in the courtyard said it, and it was said in the voice people use for good news that is very slightly too good, and everybody agreed she was lucky, and one woman said she had always been a serious girl, and another one said mm.

Her mother came to us for her hair three weeks later and sat in the chair and did not say one word for two hours, which was normal, because nobody talks in my mother's chair.

I was pleased for Bénédiction. I would like that written down as well.

---

In December there was a wedding at Kimbanseke and Josué took me because there was food.

There was a boy there with a good phone and a speaker, playing things nobody else had, the way boys with good phones do. He put on a long one. Ten minutes, twelve. It went into the sebene and stayed there and I was eating and not listening.

Then I was listening, because I had heard my name.

I put the plate down and went and stood next to the speaker with my head turned, the way I do, and Josué said something to me and I did not answer him.

The atalaku was doing a série. One after another, on the beat, no break, the way you read a list out to somebody who is writing it down.

Nineteen. I counted, because that is what I do.

Four of them were girls.

I knew the third one and I knew the seventh one and I knew the eleventh one, which was me.

The fifteenth one was Bénédiction, and Bénédiction has been in Dubai since August.

The guitar went on and on, because a sebene does not stop, that is the entire point of a sebene, it goes until the man decides.

Josué came and stood next to me and asked what was wrong. He is seventeen. He cannot find the drum.

I said nothing. I said it is nothing. It is a song.

---

I looked for it for four months.

It is not on YouTube. I have looked with my data and with Grâce's and with a boy at school who is supposed to be good at this. It has no title anybody agrees on. It was never released, there is no cover, and when I ask, people say ah yes, that one, and then they cannot help me.

It lives on phones. Somebody sent it to somebody on WhatsApp and it went from there, and every time it goes it gets smaller and worse, and by now the version you would find has been through so many phones that even I cannot pull the names out of it.

I have the third and the seventh and the eleventh, which is me.

I do not have the fifteenth any more. I know it was there. I counted it. But I have listened to what I can find maybe two hundred times, and at that place in the série there is a noise now and I cannot make it be a name, and I am the best on this street at making it be a name.

Tonton came in April and brought sugar.

I asked him for the name of the grand at Limete and he did the thing with his hand. I asked again. I did not stop this time. I asked four times, and on the fourth my mother came out of the house with the comb still in her hand and stood in the doorway and looked at her brother and said three words.

They were the same three words he had said on the stairs at Matonge.

He left the sugar on the step and went up the road, and she watched him go the whole way to the corner, which takes a while.

---

> **CHEZ MAMAN GLOIRE — TRESSES.** Petites 5 000. Grosses 3 000. Rajout non compris. Enfants moitié prix. On paie avant, jamais après.

She put the wire back in the speaker last week.

She did not say anything about it and neither did I. It plays from seven while she works, and the women come from Kalamu, and when a sebene starts she reaches over without looking and turns it down — not off, down — and then up again after, and she has never once explained this and I have never once asked.

Last night she called me and sat me on the low stool in front of her, between her knees, the way she used to when I was small, and took the matchstick with the cotton wound on the end of it and cleaned my ears.

I am fourteen. I am much too old for this.

She turned my head with two fingers under my jaw, the way she turns a client's head, and did one side and then the other, and did not talk, because she does not talk while she is working.

I sat there and let her, and outside the Église du Réveil was going, and Pasteur Nkulu was shouting about the spirit of poverty, and it carried all the way down the hill on the generator he bought with it.

## Afterwards — short, and honest

I am not going to score this against the rubric line by line, because you asked me not to and because I think doing that to it now would be the same mistake I made the first time. Two notes only, both of them things I did on purpose and one of them a thing I am not sure about.

**The mother is the story and she is never explained.** The speaker wire, the money counted twice with her lips moving, the third question she does not ask the landlord, the fact that she does not talk while she works and that this is what women are actually paying for — and then, at the end, her own price list in exactly the format of the tariffs that have been running above every section. Hers is the only honest one in the story. Nobody says so. If that lands, it is the whole thing; if it doesn't, nothing else here rescues it.

**The Lingala.** Three words, three times, three mouths — a man at the tap who is careless, Tonton on the stairs who is not, and Gloire in the doorway who ends something with them. Merveille reports all three accurately and understands none of them, and I never gloss it. A reader with no Lingala should still feel the third one land, because by then the shape has been laid twice. That is straight out of *Dite* and I make no claim to have invented it — what *Dite* does with *bour to sime ale* across three generations is better than what I have done here, and it is worth going back to that annotation and reading the three placements together.

**One measurement, after the fact, because it caught me.** 3,006 words against the first pass's 2,551 — so more room, and about ten pieces of true surplus instead of eight: Maman Bijoux and the schedule nobody has seen, the badly poured cement and the mosquitoes' arrangements, the landlord asked twice in six years, the fan that did not work, Josué's two plates, the step where you have to be lazy in the shoulders. **And sentences over thirty words went from 14.7% to 19.5%** — worse, and the worst figure on the board.

I documented this exact trade myself in [[The X-Factor]] — *a digression is a long sentence* — and then walked straight into it again. Some of it I stand behind: the sentence about my mother taking a client's head in both hands is fifty-five words on purpose, because it is enacting two hours of silence, and the sebene sentences run on because a sebene runs on. The rest is me not noticing. **I am not going to "fix" it by chopping, because *Dite* runs 17.5 with 20% short sentences and is better than either of these** — but you should know the number, and you should know I produced it while believing I was being careful.

**The thing I am unsure about:** the wedding scene may be too quiet. In the first pass I had her counting nineteen names and it read as a discovery. Here I have tried to let the guitar carry it — *it goes until the man decides*, which is the sentence I actually wrote the story toward — and I think the ending of that section is right. But if the list goes past you on a first read, then the whole story fails on the axis that matters most, and I would rather you tell me it failed than have me pretend otherwise.
