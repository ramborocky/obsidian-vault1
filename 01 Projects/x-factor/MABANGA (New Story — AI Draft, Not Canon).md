## The constraint, and how it was actually handled

You set the hard one: **a 48-year-old man writing a 14-year-old girl.**

That is not a costume, it is a failure mode, and it fails in four known ways. A middle-aged man writing a girl tends to (1) describe her body from outside her, (2) make her knowing — a child with an adult's irony about her own danger, (3) make her nothing but her danger, so she has no appetites the plot does not need, and (4) give her a vocabulary she would not have. **Every one of those is a craft error before it is anything else**, and Gardner names three of them.

So the discipline was: **she is never looked at. She is only ever looking.** She is funny. She is vain about the right things and blind about the wrong ones. She is *the most competent person in the story at one specific skill*, and the reader is not as good at it as she is. And she wants four things that have nothing whatever to do with what is happening to her.

### What was taken from each benchmark

| From **Dite** | How it is used here |
| --- | --- |
| **Instruction-as-epigraph** — each section opened by a tea recipe in the trade's own flat voice | Each section opens with a line from **the libanga price list.** Same device, and it does more work: **the tariff knows what the narrator does not** |
| One body-motif carrying the idea across every section — *the throat* | **The ear.** Hers is the best in the parcelle. It is her gift, her income and, in the last section, the instrument of her comprehension |
| A physical measurement paying off inside an archival document — *Height: 3 feet 2 inches* | **A count.** She counts the names, because counting is what her ear does |
| Ends on one untranslated word in the mother tongue — *dite* | Ends on **mabanga** |

| From **Mothers Not Appearing in Search** | How it is used here |
| --- | --- |
| **The opening obscenity the child reports innocently** — condoms as balloons | The first name she ever pulled from a song, at nine, said proudly, and what that name was |
| The narrator's affection pointed at exactly the wrong adult | Tonton Chicago, whom she loves, and who is not the villain, which is worse |
| The mother read as the antagonist for most of the story | Maman unplugs the speaker and will not say why, and Merveille thinks it is jealousy |
| **Ends on a failed search** — a thousand Fatimas, none of them polytechnic | The song is not on YouTube. It was never released. It exists only on people's phones |
| Comedy braided all the way through a story about a woman's humiliation | Kept. There are real jokes in here and they are load-bearing |

**One thing taken from neither, and it is the reason the form works:** *Dite*'s recipes are neutral. **This tariff is not.** It is the antagonist, printed at the top of each section in plain commercial language, describing precisely what is being done to her, while she narrates underneath it not understanding. The reader reads the price list. Merveille never does.

# MABANGA

### *Claude, 2026-07-31. Free invention. Not canon.*

> **LIBANGA SIMPLE — 50 $.** Le nom une fois. Dans le sebene. Sans adjectif. On paie avant le studio, jamais après.

I can hear a name inside a sebene when nobody else can.

That is not a small thing, so let me explain it properly. In a sebene there is the solo guitar going and going and not stopping, and the drum under it, and three or four men shouting at the same time, and on the recording there is a crowd shouting on top of the men. Somewhere in all of that the atalaku throws a name. It takes less than one second. It is not sung. It is thrown.

Most people hear a noise.

I hear Ferdinand Mukendi.

I have been able to do it since I was nine and I did not know it was unusual until Tonton laughed at me in front of four adults and made me do it again. Now people test me. They put a phone in my face at a wedding and say, Merveille, what did he say there, and I tell them, and they say ah, and look at each other, and I am pleased with myself for about two hours.

My brother Josué cannot do it and he is seventeen. He cannot even hear the drum properly. He claps on one and three like a Protestant.

---

> **LIBANGA AVEC TITRE — 100 $.** Le nom avec *grand* devant. Deux fois. Le grand doit être content.

We live at Ngaba, past the roundabout, in the third parcelle after the tap that has not worked since before Josué was born.

Maman plaits hair in the yard from seven in the morning. She is the fastest in our street and she is not the cheapest and she will tell you the connection between those two facts if you have twenty minutes. Women come from Kalamu for her. She does the small ones tight and she does not talk while she works, which women pay extra for without knowing that is what they are paying for.

Papa is in Lubumbashi. He has been in Lubumbashi for four years. He sends something in July and something in December and Maman puts it towards the écolage and does not discuss it.

Tonton Chicago is Maman's younger brother and he is an atalaku.

His real name is Bernard. Nobody has called him Bernard since before I was born, including Maman, who calls him Chicago while she is insulting him, which I think should count for something.

He is a small man with a very large voice and one good shirt. He has been in four orchestres and left three of them badly. He can do the cri that goes *tshouku-tshouku* and he invented one that went around Kinshasa for a whole season in a song he was not paid for, and he will tell you that too, and you should let him.

When he comes to us he brings sugar. Never money. Sugar, and once a fan that did not work.

Maman says he is thirty-six years old and has never in his life kept a thing in his hand for one week. She says it in front of him. He laughs and agrees and eats her madesu.

---

> **LIBANGA CHAUD — 200 $.** Trois fois. Et l'atalaku doit avoir l'air de te connaître.

Here is the trade, since you do not know it.

A big man wants his name inside a song. Not on the cover. Inside, in the sebene, shouted, where the whole quartier will hear it in the taxi and in the nganda and at the wedding of somebody they do not know.

He pays. The atalaku throws it in. That is a libanga. A stone.

You throw a stone into the song.

That is all it is. Nobody is ashamed of it and nobody pretends it is art. Tonton says a libanga is the only honest thing in the music business because the price is agreed in advance, which is more than you can say for the record company, and then he says the name of the record company, and then Maman tells him to lower his voice because the neighbours have not asked to hear about his career.

The men who buy are transporteurs, and men with depots at Limete, and men who have come back from Dubai, and men who want a particular woman to hear their name in a song and understand that he is the kind of man whose name is in a song.

I know the tariff by heart. I have known it since I was eleven. It is not a secret. It is printed on nothing but everybody has it.

---

> **LIBANGA DÉDICACE — 350 $.** Le nom du client **et** un deuxième nom, ensemble, l'un après l'autre, pour que les gens comprennent.

The first time my name went into a song I was thirteen and a half and I did not stop talking for two days.

Tonton did it as a joke. It was a small studio at Matonge above the pharmacy, four of them in a room with a mattress against the window for the sound, and he put me in the corner and told me not to breathe. Then in the sebene he threw *Merveille* twice.

Nothing else. No adjective. Just my name, going past, in a song, like a taxi.

I made him play it back eleven times. I counted. He said if I made him do twelve he would throw Josué instead.

When we came out onto the avenue at Victoire it was that hour when the light goes orange and everything is loud, and I remember thinking that everybody walking past me was going to hear my name that week and not one of them would know it was me, and that this was the best thing that had ever happened to anybody.

I told Grâce at school. Grâce said, prove it. I said I cannot prove it, it is a song. She said then it did not happen. Grâce is my best friend and she is like this and I have decided to keep her anyway.

---

> **LIBANGA CADEAU — gratuit.** L'atalaku jette un nom qu'on ne lui a pas payé. Pour la famille. Pour fâcher quelqu'un. **Ou pour faire connaître un nom.**

After that it kept happening.

Not every song. But often. *Merveille* in the sebene of a thing that played at the nganda on Boulevard Lumumba all through the dry season. *Merveille* in one that a taxi driver had so loud that Grâce and I heard it from the road, and I hit her arm, and she did not say anything about proving it that time.

I asked Tonton why he kept doing it and he said because the client pays.

I said which client.

He said a grand.

I said which grand, Tonton, and he did a thing with his hand that means the conversation has finished, and then he ate.

I want to be fair, because I have not always been. I did not push it. I did not push it because I did not want the answer to be that it was a mistake, or that it was somebody else's Merveille, and there are three Merveilles in my school alone. I wanted it to be mine. So I stopped asking, which was a decision, and I made it, and I would like that written down.

---

> **LIBANGA MOKONZI — 500 $.** Le nom seul, sans musique derrière, pendant une seconde. Le solo s'arrête. Tout s'arrête. Puis ça repart.

The grand had a Prado, green, very clean, and I only ever saw him in it.

He came to the studio twice while I was there and both times he stayed in the car and Tonton went down. The second time he put his arm out of the window and I saw the arm and the watch and part of the shirt, and I did not see the face, and it did not occur to me until much later that this was not an accident, because a car window is exactly the height of a face.

He is from Limete. He has a depot. Tonton calls him Mopao.

Once Tonton came back up the stairs counting and put a hundred dollars into my hand, folded small, and said buy something for your mother.

I bought a phone. It was ninety-two dollars and it was a Tecno and I told Maman I had found the money, which is the stupidest sentence I have ever said out loud in my life, and I have thought about it since.

She took the phone and looked at it for a long time. Not at the screen. At the back of it.

Then she gave it back to me and said nothing, and went and finished a client's head, and did not speak to me at dinner.

---

> **LIBANGA SÉRIE.** On place plusieurs noms dans le même sebene, l'un après l'autre, à la file. Prix à discuter.

At the beginning of the rains Maman unplugged the speaker.

We have a speaker in the yard for when she works, a big one with a handle, and it plays from a phone, and it has been on every morning of my life at seven o'clock. She took the wire out of it and put the wire in the cupboard where she keeps the pagnes.

I said Maman, the ladies like it.

She said the ladies can talk.

I said everybody has music, even the church has music.

She said this is my parcelle.

For three weeks she plaited hair in silence and the women asked about it and she said the speaker was broken, and one of them said her son could fix it, and Maman said thank you, no.

I was so angry I could not look at her. I decided she was jealous. I had worked it out completely — I had a whole explanation, and it was that her brother was doing something in the world and she was in a yard with a comb, and that she could not stand my name being somewhere hers was not.

I said something like that to her. Not exactly that. But near enough that she knew what I had said.

She did not answer. She put down the comb, which she does not do, and she went inside, and she came out again after a while and picked it up and carried on.

I have gone over that afternoon many times.

---

> **On ne rembourse pas un libanga.** Une fois jeté, c'est jeté.

Bénédiction lived two parcelles down. She was sixteen. She had a laugh you could hear from the roundabout and she was the one who taught me the *ndombolo* step that Grâce still cannot do properly.

In August she went to Dubai.

That is what was said. Elle est partie à Dubaï. Her aunt said it, and the women in the yard said it, and it was said in the tone people use for good news that is slightly too good, and everybody agreed she was lucky, and one woman said she had always been a serious girl and another one said mm.

Her mother did not say anything at all. Her mother came to Maman for her hair three weeks later and sat in the chair and did not say one word for two hours and Maman did not put on any music, and I thought that was because the wire was still in the cupboard.

I remember I was pleased for Bénédiction. I want that written down as well.

---

> **MABANGA** — pluriel de *libanga.* Pierre. On la jette dans la chanson. Où elle tombe, ce n'est pas ton affaire.

In December there was a wedding at Kimbanseke and Josué took me because there was food.

There was a boy there with a good phone and a speaker and he was playing things nobody had, the way boys with good phones do, and he put on a long one, ten minutes, twelve, and it went into the sebene and it stayed in the sebene, and I was eating and not listening.

Then I was listening.

Because I had heard my name.

I put down the plate and went and stood next to the speaker like a fool, close, with my head turned, the way I do, and Josué said something to me and I did not answer him.

The atalaku was doing a série. One after another, on the beat, no music stopping, the way you read a list.

I counted them, because that is what I do. There were nineteen.

Four of them were girls I know.

I knew the third one, and I knew the seventh one, and I knew the eleventh one, which was me.

And I knew the fifteenth one, which was Bénédiction, and Bénédiction has been in Dubai since August.

I stood there and the guitar went on and on and did not stop, because a sebene does not stop, that is the entire point of a sebene, it goes until the man decides.

Josué came and stood beside me and asked what was wrong. He is seventeen and he cannot hear the drum.

I said nothing. I said it is nothing, it is a song.

---

I looked for it for four months.

It is not on YouTube. I have looked with Grâce's data and with my own and I have looked with a boy at school who is supposed to be good at this. It has no title that anybody agrees on. It was never released and there is no cover and nobody knows the name of the orchestre, and when I ask, people say ah yes, that one, and then they cannot help me.

It exists on phones. That is where it lives. Somebody sent it to somebody on WhatsApp and it went from there and every time it goes it gets smaller and worse, and by now the version you would find has been through so many phones that even I cannot pull the names out of it.

I have the third one and the seventh one and the eleventh one, which is me.

I do not have the fifteenth one any more. I know it was there. I counted it. But I have listened to what I can find perhaps two hundred times, and at that place in the série there is now only a noise, and I cannot make it be a name, and I am the best in this parcelle at making it be a name.

Tonton came in April and brought sugar.

I asked him for the name of the grand at Limete and he did the thing with his hand.

I asked again. I did not stop asking this time. I asked him four times and on the fourth time Maman came out of the house with the comb still in her hand and stood in the doorway and looked at her brother, and did not say one word to him, and he left the sugar and went.

---

Maman took the wire out of the cupboard last week and put it back in the speaker.

She did not say anything about it and neither did I. She plaits from seven and it plays and the women come from Kalamu, and when a sebene starts she reaches over without looking and turns it down, not off, down, and then up again after, and she has never once explained this and I have never once asked.

I have not told her that I know. I have decided not to and I would like that written down too, next to the other things I decided.

Here is what I have instead, which is not much.

At Ngaba, past the roundabout, there is a gutter that runs when it rains and boys throw stones into it because there is nothing else to do with stones. The water is brown and moving and you cannot see anything under it. The stone goes in and there is a sound and then there is not a sound.

Where it lands is not your business.

That is the whole of the tariff and I knew it by heart when I was eleven.

Mabanga.

## Craft notes

### The engine, and where it is loaded

Both your benchmarks run on **the reader knowing before the narrator does.** Neither states its subject.

- Lubwama never once writes that Fatima is a sex worker or that Coach is coercing her. He writes *waited like I had when it was Dan, like I had when it was Hassan, like I had when it was the electrician* — a list a ten-year-old would genuinely make — and *tell her to reconsider*, and the reader assembles it.
- *Dite* never writes that the narrator was silenced by colonial schooling. It writes a throat, six times, over thirty years, and lets the reader do it.

Here the load is carried by **the price list**, and that is the one structural idea in this file worth stealing. *Dite*'s recipes are neutral instructions. **This tariff is the antagonist speaking in a commercial register**, and it is placed above the section it explains:

| The tariff says | Merveille, underneath, says |
| --- | --- |
| *pour que les gens comprennent* | She is thirteen and a half and cannot stop talking for two days |
| **Ou pour faire connaître un nom** | She stops asking who the client is, and knows she is stopping |
| *Le nom seul. Tout s'arrête.* | She describes an arm in a car window and not a face |
| *plusieurs noms, à la file* | Her mother unplugs the speaker and she decides it is jealousy |
| *Une fois jeté, c'est jeté* | Bénédiction goes to Dubai and she is pleased for her |

**The reader has the price list. Merveille never reads it.** She has known it by heart since she was eleven and has never once applied it to herself — which is the last line's actual job.

### The 48/14 problem, handled as craft

- **She is never described.** There is no sentence in the story that looks at her. Not her body, not her face, not her clothes. The one time a face could have been looked at, it is *his* — and the story notes that a car window is exactly the height of a face, which is the only time the machinery is visible, and it is her noticing it too late.
- **She is the expert and the reader is not.** Nobody reading this can pull a name out of a sebene. She can. That inverts the usual position of an adult writing a child, where the child is the one being explained.
- **Four appetites that serve nothing:** Grâce, who is annoying and kept anyway. Being right about Josué's clapping. The Tecno. Being pleased with herself for about two hours. Cut every one and the plot does not notice.
- **No irony about her own danger.** She is retrospective — *I have gone over that afternoon many times* — but the retrospection buys her regret about her **mother**, never insight about the men. Give a fourteen-year-old narrator adult insight into her own exploitation and you have written a thirty-year-old.

### Where the leakage is

The [[The X-Factor|surplus test]]: cut it, does the plot notice?

The tap that has not worked since before Josué was born · Maman's twenty-minute theory about price and speed · Chicago's one good shirt and the fan that did not work · the mattress against the studio window · the cri that went round Kinshasa for a season, unpaid · the woman whose son could fix the speaker · Grâce demanding proof of a song · the boy at the wedding with the good phone, doing what boys with good phones do.

Eight. None of them serve the plot.

### Deliberate refusals

**No violence on the page and no hotel.** Barrett's one-incident rule, and the harder reason: the story is about a *name* being moved, not a body. Showing the body would answer the question the form is built to leave standing.

**No confrontation with Tonton Chicago.** He is not a villain and the story never gets to say what he is. He brings sugar. He does the hand thing. Maman stands in a doorway with a comb and says nothing, and he goes — **and that is the only judgement passed on him in the whole story**, which is Lubwama's method exactly: the mother acts, and the child records the act without decoding it.

**No translation, anywhere.** *Sebene, atalaku, libanga, mabanga, cri, nganda, madesu, écolage, parcelle, ndombolo, Mopao, grand.* The tariff blocks stay in French. A reader who does not have French gets the numbers and the shape, which is enough, and the not-quite-getting is the point.

**Elle est partie à Dubaï** is the only sentence in the story doing two jobs at once, and it is not glossed and must never be.

**Two honest faults.**

**1 · It is half the length of either benchmark**, and that is where the leakage is thin. Lubwama can afford Pinto's femur, Jjaja Reagan's porch and the driving school's whole negotiated truce because he has 5,112 words. Eight surplus details in 2,551 is a respectable density but a small absolute quantity, and **the world here is narrower than the world in either benchmark.** If this were taken further, length is what it needs — not more plot, more parcelle.

**2 · 14.7% over thirty words is the worst figure on the whole board** — worse than Chantal, worse than *Dite*, eight times Lubwama's. Some of it is deliberate (the sebene sentences run on because a sebene runs on; *the guitar went on and on and did not stop, because a sebene does not stop, that is the entire point of a sebene*). Most of it is not. It is the same fault [[Chantal — AI Voice Benchmark Against Nazir (AI Draft, Not Canon)|the first experiment]] had, arriving by a different road.

**Note what it does not mean.** *Dite* runs 17.5 / 20% and is excellent. **The rhythm number does not predict quality** — see [[Dite — Story Sheet]]. This is logged as a fault because the long sentences here are *unmotivated*, not because the average is high.
