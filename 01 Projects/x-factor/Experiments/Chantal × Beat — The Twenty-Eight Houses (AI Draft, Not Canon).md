## What this experiment actually is

You asked for a fusion of the last AI draft in Research with [[The Beat of My Hurt (Draft 4 — Free)|Beat of My Hurt Draft 4]], keeping the Lingala and French, governed by a time-scheme built from the menstrual cycle, the moon and numerology, with the letter-science given to **D'juma**.

The load-bearing discovery is not mine and it is not invented. It is in the tradition, and [[09 The Practice — Calendar, Ritual and Ilmu Huruf]] is standing next to it without naming it:

> **The Arabic alphabet has 28 letters. The moon crosses 28 *manazil* — lunar mansions — in a month. In ʿilm al-ḥurūf these are the same 28; the letters are mapped to the mansions.** The 28th and last mansion is **Baṭn al-Ḥūt**, the belly of the fish.
>
> A nominal cycle is 28 days. **Djamela is on day thirty-four.**

Three twenty-eights that are historically one twenty-eight, a girl six days past the end of the road, and a house that smells of fish. **Nowhere below is that connection stated.** The three facts are placed in reach of each other and the reader does the arithmetic. That is the whole design.

# THE TWENTY-EIGHT HOUSES

### *Claude's fusion experiment — Chantal × Beat of My Hurt, 2026-07-31. Not canon.*

There are twenty-eight letters and my father can put a number on every one of them. I have nineteen.

He will not give me the last nine. When I ask he says the thing he has said since I was small, which is that a girl who learns the whole alphabet goes looking for something to count.

Rule one. You never run the letters on yourself. A man who counts his own name has already decided what he wants the answer to be.

My name is Djamela. We live in Mihogoni, the down part of town, where the road stops being a road and becomes a suggestion. My father sells fruit on Ave de Tanganyique in the mornings and lays his hands on people in the afternoons. Both of these are jobs. Neither is the kind you say out loud at school.

Rule four. You give them the letter. You never give them the number.

---

The paper is the cheap exercise-book kind, unlined, cut into squares with a razor and not with scissors, because scissors leave a lip and the lip shows through the leather.

He writes right to left. He does the name first and the mother's name under it, because a person is their mother's before they are anybody's. Then he sums.

Alif is one. Ba is two. Jim is three. It goes on like that, and it does not go in the order you learned it in, and that is the first thing that has to come out of your head before anything can go into it.

He reduces. He divides. He looks at what is left over.

Then he folds the square four times, always four, and it goes into a scrap of leather from the man behind the market who does sandals, and the person wears it, and pays him what they have.

In the wet season the ink runs if he writes before eleven. So he does the fruit in the morning and the letters after, and my mother has never once had to be told which part of the day it is.

She comes out and stands in the doorway while he works. She has watched him do it maybe four hundred times. She still comes out.

---

Rule six is not a rule. It is a fact, and he says it in the voice he keeps for facts.

The moon sleeps in twenty-eight houses. The alphabet has twenty-eight letters. This is not a coincidence, it is an arrangement.

The last house is Batn al-Hut. He taught me that one early, because it is the end of the road and he wanted me to know where the road ended.

Our house smells of fish. In Primary Six Alicia told a whole row about it, back when she was somebody else's friend and not mine, and everybody laughed and I laughed too, because that is the fastest way to stop being the thing people are laughing at.

I was behind the school gate counting on my fingers, the way you count coins when you already know they are not enough.

Thirty-four days.

I counted twice, and the first time I got thirty-three, and I wanted the smaller number to be the true one, so I counted again and it got bigger.

There is no twenty-ninth house.

The bell went. Alicia took my arm as she does and I let her, and we walked in past the notice board with last term's examination list still pinned to it. I sat at a desk with a wobble in it and opened a book about the digestive system of a cow. There are four stomachs. I can still name all four.

I am telling you about the Tuesday first because everybody wants to start with the man. The man comes later. The man is not the beginning of anything except his own story.

---

The boats come over from Kigoma with dagaa and secondhand words, and we keep both, so half of what gets shouted on this road is Tanzanian and nobody here has ever thought to mention it.

There is a gutter that came off our side of the house the year before last and is still lying where it landed.

There are children here we call the ghost kids. They go to Monseigneur André Kaseba and they wear checkered skirts and blazers and they sit in the back of their parents' cars looking at nothing. When the délestage takes our side of town their windows stay yellow. Our dala dala stops beside one of those cars at the lights on Ave de Industrielle and we look in and they do not look out. Not because they are proud. Because we are not there.

Morisho hangs off the side of the dala dala and shouts mia tano mia tano until his voice goes. He gave me a coin off my fare once and called me shemeji in front of the whole vehicle, and three women turned round to see whose girl I was, and one of them still asks after my mother by name.

He told us once that those children are ghosts to us, that we will never see them or talk to them. He meant it as a warning. I heard it as a price list.

---

I turned thirteen in March and my father bought a watermelon.

Not a cake. A watermelon, with one candle pushed into the top of it, standing crooked, because a watermelon is not built to hold a candle.

Baba, I said, you could have got a cake. Even a cheap one.

Watermelons are healthier, he said. More natural. More African. Cakes are a western thing.

My mother said watermelons are sweet, like you, Djamela, in the voice she uses when she is helping him be right about something.

Make a wish, he said. Blow.

I closed my eyes and said: to be Kim Kardashian, amen.

What is Kim Kardashian, my father said.

A social media influencer, my mother said, fast, the way you move a knife off a table.

I blew the candle out. The smoke went up and sat under the iron roof and did not leave.

Afterwards he took the razor and a square of paper and ran my name, which he does on the birthday and has done every year, with my mother's name under it. He worked for a while with his lips moving. Then he told me my letter.

He did not tell me the number.

I have nineteen letters and I could have got most of the way there myself, and he knows that, and he does it in front of me anyway.

He was handed a town that had been emptied out twice before he got to it. He has nothing to give me except proverbs and fruit and a road made out of letters. He decided that if he refused the world outside Kalemie hard enough I would stop wanting it. It did not work like that. It only made the wanting private.

---

Later I sat on the porch with Daudi with the lake in front of us and the four rusted ships in it that have not moved in my lifetime.

He thinks I want things, I said.

Daudi said nothing, which is what Daudi does.

I do want things, I said. I want to conquer like the Europeans did, except now you do not need weapons or degrees. You only need followers.

I was thirteen years and one hour old and I believed all of it, and I worked it out myself, from the evidence in front of me, which was a town where the people with cars had taken things and the people without cars had been taken from.

Daudi is the boy from two houses down. He has a wall in his room where he nails things he has found. A hubcap. The skull of an antelope. A bicycle wheel I am fairly sure he did not find. His mother died having him, which is a thing that gets said about him rather than by him.

When we were small I gave him a motherboard I found behind the internet café, green, with little gold roads on it going nowhere. He nailed it up between the skull and the hubcap. He has never once mentioned where he got it. Daudi thinks he found everything.

It rained in April, hard, as it does then, and I ran to his house because it was closer than mine. He had physics papers spread across the mattress and he moved them for me without being asked. There were buckets set out under the places where the roof has given up.

Do you understand entropy, I said.

It is a natural state, he said. Order needs energy. Disorder is what happens when you stop.

My father says the lake is the opposite. He says it puts things back in order, and that it remembers everything in its right place.

Your father is a wise man, Daudi said. Then, a minute later: it is hydrogen and oxygen, and it has no intelligence.

I told him about the twenty-eight houses. He listened all the way to the end, which he does. Then he said the moon takes twenty-seven and a third days to go round, and the rest is arithmetic somebody did afterwards to make it come out even.

I did not tell my father that.

I did not tell Daudi that I had already checked it, on my fingers, twice, and that he was right.

The rain came through the roof and hit the buckets and the sound filled the room so completely that there was nothing else in it. My knee was touching the edge of his knee. I had put it there.

So I kissed him. It lasted about twenty seconds. I counted, because I count everything.

Then I pulled back and said, see? Not everything falls apart.

That kiss cost me nothing at all. It was the cheapest thing I have ever given anybody, and he has carried it ever since like the down payment on a house, and I let him.

---

The car was a BMW, black, with windows you could not see into.

The window came down with a sound I had never heard a window make. Not the clink of a handle going round but a swish, like a word said quietly on purpose.

Je m'appelle Jean Luc. Tu es d'où, ma belle.

I said: Rue Saint Michel.

I lied immediately and easily and without deciding to. Rue Saint Michel is where the ghost children live and I have never set foot on it, and it came out of my mouth as though it had been sitting behind my teeth for years, waiting.

Daudi started saying the thing about strangers. Mama alisema —

We shouldn't talk to strangers, said Jean Luc, finishing it for him. And he smiled.

He told us he was a director. Consul général de Belgique. He said it in French with Lingala underneath, and his French was worse than he thought it was, and I noticed, and I decided not to have noticed.

That is the part I would like written down somewhere. I did the deciding.

He wrote a phone number on a piece of paper and put a ten thousand note under it. Ten thousand is a school skirt. A man with a car like that had stopped it, in the road, in front of the boda boda men, for me.

Je m'appelle, he asked.

Chantal, I said.

Nobody had asked me for a name before. They had only ever used the one I came with. He handed me that name like a gift, and I took it like one, and it was a collar with a good buckle.

Daudi did not say one word after mama alisema. He had his hand up on the strap of his bag and he took it off the strap and put it down flat against his leg and left it there. I did not turn my head. The hurt came off him like heat off a wall and I stood in it and warmed my hands, because a girl who has just been chosen wants somebody there to see her be chosen.

That night I ran Chantal.

It is a short name. It sits inside nineteen letters and I did not need him for any of it. I summed it twice and got the same both times, and reduced it, and there was something left over.

I do not know what a remainder means.

That is in the nine he kept.

---

I am not going to describe the hotel.

Here is what I have instead, which is the inventory, because a list is the only thing I have ever been able to hold the world in.

A key fob shaped like a lion, cartoon, plastic, the kind you would give a child. It opens a door on the third floor. I keep it in my left pocket where the seam has gone and never in my right.

A perfume. Shalis by Remy Marquis. Not mine. It gets into the weave of a khanga and will not come out with soap, only with time, so I learned to leave certain clothes at the bottom of the basket for four days before my mother does the wash.

Fingernails, done every Friday and taken off every Monday before school, because at school there are rules and at the hotel there are different rules.

A photograph on my phone. Me, and at the edge of the frame a hand on my shoulder with a gold ring on it. The ring is not real gold and he wears it like a crown. I tagged it blessed and forty-one people liked it and none of them asked me anything.

Two vaccinations, low on the arm, near the elbow, not where they usually put them.

A Thursday in June when there was nothing at the bottom of the basket that needed the four days, and I put two clean things down there anyway, and my mother washed them with the rest and said nothing.

Anybody who wanted to could have read that. It was all on the outside of me for months.

---

We went to Ave de Bobozo about the car.

I say the car. There had been a car, and it came off the road at the corner near the market and did not stop, and my leg has a mark on it going yellow at the edges. That was true, and I want you to know that one of the things I said that year was true.

Daudi came because I did not want to go alone.

The station smelled of dust and men. There was a bench along one wall with three of its slats replaced in a paler wood, and a woman in a black dress was explaining to an officer that it was not prostitution, it was escort services. He said, more like your pimp's number. Everybody laughed except her and me.

The officer who took us was young and typing with one finger, hunting each letter down as though it owed him something. Mhh. What do we have here.

Daudi said, she is reporting a hit and run.

So why are you talking, the officer said. You sound like you know more than she does. And he winked at Daudi, one man to another, over the top of me.

I held out my phone and showed him the photograph with the hand and the ring, because I had worked out on the way there that a hit and run was worth nothing and a man like that was worth something.

We deal with hard evidence, he said. Not AI. Not phantom ghosts.

I leaned close enough to smell onion and whiskey on him, and backed off, and said it.

My menstrual cycle. There is a latency of more than a month.

I got that word off a leaflet in the corridor at the clinic where they do the vaccinations, and I had been keeping it since April. It is not a word anybody in my house owns. That is why I used it.

The air in that room changed as though a door had opened somewhere.

The older one came over, big gut on him, the kind you get from a lifetime of small envelopes and large drinks, and he started reciting. The penal code. A teenage minor. Rape. Thirty years and twelve lashes on top. A buccal swab will suffice as conclusive evidence. Shame on the predator, for luring young girls.

The young one kept typing. Then, quietly: where is his number. We will call him. We will summon him now. We will finish the case, if you understand me.

I understood him perfectly. I have had the measure of men like him since I was eleven, when my auntie's neighbour let his hand sit too long on the back of my chair while a room full of adults agreed together not to see it.

I gave him the phone and dialled the number myself. Jean Luc took twenty minutes.

Twenty minutes means he was already in town, already near, already somewhere he could reach us from, which means that the whole time I had been walking around thinking I was choosing things, I had been the thing being watched.

He came in with his shoulders back and a fat envelope under his jacket and he did not look surprised and he did not look afraid.

When the older officer said the word minor, something went across Jean Luc's face and was gone, a flicker, like a bulb deciding whether to hold. He had planned for envelopes and not for consequences. Then it closed over and he smiled.

They took us to the room at the back.

Nothing happened to my body in that room. What happened is that three men worked out what I was worth, out loud, in two languages, in front of me, using the word compensation, and then agreed on it.

The Swahili was for me. The Lingala was not.

I sat on a plastic chair with a crack in it and did the arithmetic along with them, because I cannot help it, I have never been able to help it. And the number they landed on was smaller than the one I had been carrying in my head for eight months.

It came in two parts. The second part was for a doctor in Uvira, and it was the smaller one, and they said it in the same voice they said the first.

---

I came out of that room bent over. Daudi was there when I came out.

The corridor has eleven tiles across it. I counted them.

I counted the slats on the bench and got nineteen, and then I counted them again and got twenty-one, and the bench had not changed.

Alif is one. Ba is two. Jim is three.

I got as far as the tenth and could not find the eleventh. I have had the eleventh since I was six years old.

I sat down on the bench with the three pale slats in it and started again at alif and got to the tenth and stopped.

It was not shame. It was arithmetic.

---

They gave me the envelope on the way out. It had been fat going in, and it was thin coming out, and nobody said anything about that, and nobody had to.

The lake was on my left the whole way home, with the four ships in it.

The house was empty when I got home. My clothes were folded on the end of the bed, all of them, including the two from the bottom of the basket that still had four days to go. Somebody had washed them with soap and then with something after the soap. Vinegar, or paraffin, whatever it is you use when soap will not do it.

My mother has done the wash on Thursdays since before I could count.

She has no letters and she has no numbers and she has never once been wrong about a Thursday in her life.

I put the clothes in the drawer. Then I stood in the doorway of the kitchen for a while, and she was not in it, and I went to bed.

---

School on the Monday. I took the nails off on the Sunday night, the same as always.

Alicia asked me in the corridor whether it was true about the man with the car. I said what man. She said all right, and took my arm as she does, and she has not asked me anything since.

Here is the thing I have worked out and cannot put down. When this finishes, and it is going to finish, and I have known that since the twenty minutes, somebody will be taken for it and it will not be him. Him they will call as a witness, if they call him at all. They will take somebody visible and poor who cannot afford an envelope, and the whole town will agree to be satisfied.

I think it will be Morisho.

I stopped at Daudi's house on the way home and he was out, and his father was in, and his father does not look at me, which suited me.

I went into the room with the buckets and took the motherboard down off its nail. There is a board under the third bucket that comes up if you know it comes up, and I have known since I was nine. I put the fob under it, the lion, cartoon, plastic, and I put the board back, and I stood on it, and it did not creak.

Then I hung the motherboard on its nail, straight, as he likes it, between the skull and the hubcap.

At home I took a square of paper and cut it with the razor and not with scissors.

I wrote Morisho, right to left, and his mother's name under it, which I have known since Primary Four because she used to come to the gate for him when he was still going.

Then I summed it.

Mim is forty. Ra is two hundred. I did the ones I have and left the gaps where the nine are, and it came to a number, and I sat and looked at the number for a while.

I did not reduce it, because I do not know how.

I did not fold it four times and I did not put it in leather. There was nobody to give it to and there was nothing in it that would have helped him.

My mother came out and stood in the doorway.

She did not ask me what I was counting.

## Alternatives not taken

Four places where I made a call and there was a real other option.

**1 · The ending.** Draft 4 closes on the four ships and *"It came out even, the way it does."* **I cut it.** You have a story about a sum that does not come out even, closing on the word *even*, in the narrator's voice of restored competence — that is the consolation ending, and it undoes the counting-failure the story just bought. So I ended on the mother in the doorway instead, which pays off the transplant at 8% and leaves the arithmetic unresolved. **This is the single biggest departure in the file and it is arguable.** Your [[02 Comparison Board]] says do not add anything after the ships; I did not add, I replaced. Score both.

**2 · Whether D'juma's system is true.** I had Daudi produce the 27⅓-day figure and had Djamela check it and find her father wrong — and tell neither of them. The alternate is to leave the letter-science unchallenged, which keeps D'juma dignified and costs the story its cleanest enactment of the controlling idea. I took the cruel option because [[09 The Practice — Calendar, Ritual and Ilmu Huruf]] argues for exactly that: *cruel and true rather than mystical, because D'juma is not a fool and the story knows it.*

**3 · Naming Baṭn al-Ḥūt.** It is one line and it does a great deal of work, and it is also the most decorative sentence in the piece. The alternate is to keep all twenty-eight houses nameless and let the number alone carry it. Mine risks the reader feeling handled; the alternate risks nobody noticing the design at all.

**4 · Rule one.** *You never run the letters on yourself* is stated once, at 2%, and never referred to again — and the last scene is her running the letters on somebody else instead of speaking. I never close that loop out loud. The alternate is one sentence at the end acknowledging the rule. **Do not take the alternate.** That sentence is exactly the axis-7 mistake the last benchmark made.

### Three findings, in order of usefulness

**1 · This is the first text in the vault to land between Nazir and Chantal, and it is nearer Nazir.** 12.9 against 11.3 and 14.8. Short sentences at 54% clear Nazir's own 52%. Over-30-word sentences cut from Draft 4's 9.9% to 6.4%, against the target's 3.6%.

**2 · The mechanism is sentence *count*, not sentence trimming.** Nazir fits **305** sentences into 3,440 words. Chantal Draft 4 fits 262 into 3,883 — a longer story with 43 fewer sentences.
