## What "world-class" was taken to mean here

Not a higher rubric score. [[The Judging Rubric — Nine Axes|The Nine Axes]] measure control, and control is what a good story has — it is not what a great one has *extra*. So I went to the instrument in this vault that actually caught the difference.

**[[Nazir — The Serpent in the Grove|The winner]] scored 3s across Gate 3 and exactly one 5: *Leakage*.** Marsha's letter for the woman whose son was held for cussing a policeman. The British wells. The nurse's ledger. Zoongie returning with a baby. **None of it is the story. All of it is the reason the story is believed.**

> **The X-factor is surplus.** A world-class story is one where the world plainly existed before the plot arrived and will carry on after it leaves — where things happen that serve nothing, people want things the story does not care about, and jokes get made near the worst thing in the book.

Everything below is built for that. Four consequences:

| Decision | Why |
| --- | --- |
| **Comedy braided into the atrocity** | The single largest gap between the drafts and Congolese writing as it is actually done. Chantal is solemn for 3,900 words. No Congolese account of a bad year is solemn for 3,900 words. Mujila is not. **Irony is not a break from suffering here, it is the register suffering is carried in** |
| **Article 15 as the spine** | *Débrouillez-vous* — the article of the constitution that is not in the constitution. Every Congolese knows it; none has read it. # ARTICLE QUINZE

### *Claude's X-factor experiment, 2026-07-31. Not canon. No gate, no rubric.*

Our constitution has an article that is not in it.

Article Fifteen. Débrouillez-vous. Everybody in this country can quote it and nobody has read it, because there is nothing to read, because it was never written down. It is the only law here that has never once been broken. It asks nothing of you except that you manage.

I was thirteen when I found out that it covers children too.

---

My father can put a number on every letter of the alphabet. There are twenty-eight of them. He has taught me nineteen.

He will not give me the last nine. When I ask he says that a girl who learns the whole alphabet goes looking for something to count, which is the kind of sentence he makes, and which turned out to be correct, which is the annoying thing about him.

Rule one. You never run the letters on yourself. A man who counts his own name has decided the answer before he starts.

He sells fruit on Ave de Tanganyique in the morning and does the letters at the house in the afternoon, because in the wet season the ink runs if he writes before eleven. He cuts the paper into squares with a razor and never with scissors, because scissors leave a lip and the lip shows through the leather.

He writes right to left. The name first, then the mother's name underneath, because a person is their mother's before they are anybody's.

Then he folds it four times, always four, and it goes into a scrap of leather from Papa Mulenda behind the market, who does sandals and who has been overcharging my father for eleven years in a way both of them enjoy.

The person wears it and pays him what they have.

Which is Article Fifteen also. He would not put it that way.

---

We live in the cité, in Mihogoni, where the road stops being a road and becomes an opinion.

At seven the délestage takes us and the ville keeps its lights, and this has happened every evening of my life, and nobody has ever remarked on it, because what is there to say. Up there the Église du Réveil has a generator and speakers the parish cannot dream of, so on Wednesdays we get the sermon whether we go or not. Pasteur Nkulu shouts about the spirit of poverty. He shouts it into a microphone that cost more than my father's stall.

My mother says he is not wrong, only loud.

The railway ends here. It was built to go on and it does not, and men sit at the station in the afternoons the way men sit at a bedside, and one of them, Papa Kabeya, will tell you the timetable from 1974 if you stand still long enough. He has the whole thing. Departures, arrivals, the meal service.

The Liemba comes in from Kigoma on a Wednesday when it comes. It is a German boat. Not German-made, German — it was a warship, they sank it themselves in the war, and the Belgians pulled it up again, and it has been carrying goats and mattresses and people's aunties across this lake ever since. It is older than the country. It is older than the last two names of the country.

Nobody here thinks that is remarkable. It is just the boat.

The boats from Kigoma bring ndakala and secondhand words, and we keep both, so half of what gets shouted on this road is Tanzanian and it has never occurred to anyone to mention it.

Bahati sells makala on the corner. Everybody calls her Bahati and nobody has ever used it to her face, and I have never found out what the real one is, and now I never will, because you cannot ask a question like that after a certain number of years have gone by.

There is a gutter that came off our house the year before last and is still lying where it landed. My mother has stopped mentioning it, which is not the same as having forgotten.

---

Morisho hangs off the side of the taxi-bus and shouts mia tano mia tano until his voice goes.

He gave me a coin off my fare once and called me shemeji, in front of the whole vehicle, and three women turned round to see whose girl I was, and one of them still asks after my mother by name.

He supports TP Mazembe, which is a thing you find out about a man in the first four minutes. He has explained to me twice, at length, at a volume, why the 2015 side would beat anything in Africa today.

The wewa men at the dagaa stop call you beautiful in a way that is not a compliment but a notice. It means the town has finished discussing whether you are old enough and has reached its finding.

There are children here we call the ghost kids. They go to Monseigneur André Kaseba and they wear blazers in this heat and they sit in the back of their parents' cars looking at nothing. When the délestage takes us their windows stay yellow. Our taxi-bus stops beside them at the lights on Ave de Industrielle and we look in and they do not look out.

Not because they are proud. Because we are not there.

Morisho told us once that those children are ghosts to us, that we will never see them or speak to them. He meant it as a warning.

I heard it as a price list.

---

I turned thirteen in March and my father bought a watermelon.

Not a cake. A watermelon, with one candle pushed into it, standing crooked, because a watermelon is not built to hold a candle and there was no one in that house prepared to say so.

Baba, I said. You could have got a cake. Even a cheap one.

Watermelons are healthier, he said. More natural. More African. Cakes are a western thing.

The Belgians brought bread too, I said. You eat bread.

He said that was different and did not say how.

My mother said watermelons are sweet, like you, Djamela, in the voice she uses when she is helping him be right about something. She has been helping him be right about things for fifteen years and she has never once been caught doing it.

Make a wish, he said. Blow.

I closed my eyes and said, to be Kim Kardashian, amen.

What is Kim Kardashian, my father said.

A social media influencer, my mother said. Fast. The way you move a knife off a table.

I blew it out and the smoke went up and sat under the iron sheets and stayed there.

Afterwards he took the razor and a square of paper and ran my name, which he does every birthday, with my mother's name under it. His lips move when he works. Then he told me my letter.

He did not tell me the number. He never does.

I have nineteen letters. I could get most of the way there without him and he knows I could, and he sits down in front of me and does it anyway, every year, like a man putting a lock on a door he has already left open.

He was handed a town that had been emptied out twice before he got to it. He has nothing to give me except proverbs and fruit and a road made out of letters. He decided that if he refused the world hard enough on my behalf I would stop wanting it.

It did not work like that. It only made the wanting private.

---

Daudi is the boy from two houses down. He has a wall in his room where he nails things he has found — a hubcap, the skull of an antelope, a bicycle wheel I am fairly sure he did not find.

His mother died having him. That is a thing said about him and never by him.

When we were small I found a motherboard behind the internet café that used to be by the market, green, with little gold roads on it going nowhere, and I gave it to him because he was the only person I knew who would understand that a thing can be beautiful after it has stopped being useful.

He nailed it up between the skull and the hubcap. He has never once said where it came from. Daudi believes he found everything.

It rained in April, hard, as it does, and I ran to his because it was nearer. His room is one room. Buckets under the places the roof has given up, physics papers across the mattress, which he moved for me without being asked.

Do you understand entropy, I said. I had heard him say the word eleven times and I wanted him to be the one who knew something, for once, in front of me.

It is a natural state, he said. Order needs energy. Disorder is what happens when you stop.

My father says the lake is the opposite. He says it puts things back in order and remembers everything in its right place.

Your father is a wise man, Daudi said.

Then, a minute later: it is hydrogen and oxygen. It has no intelligence.

I told him about the twenty-eight houses of the moon. He listened all the way to the end, which he does, and then he said the moon takes twenty-seven days and a third, and the rest is arithmetic somebody did afterwards to make it come out even.

I did not tell my father that.

I did not tell Daudi that I had already checked it on my fingers, twice, and that he was right.

The rain came through and hit the buckets and filled the room so completely there was nothing else in it. My knee was against the edge of his knee. I had put it there.

So I kissed him. About twenty seconds. I counted, because I count.

Then I sat back and said, see. Not everything falls apart.

That kiss cost me nothing. It is the cheapest thing I have ever given anybody and he has carried it around since like a deposit on a house, and I let him, and I knew exactly what he was carrying the whole time he carried it.

---

The car was a BMW, black, windows you could not see into.

The window came down with a sound I had never heard a window make. Not the clink of a handle going round. A swish. Like a word said quietly on purpose.

Fally was on inside, low, and it was in the sebene, that part where the guitar goes and goes and the atalaku is throwing names into it, somebody's cousin, somebody's boss, mabanga for men who paid to be shouted about.

Je m'appelle Jean Luc, he said. Tu es d'où, ma belle.

I said, Rue Saint Michel.

I lied at once, easily, without deciding to. Rue Saint Michel is where the ghost children live and I have never set foot on it, and it came out of my mouth as though it had been sitting behind my teeth for years waiting for somebody worth spending it on.

Daudi started the thing about strangers. Mama alisema —

We shouldn't talk to strangers, said Jean Luc, finishing it for him.

And smiled.

He told us he was a director. Consul général de Belgique. He said it in French with Lingala underneath and his French was worse than he thought it was, and I heard that, and I decided not to have heard it.

That is the part I would like written down somewhere. I did the deciding.

He put a paper on the sill with a number on it and a ten thousand note under the paper. Ten thousand is a school skirt.

Je m'appelle, he asked.

Chantal, I said.

Nobody had ever asked me for a name. They had only ever used the one I came with. He handed me that name like a gift and I took it like one and it was a collar with a good buckle.

Daudi did not say one word after mama alisema. He had his hand up on the strap of his bag and he took it off the strap and put it flat against his leg and left it there. I did not turn my head. The hurt came off him like heat off a wall and I stood in it and warmed my hands, because a girl who has just been chosen wants somebody there to watch her be chosen.

That night I ran Chantal.

It is a short name. It sits inside nineteen letters and I did not need him for any of it. I summed it twice and got the same both times and reduced it and there was something left over.

I do not know what a remainder means.

That is in the nine he kept.

---

I am not going to describe the hotel.

Here is the inventory instead, because a list is the only shape I have ever been able to hold the world in.

A key fob shaped like a lion. Cartoon, plastic, the kind you would give a child. It opens a door on the third floor. It lives in my left pocket where the seam has gone and never in my right.

A perfume. Shalis by Remy Marquis. Not mine. It gets into the weave of a pagne and soap will not take it out, only time, so I learned to leave certain things at the bottom of the basket for four days before my mother does the wash.

Nails, done every Friday, off every Monday before school, because at school there are rules and at the hotel there are different rules and I crossed between them the way you cross a road that does not stop.

A photograph. Me, and at the edge of the frame a hand on my shoulder with a gold ring on it that is not gold, worn the way a man wears a crown. I put blessed under it and forty-one people liked it and not one of them asked me anything.

Two vaccinations, low on the arm near the elbow, not where they usually put them.

A Thursday in June when there was nothing at the bottom of the basket that needed the four days, and I put two clean things down there anyway, and my mother washed them with the rest and said nothing.

Anybody who wanted to could have read that. It was on the outside of me for months and this town has excellent eyes.

---

We went to the PNC post on Ave de Bobozo about the car.

I say the car. There had been a car. It came off the road at the corner by the market and did not stop and my leg still has the mark going yellow at the edges. That was true, and I want it on the record that one of the things I said that year was true.

Daudi came because I did not want to go alone. He said it was because his bicycle was stolen once so he knew the forms. There are no forms.

The post smells of dust and men. There is a bench along the wall with three slats replaced in a paler wood. A woman in black was explaining to the desk that it was not prostitution, it was escort services, they have cards, the cards have a company number on them.

More like your pimp's number, he said, and the room laughed, and she waited for it to finish the way you wait out rain.

The young one who took us typed with one finger, hunting each letter down like it owed him. Mhh. What do we have here.

She is reporting a hit and run, Daudi said.

So why are you talking, the officer said. You sound like you know more than she does.

And he winked at Daudi. One man to another. Over the top of me.

He asked where and what type and who was driving, typing at the speed of a man who has already worked out what this is worth, and there is a word for what he was waiting for and everybody in that room including me knew the word.

Matabiche.

I did not have it. What I had was a photograph, so I held out the phone and showed him the hand and the ring, because I had worked out on the way there that a hit and run is worth nothing and a man like that is worth something.

We deal with hard evidence, he said. Not AI. Not phantom ghosts.

I leaned in close enough to get the onion and the whiskey off him, and backed off, and said it.

My menstrual cycle. There is a latency of more than a month.

I got that word off a leaflet in the corridor at the clinic where they do the vaccinations and I had been keeping it since April. It is not a word anybody in my house owns. That is exactly why I used it.

The air in that room changed like a door opening somewhere behind it.

---

The older one came over. Big gut on him, the kind you get from a lifetime of small envelopes and large drinks, and he started to recite.

The penal code. A teenage minor. Rape. Thirty years and twelve lashes on top of it. A buccal swab will suffice as conclusive evidence. Shame on the predator, for luring young girls.

He said it in the voice Père Célestin uses at St Augustine for the parts he has said ten thousand times, and I watched him enjoy the sound of it, and somewhere under the enjoying he was already doing his own arithmetic.

The young one kept typing. Then, quietly, without looking up: where is his number. We will call him. We will summon him now. We will finish the case, if you understand me.

I understood him perfectly. I have had the measure of men like him since I was eleven, since my auntie's neighbour let his hand sit too long on the back of my chair while a room full of adults agreed together not to see it, the way you agree not to see a crack in a wall you are only renting.

I gave him the phone and dialled it myself.

Jean Luc took twenty minutes.

Twenty minutes means he was already in town. Already near. Already somewhere he could reach us from. Which means that the whole time I had been walking around this town thinking I was the one choosing things, I had been the thing being watched.

He came in with his shoulders back and a fat envelope under his jacket and he was not surprised and he was not afraid. He looked like a man whose phone had finally rung.

When the older one said the word minor, something crossed his face and was gone. A flicker. Like a bulb deciding whether to hold. He had planned for envelopes. He had not planned for consequences.

Then it closed over and he smiled and it was as though it had never been there at all.

They took us to the room at the back.

---

Nothing happened to my body in that room.

What happened is that three men worked out what I was worth, out loud, in front of me, in two languages, using the word compensation, and then agreed on it.

The Swahili was for me. The Lingala was not.

I sat on a plastic chair with a crack in it and did the arithmetic along with them because I cannot help it and I have never been able to help it.

The number they landed on was smaller than the one I had been carrying in my head for eight months.

It came in two parts. The second part was for a doctor in Uvira and it was the smaller one and they said it in the same voice they said the first.

Débrouillez-vous.

Nobody said that. I want to be clear. Nobody in that room said it. It is only that there was no other article for what was happening, and everyone present had known the article since childhood, and it was doing its work.

---

I came out of that room bent over. Daudi was standing where I left him.

The corridor has eleven ceiling tiles across it. I counted them.

I counted the slats on the bench and got nineteen, then I counted them again and got twenty-one, and the bench had not moved.

Alif is one. Ba is two. Jim is three.

I got to the tenth and could not find the eleventh. I have had the eleventh since I was six years old.

I sat down on the bench with the three pale slats in it and started again at alif and got to the tenth and stopped, and started again, and stopped.

Outside a woman was arguing with a wewa man about four hundred francs.

---

They gave me the envelope on the way out.

It had been fat going in. It was thin coming out. Nobody said anything about that and nobody had to.

The lake was on my left the whole way home.

The house was empty. My clothes were folded on the end of the bed, all of them, including the two from the bottom of the basket that still had four days to go. Somebody had washed them with soap and then with something after the soap. Vinegar, or paraffin. Whatever it is you use when soap will not do it.

My mother has done the wash on Thursdays since before I could count.

She has no letters and she has no numbers. She has never been wrong about a Thursday in her life.

I put them in the drawer. I stood in the kitchen doorway a while and she was not in it, and I went to bed, and outside the Église du Réveil was going and Pasteur Nkulu was shouting about the spirit of poverty, and it carried all the way down the hill on the generator he bought with it.

---

School on Monday. I took the nails off on the Sunday night, the same as always.

Alicia asked me in the corridor whether it was true about the man with the car. I said what man. She said all right, and took my arm as she does, and she has not asked me anything since, and that is the most anybody has done for me this year.

Here is the thing I have worked out and cannot put down.

When this finishes, and it is going to finish, and I have known that since the twenty minutes — somebody will be taken for it, and it will not be him. Him they will call as a witness, if they call him at all.

They will take somebody visible and poor who cannot afford an envelope, and the town will agree to be satisfied, and it will not even be a decision anybody makes. It will be the weather.

I think it will be Morisho, because he is the one who shouts mia tano mia tano on the road every morning where everybody can see him being exactly who he is.

He gave me a coin off my fare and called me shemeji. That is the whole of what he did. He also has opinions about TP Mazembe that he has not been asked for since 2015, and a mother who used to come to the school gate for him, and neither of those will come up.

---

I went to Daudi's on the way home. He was out. His father was in and does not look at me, which suited me.

I took the motherboard down off its nail.

There is a board under the third bucket that comes up if you know it comes up, and I have known since I was nine. I put the fob under it — the lion, cartoon, plastic — and put the board back and stood on it and it did not creak.

Then I hung the motherboard back up, straight, the way he likes it, between the skull and the hubcap.

At home I cut a square of paper with the razor and not with scissors.

I wrote Morisho, right to left, and his mother's name under it, which I have known since Primary Four.

Mim is forty. Ra is two hundred.

I did the ones I have and left gaps where the nine are and it came to a number, and I sat there and looked at the number for a while.

I did not reduce it, because I do not know how.

I did not fold it four times and I did not take it to Papa Mulenda for the leather. There was nobody to give it to and there was nothing in it that would have helped him.

My mother came out and stood in the doorway.

She did not ask me what I was counting.

---

My father says the lake keeps everything. He has said it my whole life. Some women resemble the lake, he says. People come and go and it stays the same, dark and deep, swallowing things down and holding them.

He is a wise man and he is wrong, and I have known he is wrong since Primary Five, when a teacher who has since gone to Lubumbashi drew it on the board.

Everything that falls into that lake stays in it except at one place.

The Lukuga goes out at the north end of this town, past the cement works, under the bridge where the boys jump off in December, and it takes the water down through the forest until it finds the Congo, and the Congo takes it to the sea.

One gap. In the whole of that lake, one. And it is here. And you can walk to it.

I have never told him.

He is fifty-one years old and he has built everything he believes on a lake that keeps things, and his daughter can walk to the place where it does not, in eleven minutes, and has, and stood there, and come home, and eaten what he cooked.

Article Fifteen.

Nobody wrote it down. Nobody had to.

## Deliberately not done

**No death.** [[Supporting - D_Juma|The character file]] gives D'juma his plain sentence after Djamela's death — *I was wrong. The lake doesn't put things back in order. It keeps them. There's a difference.* That is **your line and one of the best in the vault**, and I did not take it, use it, or write around it. The Lukuga ending is the inverse: he never gets the correction, because she never gives it to him. If you want his version, it is still sitting there unspent.

**No coda.** Article Fifteen returns as two words and stops. Nazir spent twenty lines explaining his serpent and lost two axes for it. **The joke does the coda's job in four syllables** and never explains itself.

**No gloss, anywhere.** *Matabiche, wewa, ndakala, makala, pagne, sebene, atalaku, mabanga, shemeji, délestage, débrouillez-vous* — every one recovers from its sentence or is deliberately left to the reader to not-quite-get, which is what reading a foreign town is actually like.

**Boda boda became wewa.** Flagged in the language note above. Your cross-lake justification is real; this is a different call, not a correction.

## Where the leakage actually is

The test for surplus is simple: **cut it and does the plot notice?** If the plot doesn't notice and the story would be poorer, it is leakage. If neither notices, it is padding.

| Surplus | Serves the plot? |
| --- | --- |
| Papa Kabeya and the 1974 timetable | No |
| Papa Mulenda overcharging for leather for eleven years, mutually enjoyed | No |
| The *Liemba* — sunk by its own navy, raised by another, still carrying goats | No |
| Pasteur Nkulu, the spirit of poverty, and the generator he bought with it | No |
| TP Mazembe, the 2015 side, twice, at length, unasked | No |
| The woman in black waiting out the laughter the way you wait out rain | No |
| The gutter my mother has stopped mentioning | No |
| The woman outside arguing about four hundred francs | **No — and it is the most important line in the police scene** |
| Bahati's real name, now unaskable | No |

Nine things the plot does not need. **That is the experiment.** The previous two AI passes had almost none of this, and it is precisely why they read as *constructed* — every object was load-bearing, which is the tell [[All the Small Things — Grief Through the Minor Object]] and [[The De-Slop Pass — English Prose|de-slop Tier 1]] both name: *no dead detail.*

The four-hundred-francs line is the one to look at hardest. A child has just been priced in a back room; outside, a woman is arguing about four hundred francs, and the story does not connect the two. **Connecting them would be the coda.** Leaving them adjacent is the leakage.

## Measured — and surplus turns out to have a price

**The highest short-sentence ratio on the board — 57%, five points clear of the winner — and simultaneously the over-30s went back up, from the fusion's 6.4% to 10.4%.**

That is not a slip. It is what surplus costs, and it is the most useful thing this experiment produced:

> **A digression is a long sentence.** Papa Kabeya's 1974 timetable, the *Liemba*'s three navies, Papa Mulenda's eleven years of enjoyable overcharging, Pasteur Nkulu's generator — every one of them arrives as a subordinate structure, because that is the grammar of an aside. **You cannot have leakage and Nazir's 3.6% at the same time.** Nazir himself doesn't: he buys his short profile partly by keeping the surplus in *clauses of dialogue and object* rather than in narrated digression.

So the board now shows two different routes and they pull against each other:

- **The fusion route** — hard section breaks, minimal digression. Buys the sentence profile. Costs the world.
- **The X-Factor route** — digression admitted. Buys the world. Costs the profile.
