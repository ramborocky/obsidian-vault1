Nobody tells you the exact day your body stops being only yours. Djamela's picked a Tuesday.

She was behind the school gate counting on her fingers, the way you count coins when you already know they are not enough. Alicia was talking about somebody's hairdo and whether the girl had paid for it herself. Thirty-four days. She had counted twice. The first time she got thirty-three, and she wanted the smaller number to be the true one, so she counted again and it got bigger.

That is not how counting is supposed to work. That was the opposite of how counting was supposed to work, and she stood at the gate with her hand still open in front of her, waiting for the number to correct itself, the way you wait at a door you have already knocked on.

The bell went. Alicia took her arm as she always did and she let her, and they walked in past the notice board with last term's examination list still pinned to it, gone soft at the edges from the damp, and she sat down at a desk with a wobble in it and opened a book about the digestive system of a cow. They had done the digestive system of a cow for three weeks. There were four stomachs. She could name all four and she had never once needed to.

They live in Mihogoni, the down part of town, where the road stops being a road and becomes a suggestion. Her father sells fruit on Ave de Tanganyique in the mornings and lays his hands on people in the afternoons, and both of these are jobs, and neither is the kind you say out loud at school. She had learned that early, the way children learn the geography of a dangerous room: some words were doors and you did not open them.

The boats come over from Kigoma with dagaa and secondhand words, and they keep both, so half of what gets shouted on that road is Tanzanian and nobody there has ever thought to mention it.

There is a gutter that came off their side of the house the year before last and is still lying where it landed. The woman who sells charcoal on the corner is called Bahati, or that is what people call her, and Djamela has never once heard anybody use it to her face.

In Primary Six, Alicia told a whole row that their house smelled of fish, back when she was somebody else's friend and not Djamela's. Everybody laughed. Djamela laughed too, loud, first, before the laugh could settle on her — because that is the fastest way to stop being the thing people are laughing at, and she had worked that out by herself, at eleven, in a corridor, with her ears going hot.

Their house does smell of fish. That was not the part that stayed.

There are children in that town they call the ghost kids. They go to Monseigneur André Kaseba and they wear checkered skirts with ties and blazers and they sit in the back of their parents' cars looking at nothing. When the délestage takes the down side of town their windows stay yellow. Djamela has stood on the road in the dark and watched them doing their homework in the light, close enough to see a girl turn a page, and gone home and done hers by the paraffin lamp with the smell in her hair that would still be there at school in the morning.

When the dala dala stops beside one of those cars at the lights on Ave de Industrielle, they look in and the children do not look out. Not because they are proud. Because we are not there.

Morisho hangs off the side of the dala dala and shouts *mia tano mia tano* until his voice goes. He told them once that those children were ghosts to them, that they would never see them or talk to them. He meant it as a warning. She heard it as a price list.

---

She turned thirteen in March and her father bought a watermelon.

Not a cake. A watermelon, with one candle pushed into the top of it, standing crooked because a watermelon is not built to hold a candle, and the wax had already begun to run down the green stripes and set there.

Baba, she said, you could have got a cake. Even a cheap one.

Watermelons are healthier, he said. More natural. More African. Cakes are a western thing.

Watermelons are sweet, her mother said, like you, Djamela — in the voice she uses when she is helping him be right about something.

He is not a stupid man. He was handed a town that had been emptied out twice before he got to it, and he has nothing to give her except proverbs and fruit, and he had decided that if he refused the world outside Kalemie hard enough she would stop wanting it. It did not work like that. It only made the wanting private.

Make a wish, he said. Blow.

She closed her eyes.

She went looking for a wish he could have given her. Something small, something inside the shape of what he had — a wish that would make his face do the thing it did when he had been right about something. She looked for it the way you look for a word you are certain you know, going round the edges of it, and it was not there. There was nothing he could give her that she wanted. There was nothing she wanted that would not, if she said it out loud in that room with the candle going crooked in the fruit, have told him exactly how far outside his hands it was.

So she gave him the joke instead, because the joke was cheaper for both of them.

To be Kim Kardashian, she said. Amen.

What is Kim Kardashian, her father said.

A social media influencer, her mother said — fast, the way you move a knife off a table.

Something went across her mother's face first, a flicker, and Djamela caught it and put it away somewhere, because you never know when a thing you have seen on your mother's face is going to be useful.

She blew the candle out. The smoke went up and sat under the iron roof and did not leave.

Later she sat on the porch with Daudi with the lake in front of them and the four rusted ships in it that have not moved in her lifetime.

He thinks I want things, she said.

Daudi said nothing, which is what Daudi does.

I do want things, she said. I want to control them. I want to conquer like the Europeans did, except now you do not need weapons or degrees. You only need followers.

She was thirteen years and one hour old and she believed all of it. Nobody had made her believe it. She had worked it out herself, from the evidence in front of her, which was a town where the people with cars had taken things and the people without cars had been taken from.

Daudi is the boy from two houses down, a year and a half older, with a wall in his room where he nails things he has found. A hubcap. The skull of an antelope. A bicycle wheel she is fairly sure he did not find. He calls it a collection and everybody else calls it rubbish.

His mother died having him, which is a thing that gets said about him rather than by him, and he has built a whole woman out of that absence — patient, soft, never once disappointed in him, because a woman who never existed does not have to be. He has never said one word of it to her. She has it anyway, entire: the shape of a room nobody has ever put a light on.

When they were small she gave him a motherboard. She found it behind the internet café that used to be by the market, in a heap of things that had stopped working. Green, with little gold roads on it going nowhere, and she thought it was the most beautiful broken thing in the heap. She gave it to him because he was the only person who would understand that a thing can be beautiful after it has stopped being useful.

He nailed it to the wall between the skull and the hubcap. He has never once mentioned where he got it, and she does not think he remembers it was her.

Daudi thinks he found everything.

---

The car was a BMW, black, with windows you could not see into.

They had got off the dala dala at the dagaa stop, and behind them Morisho was still shouting *mia tano mia tano* and getting smaller, and the boda boda men were doing what boda boda men do, which is call you beautiful in a way that is not a compliment but a notice — a way of saying that everyone has agreed you are available for comment now.

Then a horn, different from theirs. The window came down with a sound she had never heard a window make, not the clink of a handle going round but a swish, like a word said quietly on purpose.

Je m'appelle Jean Luc, he said. Tu es d'où, ma belle.

Rue Saint Michel, she said.

She lied immediately and easily and without deciding to. Rue Saint Michel is where the ghost children live and she has never set foot on it, and it came out of her mouth as though it had been sitting behind her teeth for years, waiting to be asked for.

Daudi started saying the thing about strangers. Mama alisema —

We shouldn't talk to strangers, said Jean Luc, finishing it for him. And he smiled.

She has thought about that smile more than about anything else since. He knew what he was and he said it out loud, in a joke, in front of both of them, and he was so certain it would cost him nothing that he was right.

He told them he was a director. Consul général de Belgique. He said it in French with Lingala underneath, and his French was worse than he thought it was, and she noticed, and she decided not to have noticed. That is the part she would like written down somewhere. Not that she was fooled.

He wrote a phone number on a piece of paper and put a ten thousand note under it. Her eyes went wide and Daudi has never asked her why. It was not the money. Ten thousand is a school skirt. It was that a man with a car like that had stopped it, in the road, in front of the boda boda men, for her.

Je m'appelle, he asked.

Chantal, she said.

Nobody had asked her for a name before. They had only ever used the one she came with. He handed her that name like a gift and she took it like one, and it was a collar with a good buckle.

Daudi stood beside her the whole time and did not say one word after mama alisema. She did not turn her head. The hurt came off him like heat off a wall and she stood in it and warmed her hands, because a girl who has just been chosen wants somebody there to see her be chosen.

She was thirteen.

She went home and put the ten thousand under the mattress on her side. Her mother does the wash on Thursdays and has done since before Djamela could count, so she had until Thursday to decide where it was going to live.

---

It rained in April, hard, as it does then, and she ran to Daudi's house because it was closer than her own.

His room is one room. Baked brick, a window the size of two hands, a foam mattress on the floor with a grey sheet, and buckets set out under the places where the roof has given up. He had physics papers spread across the mattress and he moved them for her without being asked.

Do you understand entropy, she said.

She had heard him say it eleven times. She asked because she wanted him to be the person who knew something, for once, in front of her.

It is a natural state, he said. Order needs energy. Disorder is what happens when you stop.

My father says the lake is the opposite, she said. He says it puts things back in order, and that it remembers everything in its right place.

Your father is a wise man, Daudi said. Then, a minute later: it is hydrogen and oxygen, and it has no intelligence.

They sat there and the rain came through the roof and hit the buckets and the sound filled the room so completely that there was nothing else in it. Her knee was touching the edge of his knee. She had put it there.

Then what do you want to remember, Mr Scientist, she said. Encrypted data? Binary codes? Or a colourful moment with no meaning in it?

He did not answer and he did not move. A boy who wanted something more than he has wanted anything, holding completely still, because moving might have cost him the version of her he kept in his head.

She kissed him.

Twenty seconds. She counted, because she counted everything, because the counting was the one thing that had ever held still while she looked at it.

His mouth was warm and he did not know what to do with his hands. She put her palm flat on his chest, over the heart, and felt it going, and she thought: this is the heart of a boy who has wanted me since we were nine. And she waited.

She waited for the thing that was supposed to come. Some answering heat, some lifting, whatever it is that other girls seem to get for free and talk about in the corridor as though it arrives on its own. She held her hand there and she waited for it and she was still waiting when the twenty seconds were used up.

It was like putting your hand on a photograph of a fire.

She could see the shape of what it was supposed to be. She could not get inside it. Somewhere in the last eight months something had been taken out of her without her noticing it go, and she had been walking around since with the space where it had been, and there was nothing left in her that could answer him.

She pulled back. His face was the face of somebody shown the back door of the universe.

She saw it. She saw the whole universe arrive in a boy's face two houses down from hers, and she felt the counting and nothing else.

See? she said. Not everything falls apart.

And she smiled at him, and the smile was real, because the smile was the only thing she still had that worked.

---

She is not going to describe the hotel.

What there is instead is the inventory, because a list is the only thing she has ever been able to hold the world in.

A key fob shaped like a lion, cartoon, plastic, the kind you would give a child. It opens a door on the third floor. She keeps it in her left pocket where the seam has gone and never in her right.

A perfume. Shalis by Remy Marquis. Not hers. It gets into the weave of a khanga and will not come out with soap, only with time, so she learned to leave certain clothes at the bottom of the basket for four days before her mother does the wash.

Fingernails, done every Friday and taken off every Monday before school, because at school there are rules and at the hotel there are different rules, and she lives between the two of them like somebody crossing a road that never stops.

A photograph on her phone. Her face, smiling, and at the edge of the frame a hand on her shoulder with a gold ring on it. The ring is not real gold. He wears it like a crown, and nobody who could tell the difference has ever come close enough to check.

She had tagged it *blessed*. Forty-one people liked it and none of them asked her anything, because forty-one people in Kalemie know better than to ask.

She looks at it at night, under the sheet, with the screen making her face blue. More than she should. More than is any use to her. She looks at the hand and the ring and she tries to feel chosen — tries to get to the thing the ghost kids have, sitting in the back of the cars, looking at nothing because nothing is required of them. She goes at it the way you press a bruise to see whether it is still there.

It does not come. What comes is the arithmetic: what she was worth in March, and what she is worth now, and the direction of travel between the two.

She does not delete the photograph. Deleting it would have been an admission, and she has not admitted anything, not to her mother, not to Daudi, not lying in the dark to herself.

Two vaccinations, low on the arm, near the elbow, not where they usually put them.

Anybody who wanted to could have read that. It was all on the outside of her for months.

---

They went to Ave de Bobozo about the car.

She says the car. There had been a car, and it came off the road at the corner near the market and did not stop, and her leg has a mark on it going yellow at the edges. That was true, and it matters to her that one of the things she said that year was true.

Daudi came because she did not want to go alone. He said it was because his bicycle was stolen once, so he knew the forms.

The station smelled of dust and men. There was a bench along one wall with three of its slats replaced in a paler wood, and a woman in a black dress was explaining to an officer that it was not prostitution, it was escort services, they had cards with a company number on them. He said, more like your pimp's number. Everybody laughed except her and Djamela.

The officer who took them was young and typing with one finger, hunting each letter down as though it owed him something. He raised his face to hers and made a sound in his throat. Mhh. What do we have here.

She is reporting a hit and run, Daudi said.

So why are you talking, the officer said. You sound like you know more than she does. And he winked at Daudi, one man to another, over the top of her.

He asked where and what type of car and who was driving, typing at the speed of a man who has already decided what this is worth. So she held out her phone and showed him the photograph with the hand and the ring, because she had worked out on the way there that a hit and run was worth nothing and a man like that was worth something.

We deal with hard evidence, he said. Not AI. Not phantom ghosts.

She leaned close enough to smell onion and whiskey on him, and backed off, and said it.

My menstrual cycle. There is a latency of more than a month.

The air in that room changed as though a door had opened somewhere.

The older one came over, big gut on him, the kind you get from a lifetime of small envelopes and large drinks, and he started reciting. The penal code. A teenage minor. Rape. Thirty years and twelve lashes on top. He said it in the voice the priest at St Augustine uses for the parts he has said ten thousand times. A buccal swab will suffice as conclusive evidence. Shame on the predator, for luring young girls.

The young one kept typing. Then, quietly: where is his number. We will call him. We will summon him now. Yes. We will finish the case, if you understand me.

She understood him perfectly. She has had the measure of men like him since she was eleven, when her auntie's neighbour let his hand sit too long on the back of her chair while a room full of adults agreed together not to see it, the way you decide not to see a crack in a wall you are only renting.

She gave him the phone and dialled the number herself.

Jean Luc took twenty minutes.

Twenty minutes means he was already in town. Already near. Already somewhere he could reach them from — which means that the whole time she had been walking around thinking she was choosing things, she had been the thing being watched.

He came in with his shoulders back and a fat envelope under his jacket and he did not look surprised and he did not look afraid. He looked like a man whose phone had finally rung.

When the older officer said the word *minor*, something went across his face and was gone, a flicker, like a bulb deciding whether to hold. He had planned for envelopes and not for consequences. Then it closed over and he smiled and it was as though it had never been there at all.

They took them to the room at the back.

Nothing happened to her body in that room. What happened is that three men worked out what she was worth, out loud, in two languages, in front of her, using the word *compensation*, and then agreed on it.

She sat on a plastic chair with a crack in it and did the arithmetic along with them, because she cannot help it, she has never been able to help it. And the number they landed on was smaller than the one she had been carrying in her head for eight months.

It came in two parts. The second part was for a doctor in Uvira, and it was the smaller one, and they said it in the same voice they said the first.

---

She came out of that room bent over.

She did not know she was bent over. She knew only that the floor was nearer than it should have been, that her feet were going and she could not feel them arriving, and that Daudi was somewhere on her left saying something with her name in it, from a long way off, like a radio in another room.

She started counting the steps to the door.

One, two, three — the floor was concrete, pitted, a crack running out from the wall towards the desk. Four, five, six — Daudi fell in beside her, matching her without being asked, the way he always did. Seven, eight, nine — the crack was getting wider, or she was making it wider, or she had stopped being able to tell those apart. Ten, eleven, twelve — she had counted to twelve before, in another room, at a desk with a wobble in it, with a book open at the digestive system of a cow, and she had wanted the number to come out smaller and it had come out bigger.

Thirteen. Fourteen. Fifteen.

And then it was gone.

She was still walking and the number was not there. It had slipped, the way things slip when you hold them too tightly — the way she had held every single thing that year, the name, the notes, the paper under the mattress, the four days at the bottom of the basket — and not one of them had stayed. She reached for the count and got nothing. She reached again. The corridor kept arriving under her feet in pieces that would not add up, and the one thing she had ever been able to do, the one thing in the whole town that answered to her when she called it, had put her down in a police station and walked off.

She stopped.

Daudi stopped too, and did not ask, because Daudi never asks. He stood in the corridor with her, and the light came through the window in strips across the floor, and there were not an even number of them, and she could not make them even, and she stood in the middle of it with the smell of dust and men in her clothes and let it be true for about four seconds.

Then she went and got the number back.

She got it back the way you get back a thing you have dropped in water — going in after it, cold, both hands. Sixteen. Seventeen. Eighteen. It came slowly and then it came properly, and by twenty it was holding, and by twenty-five it was hers again, steady, doing what it was for.

Daudi was looking at her. He is fifteen and good and he thinks shame is the only thing that can bend a person.

It was not shame, she said. It was arithmetic.

She said it to him because he needed it, and she said it because she needed it, and both of those were true at once and she did not look at the second one.

Thirty-two. Thirty-three. Thirty-four.

Even, at the door.

They gave her the envelope on the way out. It had been fat going in and it was thin coming out, and nobody said anything about that, and nobody had to.

The lake was on her left the whole way home, with the four ships in it.

---

The house was empty. Her clothes were folded on the end of the bed, all of them, including the two from the bottom of the basket that still had four days to go. Somebody had washed them with soap and then with something after the soap — vinegar, or paraffin, whatever it is you use when soap will not do it.

She put them in the drawer. Then she stood in the doorway of the kitchen for a while, and her mother was not in it, and she went to bed.

School on the Monday. She took the nails off on the Sunday night, the same as always.

Alicia asked her in the corridor whether it was true about the man with the car. What man, she said. All right, Alicia said, and took her arm as she does, and they walked out to the gate together, and she has not asked her anything since.

---

Here is the thing she has worked out and cannot put down.

When this finishes, and it is going to finish, she has known that since the twenty minutes — somebody will be taken for it, and it will not be him. Him they will call as a witness, if they call him at all. They will take somebody visible and poor who cannot afford an envelope, and the whole town will agree to be satisfied.

She thinks it will be Morisho, because he is the one who shouts *mia tano mia tano* on the road every morning where everybody can see him.

He gave her a coin off her fare once and called her shemeji. That is the whole of what he did.

She went to her locker at the end of the day when the corridor was empty. There was a good coat of dust on the inside of the door and she wrote his name in it with one finger.

Daudi.

It is a short name. It does not take long to write and it does not take long to look at.

Then she wiped it off with the side of her hand, because a name in dust is evidence, and everything she has learned this year she has learned about what happens to people when there is evidence lying around with their name on it.

Her father was on Ave de Tanganyique with the fruit when she came past. He had the watermelons stacked in a pyramid, which takes him twenty minutes and which nobody has ever bought a watermelon because of.

She did not cross over.

On the way home she stopped at Daudi's house, and he was out, and his father was in, and his father does not look at her, which suited her.

She went into the room with the buckets and took the motherboard down off its nail.

There is a board under the third bucket that comes up if you know it comes up, and she has known since she was nine. She put the fob under it — the lion, cartoon, plastic, the kind you would give a child — and she put the board back, and she stood on it, and it did not creak.

Then she hung the motherboard on its nail, straight, as he likes it, between the skull and the hubcap.

He will find it. Not soon. But he is a boy who takes things apart, and one day he will be in that room with nothing to do.

She walked home along the shore counting the ships. There are four of them and there have always been four and they have not moved in her lifetime and they will not move in his.

It came out even, the way it does.

# ⊹ WHAT THIS EXPERIMENT FOUND

> [!note] Read this against Draft 3, not instead of it.

**1 · The controlling idea now survives without being stated.**
Draft 3 says *I am not asking to be forgiven. I am asking for it to be counted correctly.* It is the best sentence in the story and it is also the reason [[The Judging Rubric — Nine Axes|axis 7]] caps at 3 — the 5-band requires *never stated*. This version deletes it. What replaces it is the count itself: she loses it in the corridor, goes in after it, and gets it back at *thirty-four* — the same number she could not make smaller at the school gate on page one. **The idea is now carried by an arithmetic rhyme instead of an aphorism.** Whether that is a gain is the author's call and nobody else's.

**2 · Third person bought one thing first person could not: her not knowing.**
*She did not know she was bent over.* A first-person narrator cannot report her own dissociation without ending it. The whole police-station passage runs on that gap — the reader watches the counting fail from outside while feeling it fail from inside. This is the [[15 The Chair Test — Scoring the Hidden Factor|Chair Test]] axis 3 move (**reader arrives before narrator**) that Draft 3 only manages once, at the folded clothes.

**3 · The sympathy engines, and where each sits**

| Engine | Where | What the reader watches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unfair suffering | The délestage — she watches a ghost girl turn a page, then does her own homework by paraffin | Felt, not reported. Draft 3 reports it |
| Moral struggle | The kiss — she puts her hand on his heart and **waits** for a feeling that does not come | She tries and fails. Cruelty becomes evidence of damage |
| Vulnerability | The corridor — the count is lost for four seconds, then retrieved | She cracks on the page instead of being described as bent |
| Vulnerability | The photograph at night — she tries to feel chosen and gets arithmetic | Alone inside her own survival |

**4 · What it cost.** Three things in Draft 3 got weaker here and the author should know which:
- *So I kissed him* — a three-word paragraph doing enormous work — becomes *She kissed him*, which is flatter. First person owned that line.
- The confessional address (*I want to tell this part exactly, because everybody who has heard it since has decided they already know*) has no third-person equivalent and is simply gone. It was doing real work on the reader's position.
- **The story is warmer, and warmth is a risk.** Draft 3's Djamela is colder and stranger. A panel that has read 7,000 sympathetic narrators may find the cold one more memorable. → [[14 The Judges — Six Years of Chair Statements]]

**5 · Length — and this is the finding that matters most.**

**4,961 words**, measured from the file, against Draft 3's ~3,570. That is **+1,391 words, a 39% inflation**, and it leaves **39 words of headroom** before the 5,000 gate disqualifies the entry.

This is not an accident of this draft. It is what the technique costs:

| What was bought | What it spent |
| --- | --- |
| *She did not know she was bent over* | The corridor sequence went from 2 sentences to ~380 words |
| She tries to want Daudi and cannot | The kiss went from ~110 words to ~260 |
| She tries to feel chosen and cannot | The photograph went from ~60 words to ~180 |
| She hunts for a wish that won't shame him | The watermelon went from ~150 words to ~290 |

**Sympathy is bought in words, and this story does not have the words.** Draft 3 has 1,430 of headroom; this version has 39. A single further insertion breaks the gate.

> [!danger] The actual lesson for the real draft
> Do not port this wholesale. **Port one engine.** The corridor — where the count is lost and retrieved — is the highest-yield passage here and it can be done in first person for perhaps 150 words, because Djamela can narrate losing the count without narrating that she has lost it. The other three cost more than they return at this length.
>
> **Re-measure in Word before trusting any number in this file**, including this one — the vault's counts disagree with each other and Word's is the only one the gate cares about.
