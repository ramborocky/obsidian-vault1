---

Nobody tells you the exact day your body stops being only yours. Hers picked a Tuesday.

She was behind the school gate counting on her fingers, the way you count coins when you already know they are not enough. **Thirty-four days.** She counted twice, and the first time she got thirty-three, and she wanted the smaller number to be the true one, so she counted again and it got bigger.

That is not how counting is supposed to work.

Alicia was talking about somebody's hairdo and whether the girl had paid for it herself.

---

They live in Mihogoni, the down part of town, where the road stops being a road and becomes a suggestion.

Her father sells fruit on Ave de Tanganyique in the mornings and lays his hands on people in the evenings, and both of these are jobs, and neither is the kind you say out loud at school. People come to the second one with what they have. A chicken. A phone with a cracked screen. A bag of charcoal, half. A husband's shirt. School fees in an envelope that has been opened and closed many times. A daughter.

He takes all of it in the same way, which is with both hands, and he does not rank them.

There is a gutter that came off the side of the house the year before last and is still lying where it landed.

---

The road is shouted in Swahili and it is the same Swahili they shout across the water, so the boats bring back words and nobody notices they have arrived.

French is what things are written in. The school register, the notice board, the forms, the sign on the bank that has not been a bank for six years. Nobody's grandmother has it and everybody's certificate is in it.

Lingala comes from Kinshasa. It arrives with the army and with men who have been posted here. It is spoken over the top of you rather than to you. It is the only language in this town that people learn by being addressed in it.

Across the water they do all the same shouting in the same Swahili and then write it down in English. At Ujiji there is a monument where one white man found another white man and said a sentence about it. Everybody on earth knows that sentence except the people who live there. A man repaints it. Nobody asked him to and nobody pays him and he has done it for eleven years.

The railway ends here. It was built to go further and it does not. In the afternoons men sit at the station the way men sit at a bedside. Papa Kabeya has the 1974 timetable entire — departures, arrivals, the meal service. He will give it to you if you stand still long enough.

> *Kaiserliche Werft, Papenburg. Zerlegt verladen: 5.000 Teile. The vessel is delivered in sections by rail and assembled upon the shore. She is armed. Draught permits any port upon either coast. Eingeborene reisen auf dem Achterdeck.*

She turned thirteen in March and her father bought a watermelon.

Not a cake. A watermelon, with one candle pushed into the top of it, standing crooked because a watermelon is not built to hold a candle, which anybody could have told him.

*Baba, you could have got a cake. Even a cheap one.*

*Watermelons are healthier. More natural. More African. Cakes are a western thing.*

*Watermelons are sweet,* her mother said, *like you, Djamela* — in the voice she uses when she is helping him be right about something.

She wants to be fair to him, because she has not always been. He is not a stupid man. He was handed a town that had been emptied out twice before he got to it, and he has nothing to give her except proverbs and fruit, and he decided that if he refused the world outside Kalemie hard enough she would stop wanting it. It did not work like that. It only made the wanting private.

*Make a wish. Blow.*

To be Kim Kardashian, amen.

*What is Kim Kardashian,* her father said.

*A social media influencer,* her mother said, fast, the way you move a knife off a table.

The smoke went up and sat under the iron roof and did not leave.

---

In the evenings he took Patrice down to the water and taught him.

He carried stones in his pocket. He did not look for them at the shore like a man who had not planned to teach. He stood side-on and threw flat and it went four, five, six.

***Count them out loud.*** Patrice counted. It came out at two.

*Your wrist. Not the water. Never the water.*

Some evenings he put the phone face up on the sand and let a long one run into the sebene, and held up a hand at the exact place the thing turned, before it turned, every time. *You heard it after. I heard it before. That is not a gift. That is forty years.* Patrice claps on one and three. Radjabu told him that is a Protestant's clap and can be unlearned, but not quickly.

Once, in one of those songs, a man's name went past in the middle of the shouting. Thrown in and gone in less than a second. Radjabu said that one was paid for, and how much. The man has a depot at Kalundu. He wants a particular woman to hear his name in a song and understand what kind of man has his name in a song.

*They call it a stone,* he said. *You throw it in. Where it lands is not your business.*

Then he gave Patrice another stone out of his pocket and told him to count.

*Do not follow the loudest. The loudest is being paid.*

She was on the porch for this, nearer the house than the water. He had a listener and it was not her. She has never decided whether that was a thing taken from her or a thing she got out of.

Four hulls sit off the point. They went down in a season nobody wrote down. Under the middle of that water a line was let down in 1858 and found no bottom at three hundred fathoms. Nothing there has turned over since before the town had this name or the two before it. The men who wrote that down were carried the last part of the way.

---

Patrice has a wall in his room where he nails things he has found. A hubcap. The skull of an antelope. A bicycle wheel she is fairly sure he did not find. He calls it a collection and everybody else calls it rubbish.

When they were small she gave him a motherboard. She found it behind the internet café in a heap of things that had stopped working, and it had been rained on for two seasons, which is what had killed it, and neither of them knew that. It was green with little gold roads on it going nowhere. She gave it to him because he was the only person who would understand that a thing can be beautiful after it stops being useful.

He nailed it up between the skull and the hubcap and has never once mentioned where he got it. Patrice thinks he found everything.

His room has three buckets under the places where the roof has given up. The third stands on a board that is not nailed down, and the ground under that board is the only dry ground in the house.

> *Procès-verbal de renflouement, Albertville. Le navire, sabordé par l'ennemi en retraite, a été relevé d'une profondeur de vingt mètres et reconnu sain. Frais de renflouement recouvrables. Les indigènes voyagent sur le pont arrière.*

The car was a BMW, black, with windows you could not see into.

They had got off the dala dala at the dagaa stop, and behind them Morisho was still shouting mia tano mia tano and getting smaller, and the boda boda men were doing what boda boda men do, which is call you beautiful in a way that is not a compliment but a notice.

Then a horn, different from theirs. The window came down with a sound she had never heard a window make, not the clink of a handle going round but a swish, like a word said quietly on purpose.

*Je m'appelle Jean Luc. Tu es d'où, ma belle.*

*Rue Saint Michel,* she said.

She lied immediately and easily and without deciding to. Rue Saint Michel is where the ghost children live and she has never set foot on it, and it came out of her mouth as though it had been sitting behind her teeth for years, waiting.

Patrice started saying the thing about strangers. *Mama alisema —*

*We shouldn't talk to strangers,* said Jean Luc, finishing it for him. And he smiled.

He knew what he was, and he said it out loud, in a joke, in front of both of them, and he was so certain it would cost him nothing that he was right.

He told them he was a director. Consul général de Belgique. He said it in French with Lingala underneath, and his French was worse than he thought it was, and she noticed, and she decided not to have noticed.

That is the part she would like written down somewhere. Not that she was fooled. That she did the deciding.

*Je m'appelle,* he asked.

*Chantal.*

Nobody had asked her for a name before. They had only ever used the one she came with. He handed her that name like a gift and she took it like one, and it was a collar with a good buckle.

Patrice did not say one word after mama alisema. She did not turn her head. The hurt came off him like heat off a wall and she stood in it and warmed her hands, because a girl who has just been chosen wants somebody there to see her be chosen.

She was thirteen. She is not asking to be forgiven for that. She is asking for it to be counted correctly.

---

It rained in April and she ran to his house because it was closer than her own.

He had physics papers across the mattress and he moved them for her without being asked.

*Do you understand entropy.*

*It is a natural state. Order needs energy. Disorder is what happens when you stop.*

*My father says the lake is the opposite. He says it puts things back in order, and that it remembers everything in its right place.*

*Your father is a wise man,* Patrice said. Then, a minute later: *it is hydrogen and oxygen, and it has no intelligence.*

The rain came through the roof and hit the buckets and the sound filled that room so completely there was nothing else in it. The note of the second bucket went lower as it filled. Her knee was touching the edge of his knee. She had put it there.

*Then what do you want to remember, Mr Scientist. Encrypted data? Binary codes? Or a colourful moment with no meaning in it?*

He did not answer and he did not move. She watched a boy who wanted something more than he has wanted anything hold completely still, because moving might have cost him the version of her he kept in his head.

So she kissed him.

**It lasted about twenty seconds. She counted, because she counts everything.**

*See? Not everything falls apart.*

That kiss cost her nothing at all. It was the cheapest thing she has ever given anybody and he has carried it around ever since like the down payment on a house, and she let him, and she had his side of it exactly, the whole time he was having it.

The third bucket filled twice that afternoon and the board under it stayed dry.

> *Tanganyika Territory. The vessel, having been raised and refitted, is transferred to this Administration and is renamed. She shall be entered as a mail and passenger steamer upon the said waters. It shall be lawful for any officer to board and search. Deck passengers to provide their own covering.*

---

She is not going to describe the hotel.

Here is what she has instead, which is the inventory, because a list is the only thing she has ever been able to hold the world in.

A key fob shaped like a lion, cartoon, plastic, the kind you would give a child. It opens a door on the third floor. Left pocket, where the seam has gone. Never the right.

A perfume, Shalis by Remy Marquis, not hers, which gets into the weave of a khanga and will not come out with soap, only with time, so she learned to leave certain clothes at the bottom of the basket for four days before her mother does the wash.

Fingernails, done every Friday and taken off every Monday before school.

A photograph on her phone. Her, and at the edge of the frame a hand on her shoulder with a gold ring on it, and the ring is not real gold and he wears it like a crown. She tagged it blessed and forty-one people liked it and none of them asked her anything.

Two vaccinations, low on the arm, near the elbow, not where they usually put them.

Anybody who wanted to could have read that. It was all on the outside of her for months.

---

They went to Ave de Bobozo about the car.

She says the car. There had been a car, and it came off the road at the corner near the market and did not stop, and her leg has a mark on it going yellow at the edges. That was true, and she wants it known that one of the things she said that year was true.

The station smelled of dust and men. A bench along one wall with three of its slats replaced in a paler wood. A woman in a black dress explaining that it was not prostitution, it was escort services, they had cards with a company number on them. *More like your pimp's number,* the officer said. Everybody laughed except her and Djamela.

The young one typed with one finger, hunting each letter down as though it owed him something. He typed in French, because that is what a form is. He said *mhh, what do we have here* in Swahili, because that is what a road is. And when the older one came over the two of them talked across her in Lingala, which is what they do when the thing being decided is not the thing being said.

Neither of them is from here. You can hear it in one sentence and everybody in that queue had heard it.

*She is reporting a hit and run,* Patrice said.

*So why are you talking. You sound like you know more than she does.* And he winked at Patrice, one man to another, over the top of her.

So she held out the phone with the photograph on it, because she had worked out on the way there that a hit and run was worth nothing and a man like that was worth something.

*We deal with hard evidence. Not AI. Not phantom ghosts.*

She leaned close enough to smell onion and whiskey on him, and backed off, and said it.

*My menstrual cycle. There is a latency of more than a month.*

The air in that room changed as though a door had opened somewhere.

The older one came over, big gut on him, the kind you get from a lifetime of small envelopes and large drinks, put his hand flat on the counter, and said that in this country there is Article 15.

He said it the way the priest at St Augustine says the parts he has said ten thousand times.

*Article 15. Débrouillez-vous.* He said every citizen of this republic has it and it is the only article that has never once been suspended. He said the constitution has been rewritten four times in his lifetime and Article 15 has survived all four, because it was never in any of them.

He said: it means the state has considered your situation.

Then he laughed, and looked at the young one, and the young one laughed, and Patrice laughed half a second late because he is fifteen and had understood that it was a joke before he understood what kind.

The young one kept typing. Then, quietly, and in Lingala, so that it would be for the room and not for the form: *where is his number. We will call him. We will summon him now. We will finish the case, if you understand me.*

She understood him perfectly, in a language nobody in her house speaks. She has had the measure of men like him since she was eleven, when her auntie's neighbour let his hand sit too long on the back of her chair while a room full of adults agreed together not to see it, the way you decide not to see a crack in a wall you are only renting.

Jean Luc took twenty minutes.

Twenty minutes means he was already in town, already near, already somewhere he could reach them from, which means that the whole time she had been walking around thinking she was choosing things, she had been the thing being watched.

He came in with his shoulders back and a fat envelope under his jacket. He did not look surprised and he did not look afraid. He looked like a man whose phone had finally rung.

When the older officer said the word minor, something went across his face and was gone, a flicker, like a bulb deciding whether to hold. He had planned for envelopes and not for consequences. Then it closed over and he smiled and it was as though it had never been there.

---

They took them to the room at the back.

Nothing happened to her body in that room. What happened is that three men worked out what she was worth, out loud, in two languages, in front of her, using the word compensation, and then agreed on it.

**She sat on a plastic chair with a crack in it and did the arithmetic along with them, because she cannot help it, she has never been able to help it. The number they landed on was smaller than the one she had been carrying in her head for eight months.**

It came in two parts. The second part was for a doctor in Uvira, and it was the smaller one, and they said it in the same voice they said the first.

On the way out the older one told her she should be grateful, because in some countries this would have taken years.

She came out of that room bent over. Patrice was there when she came out, and she can tell you what he thought, because he is fifteen and good and he thinks shame is the only thing that can bend a person.

It was not shame. It was arithmetic.

The envelope had been fat going in and it was thin coming out and nobody said anything about that and nobody had to.

The house was empty when she got home. Her clothes were folded on the end of the bed, all of them, including the two from the bottom of the basket that still had four days to go. Washed with soap and then with something after the soap, vinegar or paraffin, whatever it is you use when soap will not do it.

Somebody had worked out what was in that cloth, and gone up the road for the right thing, and come back, and used it, and said nothing.

She put them in the drawer and stood in the doorway of the kitchen for a while and her mother was not in it.

---

She crossed in the third month, on the deck, at night, because that is when it goes.

The announcements are made twice, in Swahili and then in English. The Swahili is the same Swahili as the road outside her house. The English is for nobody on that deck. It is the only journey in her life where the language does not change when she arrives.

She sat against the rail on the north side with her back to the town. Women asleep in a row with their covering over their faces. A man with two goats tied to the same rail, and the goats did not settle either. The engine is under everything and you stop hearing it after an hour and then you hear it again whenever it changes.

She had a bag with almost nothing in it, which is what people carry when they are going somewhere they intend to leave the same day.

That boat was built somewhere else in five thousand pieces and screwed together on this sand. The men who owned it sank it themselves. The men who came next raised it and charged the cost of raising it to somebody. The men after that renamed it and put it on a timetable. It is older than the country and older than the last two names of the country. It has been the property of four different flags. It has never once stopped going up and down the same water.

Somewhere past the second town a man started singing and three others came in under him. It went on a long while and turned into a sebene with nobody playing anything, just the voices and a hand on a jerrycan. It did not stop when the singing stopped, because a sebene does not stop. It goes until the man decides.

She thought about which one of them was not singing.

She went up the road at Uvira before it was light and came back down it about eleven hours later and stood in the queue for the deck and crossed again with the light behind her.

She is not going to describe that either.

---

School on the Monday. Alicia asked her in the corridor whether it was true about the man with the car. She said what man. She said all right, and took her arm as she does, and has not asked her anything since.

Somebody will be taken for it. She has known that since the twenty minutes. It will not be him — him they will call as a witness, if they call him at all. They will take somebody visible and poor who cannot afford an envelope, and the whole town will agree to be satisfied, and she thinks it will be Morisho, because he is the one who shouts mia tano mia tano on the road every morning where everybody can see him.

*Do not follow the loudest. The loudest is being paid.*

Her father said that on a shore two years ago, about a man in a song, to a boy who is not his son, and it was never once said to her.

She went to her locker when the corridor was empty and wrote his name in the dust on the inside of the door with one finger.

Patrice.

Then she wiped it off with the side of her hand, because a name in dust is evidence, and everything she has learned this year she has learned about what happens to people when there is evidence lying around with their name on it.

---

Things that were true that season and are true still. A boy jumped off the Lukuga bridge in December and broke both wrists, and jumped again the following December. Bahati's brother came back from Lubumbashi with a suitcase of shoes and every shoe in it was for the left foot, and he sold them all. The tailor by the market keeps a photograph taped inside his machine of a woman who is not his wife and has never taken it down or explained it. Papa Kabeya still has the timetable. The man at Ujiji repainted the monument in August, the twelfth year.

None of that has anything to do with any of this.

---

Her father was on Ave de Tanganyique with the fruit when she came past, the watermelons stacked in a pyramid, which takes him twenty minutes and which nobody has ever bought a watermelon because of. She did not cross over.

She stopped at Patrice's house and he was out and his father was in and his father does not look at her, which suited her.

It was the dry season and nothing was coming through the roof. The three buckets stood empty with a white ring dried into the bottom of each.

She took the motherboard down off its nail. There is a board under the third bucket that comes up if you know it comes up, and she has known since she was nine. She put the fob under it — the lion, cartoon, plastic, the kind you would give a child — and put the board back, and stood on it, and it did not creak.

Then she hung the motherboard on its nail, straight, as he likes it, between the skull and the hubcap.

He will find it. Not soon. But he is a boy who takes things apart, and one day he will be in that room with nothing to do.

She walked home along the shore counting.

**There are four of them and there have always been four and they have not moved in her lifetime and they will not move in his. It came out even, the way it does.**

Morisho gave her a coin off her fare once, and he did it in front of everybody on that dala dala, and he called her shemeji.

# ⊹ WHAT THE SIXTH PASS FOUND

**1 · One object carrying the ascendancy beats five authorities, and it is the same lesson *Dite* teaches.**
*Dite* runs four registers over **one substance**. The epigraphs here are now **one ship in four hands** — *Kaiserliche Werft, Papenburg, 5.000 Teile* → *procès-verbal de renflouement, Albertville* → *Tanganyika Territory… is renamed* → *Safari kila Jumatano.* Built in pieces, sunk by its own owners, raised and charged for, renamed, timetabled. **The sequence is the history and no sentence of prose states it.**

> **The clause that runs through all four and does the damage: who travels on the after deck.** German — *Eingeborene reisen auf dem Achterdeck.* Belgian — *les indigènes voyagent sur le pont arrière.* British — *deck passengers to provide their own covering.* Now — *staha… watoto chini ya miaka mitatu bure.* **Four flags, four languages, one deck, and by the last one the category has stopped being racial and become a fare.** Nobody comments. Then she is on that deck.

**2 · Four languages, four jobs, and the police station is where all of them are in one room.**
He **types in French** because that is what a form is, **says *mhh what do we have here* in Swahili** because that is what a road is, and **talks across her in Lingala** because that is what men do when the thing being decided is not the thing being said. Then: *she understood him perfectly, in a language nobody in her house speaks.* **Lingala is the only language in the story that arrives by being aimed at someone**, and the story says that once, flatly, four hundred words in, and never returns to it.

**3 · Swahili crosses the water and nothing else does.** *The road is shouted in Swahili and it is the same Swahili they shout across the water, so the boats bring back words and nobody notices they have arrived.* Paid off on the deck: announcements in Swahili and then English, *and the English is for nobody on that deck.* **It is the only journey in her life where the language does not change when she arrives** — and it is the journey to Uvira.

**4 · *Shemeji* is now the last word and the title, and that fixes the oldest open fault in the vault.**
[[The NAPLAN Floor — Ten Mechanical Criteria|Finding B]] has stood since July: *shemeji* is unglossed, unrecoverable, and carries the ending. **A gloss would kill it. Placement does not.** The word is now (i) the title, (ii) the final word of the story, and (iii) preceded by *he did it in front of everybody on that dala dala* — so a reader without Swahili gets **public** and **kinship** from the staging and never needs the dictionary. This is *Dite* ending on *dite*, and it is the cleanest available answer to a fault three notes have flagged and none has solved.

**5 · MABANGA fuses at the object, not at the plot.**
A *libanga* is a stone thrown into a song for money. Radjabu throws stones on the water and makes a boy count the touches. **The same man, in the same paragraph, does both** — explains who paid for the name that went past, how much, and that the payer has a depot at Kalundu — and then *"they call it a stone. You throw it in. Where it lands is not your business,"* **and hands the boy another stone and tells him to count.** The story never puts the two economies in one sentence. → [[MABANGA (New Story — AI Draft, Not Canon)]]

**6 · Patrice throughout**, and the name does work the old one could not: it is the most loaded first name in the country, worn by a fifteen-year-old who nails rubbish to a wall and cannot bring himself to speak. **The story never mentions it.**

**7 · Kept intact from [[mihogoni-3e]]:** Article 15 and the half-second laugh · the healer's list with a daughter in it · five countings, all hers · the six leakages and *none of that has anything to do with any of this* · Faulkner's white space, fleeting profiles, found objects, and an ending two paragraphs early.

**8 · 4,471 words of prose.** The chain, measured each time: 4,469 · 4,879 · 3,416 *(four scenes lost)* · 5,220 · 4,144 · **4,471**. The +327 over the fifth pass is the language stratification and the libanga paragraph, both new material rather than restored material.

**9 · The honest risk.** Four languages named and demarcated is one demarcation more than the prose can carry invisibly, and the paragraph beginning *The road is shouted in Swahili* is the closest this draft comes to **explaining its own world** rather than showing it. It survives because it is early, flat, and never returned to — but it is the first place a hostile reader would put a pencil, and I would not defend it as hard as I would defend the deck clause.
