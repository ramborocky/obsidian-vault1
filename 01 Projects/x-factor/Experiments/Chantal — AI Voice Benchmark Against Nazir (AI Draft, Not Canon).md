# CHANTAL
### *Claude's version — voice and prose-control benchmark, 2026-07-29*

They say a girl in Mihogoni knows the day it happens to her before it happens, the way you know rain from the smell of the dust first. That is not true of me. Mine picked a Tuesday and I did not smell a thing.

I was behind the school gate counting on my fingers, the way you count coins when you already know they are not enough. Thirty-four days. I counted twice, and the first time I got thirty-three, and I wanted the smaller number to be the true one, so I counted again and it got bigger. I have always been good at arithmetic. It is the one thing in my life that has never once lied to me, which is more than I can say for the rest of it.

Alicia was talking about somebody's hairdo and whether the girl had paid for it herself. That is not how counting is supposed to work, I thought, and did not say, because Alicia would have wanted to know what counting.

The bell went. She took my arm as she does and I let her, and we walked in past the notice board with last term's examination list still pinned to it, and I sat at a desk with a wobble in it and opened a book about the digestive system of a cow. There are four stomachs. I can still name all four. I have never once needed to.

I am telling you about the Tuesday first because everybody wants to start with the man. The man is not the beginning of anything except his own story, and I am not in the mood to give him mine.

We live in Mihogoni, the down part of town, where the road stops being a road and becomes a suggestion. My father sells fruit on Ave de Tanganyique in the mornings and lays his hands on people in the afternoons, and both of these are jobs, and neither is the kind you say out loud at school. The boats come over from Kigoma with dagaa and secondhand words, and we keep both, so half of what gets shouted on this road is Tanzanian and nobody here has ever thought to mention it.

There is a gutter that came off our side of the house the year before last and is still lying where it landed, and a woman who sells charcoal on the corner that everyone calls Bahati, though I have never once heard anybody use it to her face. When I was small and told her our house smelled of fish, thinking it was a secret, she looked at me the way you look at a child who has just told you the sea is wet, and said, *and? So does the lake.* I laughed because that is the fastest way to stop being the thing people are laughing at. Our house does smell of fish. That is not the part that stayed with me. Bahati is.

There are children we call the ghost kids. They go to Monseigneur André Kaseba in checkered skirts and blazers and sit in the back of their parents' cars looking at nothing. When the délestage takes our side of town their windows stay yellow, and I have stood on the road and watched them doing their homework in that light like it was owed to them. When our dala dala stops beside one of their cars at the lights on Ave de Industrielle, we look in and they do not look out. Not because they are proud. Because we are not there to be looked at.

Morisho hangs off the side of the dala dala and shouts *mia tano mia tano* until his voice goes thin at the edges. He told us once that those children are ghosts to us — that we will never see them or talk to them. He meant it as a warning. I heard it as a price list, and I have never once told him that, because a man who gives you a coin off your fare and calls you *shemeji* has already decided you are family enough not to need the warning explained twice.

I turned thirteen in March and my father bought a watermelon. Not a cake. A watermelon, with one candle pushed into the top of it, standing crooked, because a watermelon is not built to hold a candle and nobody had told him that in time to matter.

*Baba, you could have got a cake. Even a cheap one.*

*Watermelons are healthier,* he said. *More natural. More African. Cakes are a western thing.*

My mother said watermelons are sweet, like me, in the voice she uses when she is helping him be right about something. I want to be fair to him, because I have not always been. He was handed a town emptied out twice before he got to it, and he has nothing to give me except proverbs and fruit, and he decided that if he refused the world outside Kalemie hard enough I would stop wanting it. It did not work like that. It only made the wanting private, the way everything in this house eventually goes private, one drawer at a time.

*Make a wish,* he said. *Blow.*

I closed my eyes and wished to be Kim Kardashian, amen.

*What is Kim Kardashian,* my father said.

*A social media influencer,* my mother said, fast, the way you move a knife off a table before a child reaches for the wrong end of it.

I blew the candle out. The smoke sat under the iron roof and did not leave, the way things here generally don't.

Later I sat on the porch with Daudi, the lake in front of us with its four rusted ships that have not moved in my lifetime and will not move in his. *He thinks I want things,* I said.

Daudi said nothing. That is not temperament. That is a decision he makes so often it has started to look like temperament, which is a different thing, and I did not know yet how much that difference was going to cost both of us.

*I do want things,* I said. *I want to control them. I want to conquer like the Europeans did, except now you do not need weapons or degrees. You only need followers.*

I was thirteen years and one hour old and I believed all of it, worked out myself from the evidence in front of me — a town where the people with cars had taken things and the people without cars had been taken from.

Daudi is the boy from two houses down, a year and a half older, with a wall in his room where he nails things he has found. A hubcap. The skull of an antelope. A bicycle wheel I am fairly sure he did not find. He calls it a collection. Everybody else calls it rubbish. His mother died having him — a fact that gets said about him rather than by him — and he has built a whole woman out of that absence, patient, never once disappointed in him, because a woman who never existed does not have to be. He has never said one word of this to me. I have it anyway, entire, the shape of a room nobody has ever put a light in.

When we were small I found a motherboard behind the internet café that used to be by the market, in a heap of things that had stopped working. Green, with little gold roads on it going nowhere. I gave it to him because he was the only person who would understand that a thing can be beautiful after it stops being useful. He nailed it to the wall between the skull and the hubcap and has never once mentioned where he got it. I do not think he remembers it was me. Daudi thinks he found everything. Let him.

The car was a BMW, black, windows you could not see into. We had got off the dala dala at the dagaa stop, Morisho still shouting behind us and getting smaller, the boda boda men doing what boda boda men do, which is call you beautiful in a way that is not a compliment but a notice — that everyone has agreed you are available for comment now.

Then a horn, different from theirs. The window came down with a sound I had never heard a window make — not the clink of a handle going round but a swish, like a word said quietly on purpose.

*Je m'appelle Jean Luc. Tu es d'où, ma belle.*

I want to tell this part exactly, because everybody who has heard it since has decided they already know it.

*Rue Saint Michel,* I said.

I lied immediately and without deciding to. Rue Saint Michel is where the ghost children live and I have never set foot on it, and it came out of my mouth as though it had been waiting behind my teeth for years for someone worth saying it to.

Daudi started the thing about strangers. *Mama alisema* —

*We shouldn't talk to strangers,* said Jean Luc, finishing it for him, and smiled. He knew what he was and said it out loud, as a joke, in front of both of us, so certain it would cost him nothing that he was right. I have thought about that smile more than about anything else that happened after it.

He said he was a director. *Consul général de Belgique.* French with Lingala underneath, worse French than he thought it was, and I noticed, and decided not to have noticed. That is the part I would like written down somewhere. I did the deciding.

He wrote a number on paper and put ten thousand under it. Ten thousand is a school skirt. A man with a car like that had stopped it, in the road, in front of the boda boda men, for me.

*Je m'appelle,* he asked.

*Chantal,* I said. Nobody had asked me for a name before — they had only ever used the one I came with. He handed me that name like a gift, and I took it like one, and it was a collar with a good buckle.

Daudi stood beside me the whole time and did not say one word after *mama alisema.* I did not turn my head. The hurt came off him like heat off a wall and I stood in it and warmed my hands, because a girl who has just been chosen wants somebody there to see her be chosen.

I was thirteen. I am not asking to be forgiven for that. I am asking for it to be counted correctly.

I went home and put the ten thousand under the mattress on my side. My mother does the wash on Thursdays and has done since before I could count, so I had until Thursday to decide where it would live.

It rained in April, hard, the way it does, and I ran to Daudi's house because it was closer than mine. His room is one room — baked brick, a window the size of two hands, a foam mattress on the floor, buckets under the places where the roof has given up. He had physics papers spread across the mattress and moved them for me without being asked, both hands, careful of the order they were in.

*Do you understand entropy,* I said. I had heard him say it eleven times. I asked because I wanted him to be the person who knew something, for once, in front of me.

*It is a natural state,* he said. *Order needs energy. Disorder is what happens when you stop.*

*My father says the lake is the opposite. He says it puts things back in order, and remembers everything in its right place.*

*Your father is a wise man.* Then, a minute later: *it is hydrogen and oxygen. It has no intelligence.*

We sat while the rain came through the roof and hit the buckets and the sound filled the room so completely there was nothing else in it. My knee was touching the edge of his knee. I had put it there.

*Then what do you want to remember, Mister Scientist. Encrypted data? Binary codes? Or a colourful moment with no meaning in it?*

He did not answer and did not move. I watched a boy who wanted something more than he had ever wanted anything hold completely still, because moving might have cost him the version of me he kept in his head.

So I kissed him. It lasted about twenty seconds. I counted, because I count everything, and the counting did not fail me once, which tells you something about what I felt, or what I made sure I did not.

*See,* I said, pulling back. *Not everything falls apart.*

His face was the face of somebody shown the back door of the universe. That kiss cost me nothing at all. It was the cheapest thing I have ever given anybody, and he has carried it since like the down payment on a house, and I let him, and I had his side of it exactly, the whole time he was having it.

I am not going to describe the hotel. Here is what I have instead — the inventory, because a list is the only shape I have ever been able to hold the world in without it spilling.

A key fob shaped like a lion, cartoon, plastic, the kind you give a child, that opens a door on the third floor. I keep it in my left pocket, where the seam has gone, and never in my right.

A perfume, Shalis by Remy Marquis, not mine, that gets into the weave of a khanga and will not come out with soap, only with time, so I learned to leave certain clothes at the bottom of the basket for four days.

Fingernails, done every Friday and taken off every Monday before school, because at school there are rules and at the hotel there are different rules, and I live between the two of them the way you cross a road that never fully stops.

A photograph on my phone — me, and at the edge of the frame a hand with a gold ring that is not real gold and is worn like a crown anyway. I tagged it *blessed* and forty-one people liked it and none of them asked me anything.

Two vaccinations, low on the arm, near the elbow, not where they usually put them. Anybody who wanted to could have read that. It was on the outside of me for months, and the town looked straight through it, the way it looks through everything it has already decided not to see.

We went to Ave de Bobozo about the car — there had been a car, it came off the road near the market and did not stop, and my leg still carries a mark going yellow at the edges. That part was true, and I want you to know that one of the things I said that year was true.

Daudi came because I did not want to go alone. He said it was because his bicycle was stolen once, so he knew the forms.

The station smelled of dust and men. A woman in a black dress was explaining to an officer that it was not prostitution, it was escort services, they had cards with a company number on them. *More like your pimp's number,* he said, and everybody laughed except her and me. On the bench beside her, someone had left a rosary with three beads missing, counted down to nothing by whoever prayed on it last, and I looked at the gap where the beads should be longer than I looked at anything else in that room.

The officer who took us was young and typing with one finger, hunting each letter down as though it owed him something. *Mhh. What do we have here.*

*She is reporting a hit and run,* Daudi said.

*So why are you talking. You sound like you know more than she does.* He winked at Daudi, one man to another, over the top of me.

He asked where, what car, who was driving, typing at the speed of a man who had already priced this. So I held out my phone and showed him the photograph with the hand and the ring, because I had worked out on the way there that a hit and run was worth nothing and a man like that was worth something.

*We deal with hard evidence. Not AI. Not phantom ghosts.*

I leaned close enough to smell onion and whiskey on him, backed off, and said it. *My menstrual cycle. There is a latency of more than a month.*

The air in that room changed as though a door had opened somewhere behind it.

The older one came over, gut on him, the kind you get from a lifetime of small envelopes and large drinks, and began reciting — the penal code, a teenage minor, rape, thirty years and twelve lashes on top, in the voice the priest at St Augustine uses for the parts he has said ten thousand times. *A buccal swab will suffice as conclusive evidence. Shame on the predator, for luring young girls.*

The young one kept typing. Then, quietly: *where is his number. We will call him. We will summon him now. Yes. We will finish the case, if you understand me.*

I understood him perfectly. I have had the measure of men like him since I was eleven, when my auntie's neighbour let his hand sit too long on the back of my chair while a room full of adults agreed together not to see it, the way you decide not to see a crack in a wall you are only renting.

I gave him the phone and dialled the number myself. Jean Luc took twenty minutes. Twenty minutes means he was already in town, already near, already somewhere he could reach us from — which means that the whole time I had been walking around thinking I was choosing things, I had been the thing being watched.

He came in with his shoulders back and a fat envelope under his jacket, not surprised, not afraid — a man whose phone had finally rung. When the older officer said the word *minor*, something crossed Jean Luc's face and was gone, a flicker, like a bulb deciding whether to hold. He had planned for envelopes and not for consequences. Then it closed over, and he smiled, and it was as though it had never been there at all.

They took us to the room at the back.

Nothing happened to my body in that room. What happened is that three men worked out what I was worth, out loud, in two languages, in front of me, using the word *compensation*, and then agreed on it.

I sat on a plastic chair with a crack in it and did the arithmetic along with them, because I cannot help it, and the number they landed on was smaller than the one I had been carrying in my head for eight months. It came in two parts. The second was for a doctor in Uvira, and it was the smaller one, and they said it in the same flat voice they had said the first.

I came out of that room bent over.

The corridor had eleven ceiling tiles and one of them had a water stain shaped like nothing. I counted the tiles. I counted the slats on the bench, and there were still three replaced in a paler wood, and I counted those separately from the others because they did not belong to the same sum. I counted the steps to the door and got fourteen, then twelve, then fourteen again, and none of the numbers were the number I actually needed, and for the first time in my life the counting would not land anywhere, would not resolve, kept sliding off the edge of itself the way water finds every crack in a wall except the one you're pointing at.

It was not shame. It was arithmetic.

They gave me the envelope on the way out. Fat going in, thin coming out, and nobody said anything about that, and nobody had to.

The lake was on my left the whole way home, the four ships sitting in it exactly where they always sit, and I looked at them the way you look at something that has agreed, at least, not to move on you.

The house was empty when I got home. My clothes were folded on the end of the bed, all of them, including the two from the bottom of the basket that still had four days to go. Somebody had washed them with soap and then with something after the soap — vinegar, or paraffin, whatever it is you use when soap will not do it — and I understood, standing there, that my mother's hands had done this without her once asking me a single question, which was either the kindest thing anyone did for me that year or the closest anyone came to admitting they already knew, and I have never worked out which, and I am not sure she has either.

I put them in the drawer, stood in the kitchen doorway a while with my mother not in it, and went to bed.

School on Monday. I took the nails off on the Sunday night, the same as always. Alicia asked me in the corridor whether it was true about the man with the car. *What man,* I said. *All right,* she said, and took my arm as she does, and we walked out to the gate together, and she has not asked me anything since.

Here is the thing I have worked out and cannot put down. When this finishes, and it is going to finish, somebody will be taken for it, and it will not be him. Him they will call as a witness, if they call him at all. They will take somebody visible and poor who cannot afford an envelope, and the whole town will agree to be satisfied. I think it will be Morisho, because he is the one who shouts *mia tano mia tano* on the road every morning where everybody can see him being exactly who he is.

He gave me a coin off my fare once and called me *shemeji*. Not a joke, not a come-on — the word you use for a girl who might one day actually be family, said the way you'd hand someone something breakable and trust them to already know that. That is the whole of what he did, and it is going to be enough to hang him, because a town that needs someone visible does not stop to ask what the visible man actually said.

Bahati, at the charcoal corner, said it to me straight the week they took him in for questioning the first time, no name in it, no need for one. *They always find the one who was standing where they could see him.* She did not say Jean Luc's name. She did not have to. Everyone on that road already knew whose name she wasn't saying, and that not-saying did more work than saying it would have.

I went to my locker at the end of the day, when the corridor was empty, and there was a good coat of dust on the inside of the door, and I wrote his name in it with one finger. *Daudi.* A short name. It does not take long to write and it does not take long to look at. Then I wiped it off with the side of my hand, because a name in dust is evidence, and everything I learned that year I learned about what happens to people when there is evidence lying around with their name on it.

My father was on Ave de Tanganyique with the fruit when I came past, the watermelons stacked in a pyramid, which takes him twenty minutes and which nobody has ever bought a watermelon because of. I did not cross over.

On the way home I stopped at Daudi's house. He was out. His father was in, and does not look at me, which suited me. I went into the room with the buckets and took the motherboard down off its nail. There is a board under the third bucket that comes up if you know it comes up, and I have known since I was nine. I put the fob under it — the lion, cartoon, plastic, the kind you'd give a child — and put the board back and stood on it, and it did not creak. Then I hung the motherboard on its nail, straight, the way he likes it, between the skull and the hubcap, so that the one thing I ever gave him is now sitting directly above the one thing I am giving him without telling him I'm giving it.

He will find it. Not soon. But he is a boy who takes things apart, and one day he will be in that room with nothing to do, and he will lift a bucket, and the floor will tell him what I would not.

I walked home along the shore counting the ships. There are four of them and there have always been four and they have not moved in my lifetime and they will not move in his. It came out even, the way it does.

## Pseudo scene alterations — alternatives not taken

Three places where I made a call and there was at least one other real option. This is the "view alternatives" part — pick a different one if it serves the story better than mine did.

**1. The counting-fails paragraph.** I wrote it as numbers that keep sliding — thirteen, twelve, fourteen — refusing to resolve. The alternate: have her count something that resolves *correctly* and it still doesn't help — she gets the number right and it changes nothing, which is a colder, more Barrett-style ending to that beat (competence intact, meaning absent) rather than the warmer choice I made (competence itself breaking). Your brief specified it should **fail**, which is what I wrote — flagging the alternate because "fails" and "resolves but means nothing" score differently on axis 3, interiority, and it's worth knowing you had both available.

**2. Bahati's line.** I gave the "judges a man without naming him" beat to Bahati rather than to Daudi's father or Alicia, because Bahati already exists in the draft with one prior beat (the fish-smell exchange) and reusing a minor character to carry a late thematic line is a Nazir move in itself — Marsha does exactly this. The alternate is to give that line to nobody, and let the town's silence do it instead, the way the original draft mostly does. I chose the line because you asked for 90% of Nazir specifically, and Nazir uses spoken judgment twice; your draft currently uses it zero times.

**3. The motherboard/fob echo at the end.** I made them touch directly — fob hidden under the same nail-line as the motherboard, one sentence tying them together ("the one thing I ever gave him is now sitting directly above the one thing I am giving him"). The alternate is to leave them unconnected, as your draft currently does, and trust the reader to make the link unassisted. Mine is more Nazir (the towel is explicitly narrated twice); yours is more restrained. Both are defensible — this is closer to a taste call than a craft error either way.
