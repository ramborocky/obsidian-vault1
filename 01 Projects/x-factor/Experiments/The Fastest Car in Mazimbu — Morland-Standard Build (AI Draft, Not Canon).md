## THE FASTEST CAR IN MAZIMBU

On the twelfth of April 1984 I was doing a hundred and sixty on the Dodoma road, and the radio was playing *Africa Liberate Zimbabwe*.

The asphalt was four months old. In the afternoons you could smell it going soft, a smell like something burning that was not burning, and the Peugeot sat down on it the way a hand sits on a table. Before the new surface a man drove that stretch at seventy and felt brave. Now the road gave you permission. That is the thing nobody says about a good road: it does not make you safer, it makes you willing.

The car was a 504, cream, and it was not mine. It belonged to the settlement at Mazimbu, which meant it belonged to the movement, which meant it belonged to nobody I could name. In the boot there were two crates of exercise books for the school. Under the spare wheel, flat, in a fertiliser sack, there were eleven documents from Maputo that I had been told not to open, and I had not opened them, and I could describe every one of them from the outside — the weight, the grain of the envelopes, the way the third one had been folded twice and had gone soft along the crease.

I was twenty-nine. I had been in Tanzania for six years and I had learned to say *sawa* the way people from Morogoro say it, with the middle of it flattened, and this was the achievement of my life to that point.

Moses had changed the plates himself that morning, behind the workshop, with a spanner and a bad temper. He was down on the gravel and I stood over him with the chai getting cold in my hand.

"You drive like a boer," he said, to the underside of the car.

"I drive like a man with somewhere to be."

"A boer with somewhere to be." He came up off the gravel and put the old plates in a sack and did not look at me. "Sapi. Listen. Today you go slowly."

"Why today."

"Because I am telling you." He wiped his hands on his trousers, which were already the colour of everything at Mazimbu, that red that gets into the weave and stays. "Because there are cars on that road today that you should not be near."

I asked him whose cars and he said the word *serikali* and then he did not say anything else, and I have had forty years to decide whether that was a warning or an instruction, and I am no nearer.

He gave me the maandazi in the way that men who cannot say the other thing give you food. Four of them, still warm, wrapped in paper, and I put them on the passenger seat and did not look at the paper until Dumila.

At Dumila I stopped because a lorry had shed sisal across both lanes and there was a boy of about ten directing traffic with genuine authority. I ate the first maandazi and the grease came through, and when the grease came through I could read what was under it.

It was a security assessment. Typed, carbon copy, in English, with a reference number in the top corner and a distribution list of four names on the second sheet, and it concerned the movement of certain persons through Iringa and Mbeya between January and March. It had a classification stamped on it. It had been used to wrap four maandazi from a woman at the Mazimbu gate who had bought her paper from a boy who had bought it by the kilo.

I sat there with the engine off and the sisal being cleared and I read the whole of it, because I was already guilty of having read it, and there is no half of that.

Then I put the grease-marked page in the fertiliser sack with the other eleven, because I could not think what else to do with a thing like that, and I drove away from Dumila faster than I had come into it.

That is the last hour I can account for.

---

What I have of the crash is this. I have the sound of the radio going to static, which it did on that stretch every time, at the same place, near the borehole, and which I had stopped noticing years before and noticed then. I have a white car. I have the certainty — not a memory, a certainty, they are different and I know the difference — that the white car was not going to stop and that I was not going to stop and that both of us understood this at the same moment, which is the only true intimacy I have ever had with a stranger.

And I have the smell of the road going soft.

That is all. Everything else in this account I was told, or read, or worked out, and I will say which is which, because I have learned that a man who cannot separate those three has no business speaking about that day at all.

---

I woke on the nineteenth of May in Muhimbili and a nurse called Getrude told me my name before I asked for it.

For four days I could not hold anything. I would be given a fact and I would put it down somewhere and it would be gone. She told me eight or nine times that I had been thirty-seven days asleep and each time it was news, and each time I did the arithmetic out loud, and each time I got a different answer, and she stopped correcting me.

On the fifth day I held one. She said the twelfth of April and it stayed.

"That was a bad day for everybody," she said.

I asked what she meant and she looked at the door and then she told me that the Prime Minister had died on the Dodoma road at Dakawa, on the twelfth of April, in the afternoon, and that the country had stopped for a week.

I said, "Which afternoon."

"That afternoon. The same."

I asked her the time and she said she did not know the time, nobody says the time, they say the afternoon. Then she took my chart off the end of the bed and read it and told me that I had been brought in from the Dodoma road on the twelfth of April in the afternoon.

She said it the way you say a coincidence.

I want to be exact here, because this is the centre of it. I did not think: I killed him. I thought: *there were two of us on that road at that hour going too fast, and they have only spoken about one.* That is a smaller thought and a worse one, and it has never once left me.

The bulletins came at seven and at nine. I made a boy from the next bed carry the radio to me and I listened to both for eleven days, and in eleven days of national mourning, of tributes, of Mwalimu's voice cracking on the word *ndugu*, of the whole apparatus of a state grieving in public, not one bulletin mentioned a second vehicle.

Not to deny it. To deny it you have to raise it.

---

They discharged me on a Tuesday with a stick, a paper listing what had been broken, and no clothes, because whatever I had been wearing had been cut off me and thrown away. A man from the settlement had left a shirt and it was two sizes wrong.

I walked because I had no money for a daladala and because I wanted to be outside. At the war memorial on Samora Avenue I stopped and sat on the wall, and two constables came over, one older and one about twenty.

The young one wanted my papers. I did not have papers. I had a hospital discharge sheet with my name spelled Sandimba Sapi instead of Sapi Sandimba and he read it twice.

"You are from where."

"Mazimbu."

"That is not a place."

"Morogoro. Near Morogoro."

The older one had not sat down but he had stopped standing like a policeman. He looked at the memorial, which is for the askari of a war that has nothing to do with anybody now alive, and he said, "You know we guard this at night?"

"This?"

"This. The stones." He put his hand flat on the plinth. "Two of us, from six until six. And down there" — he did not point, he moved his chin, which is more definite than pointing — "in Manzese, at night, nobody. So you tell me, mzee. Who is it we are guarding? The British Embassy? Or the poor?"

The young one said, "Baba," in the voice of a man who has heard this before and would prefer not to hear it near a stranger.

"I am asking him. He has been in the hospital, he has had time to think." He turned round to me and he was smiling and he was not joking at all. "Well?"

I said I did not know.

"No," he said. "Nobody knows. That is how it is arranged."

He gave me back the discharge sheet and told me the name on it was written wrong and that I should have it corrected, because a name written wrong will one day cost me, and then they went back to the stones.

Of everything in that year, that is the conversation I have repeated most often, and I still do not know what he thought he was telling me, or whether he thought he was telling me anything at all.

---

The car was in the police impound at Oysterbay and I went to ask about the exercise books.

That was the reason I gave and it was even partly true. Two crates of exercise books for a school run by a movement with no money, and somebody would have to account for them, and I had decided on the walk over that I would rather be a man asking about stationery than a man asking about anything else.

The desk constable took my name and went away and came back and took me out to the yard himself, which I had not expected, and which I understood only later.

The yard was full. Most of it was ordinary — buses with their fronts folded, a lorry on its side, the private cars of people who had been unlucky on ordinary days. He walked me down the second row and stopped.

There was a cream Peugeot 504 with its front quarter gone.

I looked at it for a while and I felt sorry for whoever had been in it, and then I saw the roof rack, which Moses had welded, which was welded crooked, which I had complained about for two years.

"You know it," the constable said.

"It is ours."

"Yours."

"The settlement's."

He wrote nothing down. That is the detail. He had a book and a pen in his top pocket and he wrote nothing down, and he had walked me out there himself instead of sending a boy, and both of those things are the sort of thing you only see afterwards.

I asked him about the crates and he said the vehicle had been received empty.

I said that was not possible, there were two crates of exercise books and a spare wheel and a sack, and he repeated it with the same words in the same order, the way a man repeats a thing he has been given rather than a thing he knows. *The vehicle was received empty.*

Then he said, "Somebody has been asking when you would come."

"Who?"

"Somebody." He looked at the Peugeot rather than at me. "Wait inside."

---

They put me on a wooden bench in a corridor on the first floor and I sat there for four hours.

I would like to record that I spent those four hours preparing. I did not. I spent them looking at the corridor, which had a window at one end with the glass gone and a swallow that came in and went out and came in, and at some point in the third hour I understood with complete clarity that whatever happened next would not be decided in that corridor, and after that I was calm in a way I have not been since.

Nobody interviewed me.

At about four o'clock a young man in a good shirt came out of an office with a file and asked me to confirm my name and I confirmed it. He asked me to confirm that I was resident at Mazimbu and I confirmed it. He did not ask me where I had been on the twelfth of April. He did not ask me what I had been driving, or how fast, or what was in the sack under the spare wheel, or whether I had seen a white car, or a Land Cruiser, or anything at all.

He said, "You are free to go, and there is no case."

I said, "There is no case against me?"

"There is no case," he said, and he was already turning, and he said it as a full sentence with nothing after it, and I have thought about the difference between those two sentences for forty years.

I came out of the foyer into the light and stood on the step because my leg had gone stiff on the bench.

A man came out behind me in a black Kaunda suit, well cut, and went down the steps ahead of me. His shoes were the same shoes Moses wore, the brown ones with the seam across the toe that everybody at the settlement bought in Morogoro market because they were nine hundred shillings and they lasted. I noticed them because they were wrong. Everything else about the man cost more than the car I had crashed.

A black Mercedes came round from the side of the building and he got into the back of it and it took him away up the hill.

Nobody had said his name. Nobody said his name to me at any point, then or later.

---

I got back to Mazimbu at the end of June. The school had new exercise books, which nobody could explain and nobody tried to. Moses had gone to Lusaka in May and there was no address for him and there never was one. The eleven documents were never mentioned again, not by me, not to me, and when I finally asked a man in Dar in 1991 whether anything from Maputo had gone missing in April 1984 he said that a great deal had gone missing in April 1984 and that this had been the least of it.

I did not drive again for two years. When I did drive again I found that I could not go above eighty, and that this was not a decision, and that it has not changed.

In September they finished the trial.

I heard it on the seven o'clock bulletin, sitting outside the workshop with three other men and a radio balanced on a paint tin. Dumisani Dube, South African, resident of Tanzania, convicted of causing the death by dangerous driving of Edward Moringe Sokoine on the Dodoma–Dar es Salaam road at Dakawa on the twelfth of April 1984. The speed of his vehicle was given. The sentence was given. The bulletin moved to the price of cashew.

One of the men said, "So. Now we know."

Another one said, "Mm," in the way that is not agreement.

I sat there and I did the thing I have done every year since. I put the facts next to each other and I do not join them. A Prime Minister spent fourteen months making rich men explain where their money came from, and then he died on a road. A South African was convicted. Another South African was on that road, at that hour, at a hundred and sixty, with eleven documents under his spare wheel, and no policeman in that country ever asked him a single question about it, and his car was received empty, and somebody in Morogoro shoes was driven away in a Mercedes.

I am not saying it was arranged. I have no more standing to say that than anybody else who was there, which is to say none.

I am saying that a state which had spent seventeen years demanding an account of everything from everybody did not, in my case, want one.

They had a man. He fitted. He was foreign in the right way, and fast in the right way, and he had done it, and the country could be told.

And I have wondered since whether the whole apparatus stayed quiet about me for a reason, or whether it simply never noticed — whether I have spent my life inside a conspiracy or inside a filing error. That is a real question and I cannot answer it and neither can anyone.

But I know which of the two frightens me.
