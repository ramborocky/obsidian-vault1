We live in Mihogoni, the down part of town, where the road stops being a road and becomes a suggestion.

My father sells fruit on Ave de Tanganyique in the mornings and lays his hands on people in the evenings, and both of these are jobs, and neither is the kind you say out loud at school. People come to the second one with what they have. A chicken. A phone with a cracked screen. A bag of charcoal, half. A husband's shirt. School fees in an envelope that has been opened and closed many times. A daughter.

He takes all of it in the same way, which is with both hands, and he does not rank them.

There is a gutter that came off the side of the house the year before last and is still lying where it landed.

The road is shouted in Swahili and it is the same Swahili they shout across the water, so the boats bring back words and nobody notices they have arrived.

French is what things are written in. The school register, the notice board, the forms, the sign on the bank that has not been a bank for six years. Nobody's grandmother has it and everybody's certificate is in it.

Lingala comes from Kinshasa. It arrives with the army and with men who have been posted here. It is spoken over the top of you rather than to you. It is the only language in this town that people learn by being addressed in it.

Across the water they do all the same shouting in the same Swahili and then write it down in English. At Ujiji there is a monument where one white man found another white man and said a sentence about it. Everybody on earth knows that sentence except the people who live there. A man repaints it. The railway ends here. It was built to go further and it does not. In the afternoons men sit at the station the way men sit at a bedside. Papa Kabeya has the 1974 timetable entire — departures, arrivals, the meal service. He will give it to you if you stand still long enough.

Not a cake. A watermelon, with one candle pushed into the top of it, standing crooked because a watermelon is not built to hold a candle, which anybody could have told him.

*Baba, you could have got a cake. Even a cheap one.*

*Watermelons are healthier. More natural. More African. Cakes are a western thing.*

*Watermelons are sweet,* my mother said, *like you, Djamela* — in the voice she uses when she is helping him be right about something.

I want to be fair to him, because I have not always been. He is not a stupid man. He was handed a town that had been emptied out twice before he got to it, and he has nothing to give me except proverbs and fruit, and he decided that if he refused the world outside Kalemie hard enough I would stop wanting it. *Make a wish. Blow.*

*What is Kim Kardashian,* my father said.

*A social media influencer,* my mother said, fast, the way you move a knife off a table.

He carried stones in his pocket. He did not look for them at the shore like a man who had not planned to teach. He stood side-on and threw flat and it went four, five, six.

***Count them out loud.*** Patrice counted. It came out at two.

*Your wrist. Not the water. Never the water.*

Some evenings he put the phone face up on the sand and let a long one run into the sebene, and held up a hand at the exact place the thing turned, before it turned, every time. *You heard it after. I heard it before. That is not a gift. That is forty years.* Patrice claps on one and three. My father told him that is a Protestant's clap and can be unlearned, but not quickly.

Once, in one of those songs, a man's name went past in the middle of the shouting. Thrown in and gone in less than a second. My father said that one was paid for, and how much. The man has a depot at Kalundu. He wants a particular woman to hear his name in a song and understand what kind of man has his name in a song.

*They call it a stone,* he said. *You throw it in. Where it lands is not your business.*

Then he gave Patrice another stone out of his pocket and told him to count.

*Do not follow the loudest. The loudest is being paid.*

I was on the porch for this, nearer the house than the water. He had a listener and it was not me. I have never decided whether that was a thing taken from me or a thing I got out of.

Four hulls sit off the point. They went down in a season nobody wrote down. Under the middle of that water a line was let down in 1858 and found no bottom at three hundred fathoms. Nothing there has turned over since before the town had this name or the two before it. The men who wrote that down were carried the last part of the way.

Patrice has a wall in his room where he nails things he has found. A hubcap. The skull of an antelope. A bicycle wheel I am fairly sure he did not find. He calls it a collection and everybody else calls it rubbish.

When we were small I gave him a motherboard. I found it behind the internet café in a heap of things that had stopped working, and it had been rained on for two seasons, which is what had killed it, and neither of us knew that. It was green with little gold roads on it going nowhere. I gave it to him because he was the only person who would understand that a thing can be beautiful after it stops being useful.

He nailed it up between the skull and the hubcap and has never once mentioned where he got it. Patrice thinks he found everything.

His room has three buckets under the places where the roof has given up. The third stands on a board that is not nailed down, and the ground under that board is the only dry ground in the house.

We had got off the dala dala at the dagaa stop, and behind us Morisho was still shouting mia tano mia tano and getting smaller, and the boda boda men were doing what boda boda men do, which is call you beautiful in a way that is not a compliment but a notice.

It rained in April and I ran to his house because it was closer than mine.

He had physics papers across the mattress and he moved them for me without being asked.

*Do you understand entropy.*

*It is a natural state. Order needs energy. Disorder is what happens when you stop.*

*My father says the lake is the opposite. He says it puts things back in order, and that it remembers everything in its right place.*

*Your father is a wise man,* Patrice said. Then, a minute later: *it is hydrogen and oxygen, and it has no intelligence.*

The rain came through the roof and hit the buckets and the sound filled that room so completely there was nothing else in it. The note of the second bucket went lower as it filled. My knee was touching the edge of his knee. I had put it there.

*Then what do you want to remember, Mr Scientist. Encrypted data? Binary codes? Or a colourful moment with no meaning in it?*

He did not answer and he did not move. I watched a boy who wanted something more than he has wanted anything hold completely still, because moving might have cost him the version of me he kept in his head.

So I kissed him.

**It lasted about twenty seconds. I counted, because I count everything.**

*See? Not everything falls apart.*

That kiss cost me nothing at all. It was the cheapest thing I have ever given anybody and he has carried it around ever since like the down payment on a house, and I let him, and I had his side of it exactly, the whole time he was having it.

I say the car. There had been a car, and it came off the road at the corner near the market and did not stop, and my leg has a mark on it going yellow at the edges. That was true, and I want it known that one of the things I said that year was true.

The station smelled of dust and men. A bench along one wall with three of its slats replaced in a paler wood. A woman in a black dress explaining that it was not prostitution, it was escort services, they had cards with a company number on them. *More like your pimp's number,* the officer said. Everybody laughed except her and me.

The young one typed with one finger, hunting each letter down as though it owed him something. He typed in French, because that is what a form is. He said *mhh, what do we have here* in Swahili, because that is what a road is. And when the older one came over the two of them talked across me in Lingala, which is what they do when the thing being decided is not the thing being said.

Neither of them is from here. You can hear it in one sentence and everybody in that queue had heard it.

*She is reporting a hit and run,* Patrice said.

*So why are you talking. You sound like you know more than she does.* And he winked at Patrice, one man to another, over the top of me.

So I held out the phone with the photograph on it, because I had worked out on the way there that a hit and run was worth nothing and a man like that was worth something.

*We deal with hard evidence. Not AI. Not phantom ghosts.*

I leaned close enough to smell onion and whiskey on him, and backed off, and said it.

*My menstrual cycle. There is a latency of more than a month.*

The air in that room changed as though a door had opened somewhere.

The older one came over, big gut on him, the kind you get from a lifetime of small envelopes and large drinks, put his hand flat on the counter, and said that in this country there is Article 15.

He said it the way the priest at St Augustine says the parts he has said ten thousand times.

*Article 15. Débrouillez-vous.* He said every citizen of this republic has it and it is the only article that has never once been suspended. He said the constitution has been rewritten four times in his lifetime and Article 15 has survived all four, because it was never in any of them.

He said: it means the state has considered your situation.

Then he laughed, and looked at the young one, and the young one laughed, and Patrice laughed half a second late because he is fifteen and had understood that it was a joke before he understood what kind.

The young one kept typing. Then, quietly, and in Lingala, so that it would be for the room and not for the form: *where is his number. We will call him. We will summon him now. We will finish the case, if you understand me.*

I understood him perfectly, in a language nobody in my house speaks. I have had the measure of men like him since I was eleven, when my auntie's neighbour let his hand sit too long on the back of my chair while a room full of adults agreed together not to see it, the way you decide not to see a crack in a wall you are only renting.

Jean Luc took twenty minutes.

Twenty minutes means he was already in town, already near, already somewhere he could reach us from, which means that the whole time I had been walking around thinking I was choosing things, I had been the thing being watched.

He came in with his shoulders back and a fat envelope under his jacket. He did not look surprised and he did not look afraid. He looked like a man whose phone had finally rung.

When the older officer said the word minor, something went across his face and was gone, a flicker, like a bulb deciding whether to hold. He had planned for envelopes and not for consequences. Then it closed over and he smiled and it was as though it had never been there.

They took us to the room at the back.

Nothing happened to my body in that room. What happened is that three men worked out what I was worth, out loud, in two languages, in front of me, using the word compensation, and then agreed on it.

**I sat on a plastic chair with a crack in it and did the arithmetic along with them, because I cannot help it, I have never been able to help it. The number they landed on was smaller than the one I had been carrying in my head for eight months.**

It came in two parts. The second part was for a doctor in Uvira, and it was the smaller one, and they said it in the same voice they said the first.

On the way out the older one told me I should be grateful, because in some countries this would have taken years.

I came out of that room bent over. Patrice was there when I came out, and I can tell you what he thought, because he is fifteen and good and he thinks shame is the only thing that can bend a person.

It was not shame. It was arithmetic.

The envelope had been fat going in and it was thin coming out and nobody said anything about that and nobody had to.

# ⊹ WHAT THE CONTROLLED TEST FOUND

**Design.** One variable. 3g is [[mihogoni-3f]] with the grammatical person changed and nothing else. No scene moved, no image improved, no fault repaired — including the faults I named in the blind read, which are all still here on purpose.

**Measured, both files, same method** — everything from the opening epigraph to the last line of prose, appendices excluded: **3f = 4,482 words · 3g = 4,483.** A one-word difference across a whole story. The flip is length-neutral, which is what a controlled test should look like and is the evidence that nothing was quietly rewritten. *(3f's own note says 4,471; that is a different cut of the same file, not a discrepancy in the prose.)*

## Held — five of seven

**1 · The four ship epigraphs. Held completely.** They are extra-diegetic — they belong to the document, not to the narrator — so person cannot touch them. Djamela does not have to know about the Kaiserliche Werft for the sequence to sit above her sections, exactly as the tea recipes sit above *Dite*'s. **This was the hypothesis's main claim and it is confirmed.**

**2 · The after-deck clause. Held, and got stronger.** *Eingeborene → les indigènes → deck passengers to provide their own covering → staha.* Four flags, one deck, the category quietly turning from a race into a fare. Then the girl is on that deck. In third person the reader watched that land on her. In first person **she rides the deck and reports it without connecting it to the papers above her head** — which is the naive-narrator engine running on the story's best material. This is the one device the flip actively improved.

**3 · Article 15. Held.** *The constitution has been rewritten four times in his lifetime and Article 15 has survived all four, because it was never in any of them.* It is spoken aloud by a character, so person is irrelevant to it. Patrice's half-second late laugh survives untouched.

**4 · The surplus paragraph. Held.** The Lukuga bridge, the suitcase of left shoes, the tailor's photograph, *none of that has anything to do with any of this* — first person makes it marginally better, because a girl who counts everything is exactly the kind of narrator who keeps an inventory of irrelevant true things.

**5 · *Shemeji* as the last word. Held.** Placement does the work, not person. *He did it in front of everybody on that dala dala* still delivers **public** and **kinship** without a gloss.

## Broke — two of seven

**6 · The language stratification broke, and the flip is what exposed it.**

In third person, *French is what things are written in… Lingala comes from Kinshasa… it is spoken over the top of you rather than to you* reads as a narrating intelligence describing a town. Ported unchanged into first person it becomes **a thirteen-year-old delivering four paragraphs of sociolinguistics about her own home**, and there is nowhere for it to hide. It is the same prose. Only the speaker changed, and the speaker cannot carry it.

I flagged this passage in the blind read as the story explaining its own world. **The test upgrades that from a stylistic complaint to a structural fault**: it was never doing the work, it was being covered by the third person. Everything in it is shown later and better — the police station does French, Swahili and Lingala in one paragraph of action, and the deck does Swahili and English in one sentence. **The passage is redundant, and in first person it is also implausible.** It would have to go, or shrink to the single line about Lingala arriving by being aimed at people.

**7 · The father's teaching scene broke at the *stone*, in a way I did not predict.**

*They call it a stone. You throw it in. Where it lands is not your business* is spoken by Radjabu, so the line itself survives. What does not survive is **who is listening**. In third person the narration can sit at the water with Patrice and record the lesson at full length. In first person Djamela is on the porch, *nearer the house than the water* — she is out of earshot of a quiet man teaching a boy, and the scene is reported in a detail she could not have.

The flip therefore turns *he had a listener and it was not me* from a good line into **an admission that the preceding six paragraphs are impossible.** Either the scene moves closer and loses the exclusion, or it keeps the exclusion and shrinks to what carries across sand. Third person was paying for that scene without the bill being visible.

## The finding

**Five of seven devices were free of person. Two were being subsidised by it.**

Both failures are the same failure: **third person was buying access the narrator does not have** — to a lecture she would not deliver, and to a conversation she could not hear. Neither cost was visible until the person changed, because close third does not itemise what it is paying for.

That is the useful result, and it points the other way from the obvious one. The question was *what does close third buy?* The answer is **not atmosphere and not authority — reach.** And reach is the thing a first-person story about a girl who is systematically kept out of rooms should probably not have.
