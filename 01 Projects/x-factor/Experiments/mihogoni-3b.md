# THE LAST INCH
### Mihogoni cycle · #3, second pass

> [!danger] AI DRAFT · NOT CANON · NOT THE USER'S WORK

> [!abstract] What changed from the first pass
> [[mihogoni-3]] announced itself: *we are the lake, and we are also in the fruit.* This one never does.
>
> **Close third on Djamela, narrator unattributed.** No first person anywhere. Some sentences are hers in free indirect discourse — her idiom, her arithmetic. Some are perceptions no person could have: the inside of a watermelon before it was bought, the temperature of her blood to half a degree, a wash that happens while she is out of the house. **The reader cannot assign the voice, and it is never resolved.**
>
> **The substance is declared the way *Dite* declares colonialism** — by four flat instruction blocks in four registers, never commented on: a mother's household instruction → a state health poster → a hotel laundry card → a tourism brochure. The registers climb away from her and toward money. Nobody says the word class.
>
> Motifs carried over: **the dry board under the third bucket**, and **what will not come out of cloth.**

Nobody tells you the exact day your body stops being only yours. Hers picked a Tuesday.

She was behind the school gate counting on her fingers. Thirty-four days. She counted twice and got the smaller number first and wanted that one to be true, so she counted again and it got bigger, which is not how counting is supposed to work.

The inside of a head is a dry place and nothing of that arithmetic reached the air. What reached the air was this: she stood still with her hands moving, her fingers had gone cold at the tips, and the back of her neck was giving her up faster than the hour called for. She had drunk about a cup at the house that morning, boiled the night before and stood overnight in a jug with a saucer on it.

By that Tuesday there had been a second small closed warmth in her for some weeks. It had its own temperature, about half a degree over hers, which is ordinary and happens to all of them, and it did not mix with the rest of her.

Alicia took her arm as she does and she let her, and they went in past the notice board with last term's list still pinned to it.

---

Mihogoni is the down part of town, where the road stops being a road and becomes a suggestion.

The division that runs through that town is plain and nobody there has ever said it out loud. There is a part of it that is reached through a pipe and a part of it that is reached in a bucket on a head. In the piped part it arrives under pressure and comes out of a tap in a room with a door. In the carried part it is lifted at a standpipe and goes up the road in a plastic bucket with a lid that does not fit, and spills at every third step, and leaves a line on the road for as long as the sun takes.

When the délestage comes down on the carried side the pump for that side stops. The pipes on the other side stay full. The queue at the standpipe gets longer and quieter, and both things are true at once, at different pressures, and the difference has never once been counted by anybody who could do anything about it.

Djamela has stood on the road at night looking at the yellow windows of the children who go to Monseigneur André Kaseba. She stood a long time for somebody not doing anything. Her face had the thin evening film on it that everyone has, and it did not change temperature the whole time she stood there.

Her father sells fruit on Ave de Tanganyique in the mornings and lays his hands on people in the afternoons, and both of these are jobs.

There is a gutter that came off the side of the house the year before last and is still lying in the yard where it landed. It is still shaped the way it was shaped. In April it fills, and runs its whole length, and comes out the far end into the dirt, because shape is most of what decides where a thing goes. The other thing is downhill.

Morisho hangs off the side of the dala dala and shouts mia tano mia tano every morning until his voice stops working. It stops working because he has gone dry at the throat and will not stop to drink, and shouting is a small amount of a man leaving him, and the alternative to shouting is not being paid. He gave her a coin off her fare once and said a word close to her face. Said that close, into no wind, the word went nowhere at all.

---

She turned thirteen in March and her father bought a watermelon.

It had been in the ground under a field two districts over, and it came up a stem in the dark, and it sat inside its own rind getting sweet, which is a thing that happens and about which nothing has a view. A man carried it eleven kilometres on a bicycle and stood it in a pyramid with its brothers, and one of them went home under his arm.

Baba, she said, you could have got a cake. Even a cheap one.

Watermelons are healthier, he said. More natural. More African. Cakes are a western thing.

Watermelons are sweet, her mother said, like you, Djamela, in the voice she uses when she is helping him be right about something.

He pushed the candle in about a thumb's depth. The wax went in cold and the rind closed round it. She wanted to be fair to him and had not always been. He is not a stupid man. He was handed a town that had been emptied out twice before he got to it, and he has nothing to give her but proverbs and fruit, and he decided that if he refused the world outside Kalemie hard enough she would stop wanting it. It only made the wanting private.

Make a wish, he said. Blow.

To be Kim Kardashian, amen.

What is Kim Kardashian, her father said.

A social media influencer, her mother said, fast, the way you move a knife off a table.

The candle went out and the smoke came up. It rose to the iron roof and hit it and spread out flat and stayed, because iron in the evening is cooler than the room, and everything goes where it is cooler, and there was nowhere else in that house for it to be. It hung under the roof for about an hour after they had stopped looking at it.

Later she sat on the porch with Daudi and the lake in front of them and the four rusted ships in it that have not moved in her lifetime. He thinks I want things, she said.

Daudi said nothing, which is what Daudi does.

I do want things, she said. I want to control them. I want to conquer like the Europeans did, except now you do not need weapons or degrees. You only need followers.

She was thirteen years and one hour old and she believed all of it, and nobody had made her believe it, and she had worked it out herself from the evidence in front of her.

Three of the four hulls are going over and one is upright and the mud is taking all four at the same rate. The one furthest out has something in her hold that came in a long time ago and has not been out since. It is colder than the lake around it. It moves once a year, slowly, in a circle, and the fish do not go in.

Below a certain depth out there nothing turns over at all. The top three hundred feet go up and come down with the year. What is under that has not been at the top since before the town had its name, and nothing lives in it, and it is not dark down there so much as still.

Daudi's room is one room with a roof that has given up in four places.

Three of the four have buckets under them and the fourth is over the door where it does not matter. The buckets are plastic and were different colours once and are known by the note they make. The first is nearly empty most of the time and rings. The second is squat and takes the heaviest of the four and does not ring at all.

The third stands on a board that is not nailed down.

Nothing has ever been under that board. The board sits flat, the earth under it is packed, it is above the run of the yard so nothing gets in from the side, and the bucket on top takes whatever comes down. Everything else in that room has been got into — the mattress, the physics papers, the mortar between the bricks, the boy, the girl, many times, for years. The space under the board is the one dry place in that house.

He has a wall in there with things nailed to it. A hubcap. The skull of an antelope. A bicycle wheel she is fairly sure he did not find. He calls it a collection and everybody else calls it rubbish.

His mother died having him, which is a thing that gets said about him rather than by him, and he has built a whole woman out of that absence, patient, soft, never once disappointed in him, because a woman who never existed does not have to be. He has never said one word of it to her and she has it anyway, entire, the shape of a room nobody has ever put a light on.

Between the skull and the hubcap there is a green board with gold roads on it going nowhere.

It lay in a heap behind the internet café by the market for two seasons and it rained on that heap both of them. That is all it takes. A very small amount in the wrong places and a thing like that stops working and does not start again. She picked it out of the heap because she thought it was the most beautiful broken thing in it, and she gave it to him because he was the only person who would understand that a thing can be beautiful after it stops being useful. He nailed it up and has never once mentioned where he got it. Daudi thinks he found everything.

---

It rained hard in April and she ran to his house because it was closer than her own.

She came in with it running out of her hair onto the floor, and it was coming through the roof into the three buckets, and the noise filled that room so completely there was nothing else in it. The note of the second bucket went lower as it filled. That is the only clock the room has.

He moved the physics papers for her without being asked. Her knee was against the edge of his knee. She had put it there, and there was a film between the two knees before the knees touched.

Do you understand entropy, she said.

She had heard him say it eleven times. She asked because she wanted him to be the person who knew something, for once, in front of her.

It is a natural state, he said. Order needs energy. Disorder is what happens when you stop.

My father says the lake is the opposite, she said. He says it puts things back in order, and that it remembers everything in its right place.

Your father is a wise man, Daudi said. Then, a minute later: it is hydrogen and oxygen, and it has no intelligence.

Both of those sentences were said in a room where it was coming through the roof into three buckets, and it went on coming through at exactly the same rate through both of them.

Then she kissed him. It lasted about twenty seconds and she counted, because she counts everything. Both surface temperatures went up. Both breathing stopped and started again. The film between the knees got wider.

See, she said. Not everything falls apart.

His face, when she pulled back, was the face of somebody shown the back door of the universe. That kiss cost her nothing at all. It was the cheapest thing she has ever given anybody and he has carried it ever since like the down payment on a house, and she let him, and she had his side of it exactly, the whole time he was having it.

The third bucket filled twice that afternoon. The board under it stayed dry.

> *Guests' garments are collected daily before 10:00 and returned within 24 hours. The management cannot accept responsibility for delicate fabrics, or for stains which have been permitted to set.*

---

The car was a BMW, black, with windows you could not see into. It had been washed that morning in a yard on the other side of town, out of a hose, by a man who was paid to do it, and it was still beaded along the top of the door when it came down to the dagaa stop. When the window went down the beads ran into the mechanism, which is where they always end up on a car like that, and which is what will finish that car in eleven years.

She was standing in the road in the heat and it was coming off her at the temples and behind the knees.

Then a horn, different from the boda boda men's. The window came down with a sound she had never heard a window make, not the clink of a handle going round but a swish, like a word said quietly on purpose.

Je m'appelle Jean Luc, he said. Tu es d'où, ma belle.

She said: Rue Saint Michel.

She lied immediately and easily and without deciding to, and it came out as though it had been sitting behind her teeth for years, waiting.

Daudi started saying the thing about strangers. Mama alisema —

We shouldn't talk to strangers, said Jean Luc, finishing it for him. And he smiled.

She has thought about that smile more than about anything else. He knew what he was and he said it out loud, in a joke, in front of both of them, and he was so certain it would cost him nothing that he was right.

He told them he was a director. Consul général de Belgique. He said it in French with Lingala underneath and his French was worse than he thought it was, and she noticed, and she decided not to have noticed. That is the part she would like written down somewhere. Not that she was fooled. That she did the deciding.

Things that go downhill go downhill. Things go up when it is hot and come down somewhere else and take whatever shape is offered them, and that is the whole of their account of themselves, and it is a different kind of account.

He wrote a number on a piece of paper and put a ten thousand note under it. Paper is easily ruined so she was careful with it, and it came off her palm into the corner of the note anyway and made it soft there over the week under the mattress.

Je m'appelle, he asked.

Chantal, she said.

She said it out clear, into a road, over the noise of the boda boda men, the way you say a thing you want carried. It went sixty metres across hot air and out over the water and it is still out there. It is one of two names out of that whole year that ever got that far, and it is not the name she is called in the house.

Nobody had asked her for a name before. They had only ever used the one she came with. He handed her that name like a gift and she took it like one, and it was a collar with a good buckle.

Daudi stood beside her and did not say one word after mama alisema. She did not turn her head. His surface temperature went up and stayed up long after the car had gone, which is what happens to a person who has been running, and he had not been running.

She was thirteen. She is not asking to be forgiven for that. She is asking for it to be counted correctly.

---

There is not much to be had about the hotel.

There is a tap on the third floor that comes out hard for the first three seconds and then evenly. It was run twice each time, once short and once long. There is a drain in that floor that goes down slowly because of what is in it, and what is in it is hair and soap.

And there is a thing that goes into cloth there and does not come out.

There is a short list of those and nothing has ever been added to it or taken off it. Everything else comes out — dirt, blood, fish, smoke, sweat, the mango a child has been holding. This one is an oil and it is made to sit down in the fibre and stay, and soap gets everything else and lifts around it and leaves it, and it will go, but only by air, and only by time. Four days. Five.

Shalis by Remy Marquis. Not hers.

She learned the four days. The clothes started going to the bottom of the basket instead of the top and staying there, and the basket sits in the corner where the wall is always a little cool, and the order of that pile changed and stayed changed.

Her mother does the wash on Thursdays and has done since before she could count.

The rest of it is the inventory, because a list is the only thing she has ever been able to hold the world in. A key fob shaped like a lion, cartoon, plastic, the kind you would give a child; it opens a door on the third floor and it lives in her left pocket where the seam has gone, never the right. Fingernails, done every Friday and taken off every Monday before school. A photograph on her phone with a hand at the edge of the frame and a gold ring on it that is not gold, tagged blessed, liked by forty-one people, none of whom asked her anything. Two vaccinations, low on the arm, near the elbow, not where they usually put them.

Anybody who wanted to could have read that. It was all on the outside of her for months.

> *Lake Tanganyika: the second deepest freshwater lake on earth, and the longest. Sunsets from the terrace are widely held to be the finest in the region. Guests wishing to swim are advised to do so before 17:00. Kindly hang towels you are willing to reuse.*

The station is a dry building. The tap in the yard has not run in a while and there is dust on everything, and dust is what there is where nothing gets in.

A bench along one wall with three of its slats replaced in paler wood. A woman in a black dress explaining that it was not prostitution, it was escort services, they had cards with a company number on them. More like your pimp's number, the officer said, and everybody laughed except her and Djamela.

The young one typed with one finger, hunting each letter down as though it owed him something. He raised his face and made a sound in his throat. Mhh. What do we have here.

She is reporting a hit and run, Daudi said.

So why are you talking, the officer said. You sound like you know more than she does. And he winked at Daudi, one man to another, over the top of her.

She held out the phone with the photograph on it, because she had worked out on the way there that a hit and run was worth nothing and a man like that was worth something.

We deal with hard evidence, he said. Not AI. Not phantom ghosts.

She leaned close enough to smell onion and whiskey on him — both of those come dissolved, and both carry — and backed off, and said it.

My menstrual cycle. There is a latency of more than a month.

Four men stopped moving at once. The air in a room where four men have stopped moving at once goes damp very fast.

The older one came over and began reciting. The penal code. A teenage minor. Thirty years and twelve lashes on top. A buccal swab will suffice as conclusive evidence. Shame on the predator, for luring young girls. He said it in the voice the priest at St Augustine uses for the parts he has said ten thousand times.

Then the young one, quietly: where is his number. We will call him. We will summon him now. Yes. We will finish the case, if you understand me.

She understood him perfectly. She has had the measure of men like him since she was eleven, when her auntie's neighbour let his hand sit too long on the back of her chair while a room full of adults agreed together not to see it.

Jean Luc took twenty minutes. Twenty minutes means he was already in town, already near, already somewhere he could reach them from, which means that the whole time she had been walking around thinking she was choosing things, she had been the thing being watched.

They took them to the room at the back. Nothing happened to her body in that room. What happened is that three men worked out what she was worth, out loud, in two languages, in front of her, using the word compensation, and then agreed on it. She sat on a plastic chair with a crack in it and did the arithmetic along with them, because she cannot help it and never has been able to, and the number they landed on was smaller than the one she had been carrying for eight months. It came in two parts. The second part was for a doctor in Uvira, and it was the smaller one, and they said it in the same voice.

She came out of that room bent over. Daudi was there and she can tell you what he thought, because he is fifteen and good and he thinks shame is the only thing that can bend a person.

It was not shame. It was arithmetic.

Her surface temperature had dropped by the time she came out, which happens after a fright and also happens after sitting still in the shade, and there is no telling those two apart from the outside.

The lake was on her left the whole way home, with the four ships in it. It was flat that evening. It is flat most evenings in that season.

---

The house was empty and her clothes were folded on the end of the bed. All of them, including the two from the bottom of the basket that still had four days to go.

They had been washed twice. Once with soap, in the tub in the yard, in the ordinary way. Then again, after, with something sharp and thin in it, the vinegar or the paraffin, and by then it had already carried the thing off and let it go and there was no telling which.

It had not come out in four months. Somebody put the right thing in on a Tuesday afternoon and it came out in one go and went down into the yard and into the ground and, in about nine months, back into the lake, in a quantity too small to find.

Somebody in that house had worked out what was in that cloth, and got what was needed, and used it, and said nothing.

She put them in the drawer. Then she stood in the doorway of the kitchen for a while and her mother was not in it, and the kettle in there had gone cold about two hours before, and she went to bed.

---

In the third month the second small closed warmth was not in her any more.

There had been a journey in it — upright and moving for a long time, and very little drunk, which shows. After that it was not there, and her own weight came back over about a week, and the half degree went out of her.

Things leave all the time. Off the whole surface of that lake more goes up into the air every day than goes out by the river, and the river is the only way out there is. Almost nothing leaves by the river. Almost all of it leaves by going up, and comes down somewhere, and is back inside a week or a hundred years.

Those two facts are put next to each other because they happened in the same season. There is no other reason.

---

School on the Monday. She took the nails off on the Sunday night, the same as always. Alicia asked in the corridor whether it was true about the man with the car. She said what man. Alicia said all right, and took her arm as she does, and has not asked her anything since.

Somebody will be taken for this. She has known that since the twenty minutes. It will not be him — him they will call as a witness, if they call him at all. They will take somebody visible and poor who cannot afford an envelope and the whole town will agree to be satisfied, and she thinks it will be Morisho, because he is the one who shouts mia tano mia tano on the road every morning where everybody can see him. He gave her a coin off her fare once and called her shemeji. That is the whole of what he did.

She went to her locker at the end of the day when the corridor was empty. There was a good coat of dust on the inside of the door and she wrote his name in it with one finger.

Daudi.

Everybody's finger has enough on it to darken a line in dust. That is the only reason the letters were legible at all.

It is a short name, and it does not take long to write and it does not take long to look at. Then she wiped it off with the side of her hand, and the dust and the letters came away onto the heel of her palm and sat there damp for about ninety seconds and then dried.

That is the second name out of that year that got anywhere. The first one went sixty metres across a road in a shout and is still out there.

---

Her father was on Ave de Tanganyique with the fruit when she came past, with the watermelons stacked in a pyramid, which takes him twenty minutes and which nobody has ever bought a watermelon because of. She did not cross over.

She stopped at Daudi's house and he was out, and his father was in, and his father does not look at her, which suited her.

It was the dry season and nothing was coming through the roof. The three buckets stood empty with a white ring dried into the bottom of each, which is the mineral part, the part that stays when the rest goes.

She took the green board down off its nail. Then she knelt at the third bucket and moved it and lifted the board, and put the fob into the space under it — the lion, cartoon, plastic, the kind you would give a child — and put the board back, and stood on it with her weight, and it did not creak.

Nothing has ever been under there. Nothing gets in from the side and the bucket takes what comes down, and whatever is in there now will keep, in the one dry place in that house, for as long as the house does.

Then she hung the motherboard on its nail, straight, as he likes it, between the skull and the hubcap. He will find it. Not soon. But he is a boy who takes things apart, and one day he will be in that room with nothing to do.

She walked home along the shore counting the ships. It came up the sand about four inches beside her and went back, eleven times a minute in that weather, the whole way.

There are four of them and there have always been four and they have not moved in her lifetime and they will not move in his. It came out even, the way it does.

Her father says the lake puts things back in order and remembers everything in its right place. He says it standing in front of it with his hands out, and men on that shore were saying it before he was born.

Everything that has ever gone in is still in. The gutter run-off, the soap, the sharp thing from the second wash, the rain off the iron roof, the ninety seconds of a name off a palm. None of it has left. Some of it went up and came down and is back. Some of it is at the bottom where nothing has turned over since before the town.

And none of it is where it was put.

# ⊹ WHAT THE SECOND PASS FOUND

**1 · Concealing the narrator is strictly better, and the reason is mechanical.**
[[mihogoni-3]] spends its whole first section establishing what the narrator is and what it can perceive — *we have no eyes, what we have is weight* — which is 400 words of housekeeping and, worse, a promise the reader then audits. Here the apparatus is never declared, so it is never checked. **The perceptions do the declaring**: half a degree, the note of the second bucket, the beads running into the door mechanism, the white ring in the dry bucket. By the third or fourth of those the reader has worked out what is speaking, and the story never confirms it. **A reader who has assembled a narrator will not put the story down to argue with it.**

**2 · The unassignable voice is the actual find, and free indirect discourse is what makes it possible.**
*"She is not asking to be forgiven for that. She is asking for it to be counted correctly."* — Djamela, transposed. *"The rind closed round it."* — not Djamela, and not anyone. They sit in the same paragraph in the same register with no seam. **The reader cannot say where one ends**, and the third-person pronoun is what allows the two to share a sentence; the first-person plural of the first pass made every hand-off visible. → [[Psychic Distance]]

**3 · The four epigraphs carry the class argument and the prose never touches it.**
Household instruction → state health poster → hotel laundry card → tourism brochure. The registers climb away from her and toward money, exactly as *Dite*'s four climb from family recipe to colonial brochure. **The fourth one advises guests to swim before 17:00 in the lake this story is narrated by.** No sentence anywhere says rich or poor. → [[Dite — Story Sheet]]

**4 · The title comes out of an epigraph and is unexplained.** *Pour off the top for drinking. Leave the last inch.* It is a boiling instruction, it is what the men in the back room did with the second smaller payment, and the story never once connects them. → [[Titles Good and Bad]]

**5 · The motifs held, and the absence-motif is stronger in concealment.**
The dry board under the third bucket, six placements: the room, the rain, the kiss, the dried rings, the hiding, the close. In the first pass the narrator says *we have never been under that board*, which is a narrator talking about itself. Here it is *nothing has ever been under that board* — the same fact, no self. **The blind spot stops being a confession and becomes a property of the house.**

**6 · The pregnancy survives the move and gets colder.**
*"A second small closed warmth… about half a degree over hers, which is ordinary and happens to all of them."* And the termination: *the second small closed warmth was not in her any more*, followed by a paragraph about evaporation and the Lukuga being the only way out, with *"those two facts are put next to each other because they happened in the same season. There is no other reason."* **The disclaimer is the thing that makes the reader join them.** → [[15 The Chair Test — Scoring the Hidden Factor|axis 3]]

**7 · Djamela's dialogue comes back, and that was the first pass's fatal cost.**
The water narrator got *"we had the sound of all of it and about half the shape of it"* and lost every exchange in Draft 3. Close third keeps the watermelon argument, *mama alisema*, *more like your pimp's number*, *we will finish the case, if you understand me* — so the Constitution's **dialogue reveals class** is intact while the perceptual layer stays inhuman. **This is the version that could survive contact with a judge.**

**8 · The freedom problem is now a two-sentence graft instead of a section.**
*That she did the deciding* is followed immediately by *things that go downhill go downhill… and that is a different kind of account.* Four lines, unattached, never referred to again. The first pass gave it a whole section break and it read as an essay.

**9 · Length. 4,879 words**, measured from the file, prose and epigraphs, findings excluded. **This went the wrong way and the prediction was wrong.** Dropping the narrator's 400 words of housekeeping was supposed to make it shorter than the first pass's 4,469; instead it is **410 longer**, because finding 7 costs more than finding 1 saves — **restoring the dialogue is expensive, and it was the right thing to restore.**

**10 · What is portable, and what is not.** Not the prose — none of this goes near the manuscript. The devices are: **an unattributed close third whose perceptions belong to something that is not the protagonist**; **instruction-as-epigraph in climbing registers**; and **an absence as the motif**.
