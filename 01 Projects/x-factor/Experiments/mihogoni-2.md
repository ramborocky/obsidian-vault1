# THE FOURTH
### Mihogoni cycle · #2 · the ships

> [!danger] AI DRAFT · NOT CANON · NOT THE USER'S WORK
> Written by Claude as a technique experiment. **Never submit, never merge into `01 Story Brain`, never present as the author's.** → [[12 The Rules — Source of Truth]]

> [!abstract] What is being tested
> The *Dite* move — a **first-person-plural object-chorus** with total access, no body and no agency — applied to the Mihogoni world on a new incident, so the voice can be heard without risking Chantal.
>
> **The rule held throughout: the indifference is in the world, never in the voice.** No sentence says the universe does not care. Four rusted ships report accurately and understand nothing, and the reader assembles the rest. That is the axis 3 engine — *the reader arrives before the narrator* — run for the length of a story instead of once.
>
> **Cycle payoff:** [[2026-07-28 — Chantal (Draft 3, Prize Pass)|Chantal]] closes on *there have always been four and they will not move in his lifetime.* This story ends on three. Only a reader of both gets it.

---

We are four, and we have been four for a long time.

The one nearest the shore went down at her mooring in a season nobody wrote down. She lies over to the left, and the water comes to her rail, and in the dry months her rail comes back out of the water with a white line on it. The second is behind her and deeper. The third is turned the other way, which happened slowly and not at once. The fourth is furthest out, where the bottom drops, and she is the only one of us still standing straight up, because the mud there took her evenly.

Rust is not fast. It is arithmetic. A plate gives up one flake at a time and the flake goes down into the silt and stays, and the plate is thinner by exactly that flake and by nothing else. We have been doing this sum for fifty years. Nobody has ever come out to check our working.

The water is warm at the top and cold underneath and the line between them moves up and down with the year. Small fish live in the third one's engine room where the light comes through a hole shaped like a hand. Cormorants stand on us in the mornings with their wings open, and they do not stand on the fourth, and we do not know why, and it has been that way since before the cormorants that stand on us now were hatched.

That is most of what happens.

---

From where we are we can see the shore and nothing behind it.

We can see the road where it comes down to the water and stops being a road. We can see the woman who sells charcoal, and the smoke off her fire goes sideways in the afternoons and straight up in the evenings, and we have watched the smoke go sideways and straight up for as long as she has been there, and for a long time before that, when it was somebody else's fire in the same place.

We can see the buses. There is a man who hangs off the side of one and shouts the same two words over and over until his voice stops working, and then the next morning it works again. The words carry across water better than most words do. We do not know what they mean. We have heard them ten thousand times.

We can see the school with the wall around it and we cannot see into it.

In the evenings a girl walks along the shore and counts us. She points with her chin, one two three four, and then she goes on walking. She has been doing it since she was small and she does it now that she is not. She always gets four. We do not know what she is checking for, and she has never looked as though the answer pleased her.

We can see when the lights go out on the down side of town and stay on further up. It happens most weeks. When it happens the water on our left goes black and the water on our right keeps a yellow shake in it, and the line between the two is very straight and lasts until morning.

We do not know why the line is straight. We only know that it is, and that it is straight in the same place every time.

---

The man came in a pirogue in the dry season, alone, and went round all four of us.

He did not fish. He put his hand flat on the first one's plate above the waterline and left it there. He knocked on it twice with his knuckle and listened. He did the same to the second and the third. He was slow about it and he did not talk to himself, which most of them do.

Then he went out to the fourth, and he was longer there than he had been at any of us, and he knocked in six places instead of two, and at the last one he stopped and put both hands on her and stayed like that while the pirogue turned itself around under him.

He came back the next week with a length of chain and measured her from the water to the rail, and wrote it on his hand.

The week after that he brought the boy.

---

The boy was small enough that the man lifted him out of the pirogue and onto the first one's rail with one arm.

He went over all of us that day, the boy. He got inside the third one through the hole shaped like a hand and made a noise in there that came out of four different places at once, and he did it again to hear it again. He found the cormorant mess and did not like it. He put his arm into the water up to the shoulder to get at a thing that was not there.

The man let him. The man sat on the rail with his legs over the side and watched the shore, and when the boy came near the edge where the plate has gone soft he said the boy's name without turning his head, and the boy came away from it.

That is how we learned the name. He shouted it across water so it would carry, and the water carried it, and now we have it. *Trésor.*

Names do not usually reach us. People say them close to each other's faces on the shore and the sound falls in the sand. To get a name out to us somebody has to be frightened enough to shout.

---

The cutting started on a Monday.

The man had a bottle on the pirogue with a hose off it and a torch at the end of the hose, and when he opened the valve the flame was blue and small and made a sound like a held breath.

We had not had a light like that. The sun comes at us from one side and then the other and then it goes. This came from inside. It went through the plate of the fourth one at the rail and made a bead of the plate and the bead ran down and fell in the water and went out, and there was a hole where it had been, and the hole had a bright edge that stayed bright for a moment and then did not.

Fifty years of arithmetic on one flake at a time, and the flame did a hand's width in the time it takes a cormorant to settle.

The boy held the hose out of the water. That was his work. He was good at it and he did it the whole day and his father did not have to say the name once.

They took the rail off in lengths and put the lengths in the pirogue until the pirogue sat low, and then they went in, and we could see the pirogue going in with the sun behind it, low in the water, the two of them and the metal.

The next morning the pirogue came out empty and they started again.

---

We should say what is in the fourth one's hold, because it is the part we know best and we have never had anything to do with it.

There is water in there. There has been water in there since the second time the town emptied out. It is colder than the water outside because the light does not get to it, and it does not move except very slowly, in a circle, when the lake outside changes its mind about the season.

There are shapes on the floor of the hold. There are a great many of them. They are long and they are not all the same length, and the small ones are together in one part and the longer ones are more spread out. They are covered in a soft grey that we have watched get thicker over the years, the way the silt gets thicker on us, at about the same speed, so we take it to be silt.

Some of the shapes have hard round parts near the ends. Some of them have cloth on them still, and the cloth has gone the colour of the water. There is a shoe. It is on its own, away from the others, near the ladder, and it is small, and it has been near the ladder for fifty years.

They do not move. We want to be accurate about that, because it is the only thing about them we can be accurate about. Nothing in the hold has moved since the water finished coming in. Not once. Not in fifty years, not when the lake is high, not when the lake is low, not when the earth shook in the year the third one turned.

The fish do not go in there either. We do not know why. It is the same lake.

---

Men came out to look, that first week.

They came in twos, in pirogues, and they did not bring tools. They came alongside and they talked to the man and the man kept working while they talked, so some of what they said went into the sound of the flame and did not reach us.

One of them said the word for permission. The man said a name that was not his own. The one who had come nodded at that name for a while.

One of them said, *that one*, and pointed at the fourth, and the man said, *that one*, and the one who had come did not say anything else for a long time and then said that the price of metal was good this year and went away.

One of them was old and did not speak at all. He came alongside and sat in his pirogue and looked at the fourth one from the waterline up to where the rail had been. He was there a long time. The man stopped the flame for him, which he had not done for the others, and the two of them were quiet together, and then the old one turned his pirogue with one hand and went in.

Nobody came out twice.

That is what we noticed and we have no use for it. A thing that happens once is the same to us as a thing that happens never. We have only ever been able to count what keeps happening.

---

The girl was on the shore that week too, in the evenings, counting.

One two three four. The fourth one was shorter by then, with her rail gone and a bite out of her side, but she was still there and the girl still got four, and went on walking.

We would have liked her to keep coming, and we say that without knowing what we mean by it. There was no difference to us between the evenings she came and the evenings she did not. The water was the same. But we noticed her the way we notice the cormorants, and we had noticed her a long time, and things we have noticed a long time are the closest we have to anything.

She stopped coming in the second week. We do not know why. People stop.

---

In the third week the man cut into the hull below where the rail had been, and worked down, and the hole in the side of the fourth one got taller.

The boy had learned more by then. He held the hose and he also held the piece that was coming away, so it would not drop, and when it came free he took the weight of it with both arms and his father took it off him and put it in the pirogue. He was too small for the weight and he took it anyway and his father let him, and each time the piece came away the boy looked at his father's face first and the metal second.

On the Thursday of that week they went in early because the lake got up.

On the Friday the man cut through into the hold.

---

We had better say this carefully.

He cut a line down, and then a line across at the bottom, and the plate hinged out at the bottom the way plate does, and the cold water came out of the hold and met the warm water outside, and the two of them made a shiver in the light that went along the fourth one's side and reached us and was gone.

The soft grey came out with it. Fifty years of it. It came out in a slow cloud and hung and spread and went down, and the water in front of the fourth one went dark and stayed dark, and behind the cloud the hold was open.

The man had the torch in his hand and the torch was still lit. He did not turn the valve. The flame went on making its sound.

He was looking into the hold.

The boy was behind him in the pirogue with the hose across his knees, and the boy said something, and the man did not answer, and the boy started to stand up.

Then the man moved. He put the torch down on the plate still lit, which he had not done once in three weeks, and he turned round and got hold of the boy with both hands and put him down in the bottom of the pirogue facing the shore, and he held him there. He did not shout the name. He held the back of the boy's head with one hand so that the boy's face was in his shirt, and he kept the boy like that.

He looked over the boy's head at the hold for a while longer.

Then he picked the torch up off the plate and turned the valve and the sound stopped, and he came down into the pirogue and started the outboard with the boy still against him, and they went in.

He left the hose and the bottle. He had never left them before. They were on the fourth one's plate all night and they were still there in the morning, and the cormorants stood on the bottle and did not stand on the fourth one, the same as always.

---

The lake was quiet that weekend. It often is.

The cloud of grey settled out of the water in about a day and a half, which is fast, and the water in front of the fourth one went clear again, and the hold stayed open with the plate hinged down at the bottom like a tongue.

Nothing came out of it. Nothing went in. The shapes on the floor of the hold did not move, and we had not expected them to, and we noted it because we note that every year.

The shoe is still near the ladder.

---

On the Monday nobody came.

On the Tuesday nobody came.

On the Thursday the pirogue came out at first light with the man in it, alone, and he came alongside the fourth one and picked up the hose and the bottle and checked the valve, and opened it, and the flame came back with its held-breath sound.

He worked all day. He worked to the left of the hold, away from it, and he did not look into it, and he cut faster than he had cut before, in longer runs, with less measuring.

He brought the boy again the following week.

The boy held the hose. When the boy looked at the open hold his father said his name — *Trésor* — flat, without turning his head, the way he said it at the soft plate on the first day, and the boy looked back at the hose.

We had the name because a man was frightened enough once to shout it over water. We heard it a great many times after that and never once shouted again.

---

They took the fourth one down through the dry season and into the rains.

Some weeks there were two other men with them and the pirogue was a bigger boat. The price of metal was good that year. It had not been good other years and then it was good, and that is the whole of the reason, as far as we could see, and we watched all of it and there was nothing else in it.

Rust took fifty years off her and did not finish. The flame took the rest in seven months.

By the end there was a piece of her standing out of the water about the height of a man, and then there was a piece about the height of a boy, and then in a week when the lake was high there was nothing standing out of the water at all, and the pirogue went over the place where she had been without slowing down.

The hold went last, because the hold was deepest, and the mud there took it back before the flame could. It is down there now with the plate open and the shapes on the floor of it, under the silt, in the cold water, in the dark.

They have not moved.

---

The lake is the same.

The warm water is at the top and the cold water is underneath and the line between them goes up and down with the year. The small fish are still in the third one's engine room where the light comes through the hole shaped like a hand. The cormorants stand on us in the mornings with their wings open, and now there is nowhere they are avoiding, and they have not spread out into the space, and they will not.

The woman still sells charcoal and the smoke still goes sideways in the afternoons. The man still hangs off the side of the bus and shouts the same two words. The lights on the down side of town still go out and stay out, and the line across the water is still straight, and it is still straight in the same place.

The boy came back once on his own, later, older, in a pirogue he handled badly. He came out to where the fourth one had been and cut the engine and sat there. There was nothing to see. The water there is the same water. He sat over it for a while and then he put his hand in, up to the shoulder, the way he had done when he was small, reaching for a thing that was not there.

Then he took his arm out and started the engine and went in.

---

We are three now.

We have gone over this. Three is what we are and four is what we were, and the difference between them is one, and one is a number we have had no use for in fifty years because nothing here has ever happened to just one of us.

We do the sum again in the mornings when the cormorants come.

The first one lies over to the left and her rail comes out white in the dry months. The second is behind her and deeper. The third is turned the other way.

There is a fourth place, and the water over it is the same temperature as the water beside it, and the light does the same thing on it that it does on everything else, and there is no mark on the lake at all.

The girl does not come to count.

---

# ⊹ WHAT THIS EXPERIMENT FOUND

**1 · The indifference stayed in the world and never got into the voice.**
No sentence says the universe does not care. What carries it: the flame doing in one afternoon what rust took fifty years to do; the price of metal being good that year *and that being the whole of the reason*; the lake going clear again in a day and a half. **Cosmic indifference delivered entirely by rusted metal and a commodity price.** If any line had articulated it, [[The Judging Rubric — Nine Axes|axis 7]] would cap at 3.

**2 · The naive-narrator engine ran the whole length, not once.**
The ships report the hold with total precision — lengths, the grey silt, hard round parts near the ends, cloth gone the colour of the water, a shoe near the ladder — and understand none of it. *"We take it to be silt."* **The reader gets there several pages before the narrator, and the narrator never arrives.** This is the *Mothers Not Appearing in Search* move at story scale and it is what Chantal cannot currently do, because Djamela understands everything. → [[15 The Chair Test — Scoring the Hidden Factor]]

**3 · Non-intervention became structural instead of moral.**
The ships do not fail to help. They have no apparatus for helping, or for wanting to. *"A thing that happens once is the same to us as a thing that happens never."* That is the posthumanist decentring done properly — the humans are peripheral to a narrator with its own concerns (silt, cormorants, the thermocline), rather than a wise chorus withholding comfort.

**4 · The father is the existential turn, and it is unstated.**
He covers his son's eyes, goes home, stays away Monday and Tuesday, and comes back on Thursday and cuts faster. **Nobody says he needs the money.** The world does not care, and the poor cannot afford to either, and the story never puts those two facts in the same sentence.

**5 · Two devices worth stealing back into the real work**
- **Names only reach the ships when somebody is frightened enough to shout.** A hard constraint on the narrator that generates meaning for free — and it pays off at the end, when the name is said flatly forever after.
- **The girl who counts.** One evening, unnamed, walks off. She is [[2026-07-28 — Chantal (Draft 3, Prize Pass)|Djamela]], and the ships never learn it. This is the Atelier's *"a person whose life the story does not follow"* — the axis 5 surplus detail Chantal is currently a **2** on.

**6 · The cycle payoff.** Chantal ends: *there have always been four and they have not moved in my lifetime and they will not move in his.* She is wrong, and she will never know she was wrong, and only a reader of both stories feels it. **No single story can buy that.**

**7 · Length.** **3,302 words**, measured from the file, not estimated. Comfortably inside the 2,000–5,000 gate with 1,698 of headroom — unlike [[chantal-3rd]], which came in at 4,961 with 39. The object-narrator is *cheaper* than free indirect discourse, because a narrator with no interiority cannot spend words on it. Re-measure in Word if it ever matters.
