# THREE HUNDRED FATHOMS
### Mihogoni cycle · #3, fourth pass — **the live one**

> [!abstract] The instruction, and where the boundary went instead
> [[mihogoni-3c]] fixed the padding by fencing the narrator to the shore — and lost the room, the buckets, the kiss and the fob to get there. **The author's ruling: go inland. Close third, into her mind, and the reader must not be able to tell whether a sentence is Chantal's or the narrator's.**
>
> **So the boundary is her.** Not the shore, not the substance. The narrator goes exactly where she goes and knows exactly what she attends to. That kills the padding at the source without giving up a single scene — there is no cup of boiled water because her attention was never on the kettle.
>
> **What tells you it is not only her: deep time.** Seven intrusions, none of which a thirteen-year-old could have — soundings at three hundred fathoms, a ship arriving in five thousand crates, water that has not turned over since before the town had a name. They arrive in the same register as her own thoughts, with no seam, and the story never explains who is holding them.

> *Stand side-on. Throw flat, not down — it will not take a stone that comes at it straight. Count the touches out loud; if you count in your head you will lie. If it goes under on the first one that is your wrist and not the water. And do not throw at the lake. Throw along it. A man who throws at a thing has already decided what it is.*

---

Nobody tells you the exact day your body stops being only yours. Hers picked a Tuesday.

She was behind the school gate counting on her fingers, the way you count coins when you already know they are not enough. Alicia was talking about somebody's hairdo and whether the girl had paid for it herself. Thirty-four days. She counted twice, and the first time she got thirty-three, and she wanted the smaller number to be the true one, so she counted again and it got bigger.

That is not how counting is supposed to work.

The bell went. Alicia took her arm as she does and she let her, and they went in past the notice board with last term's examination list still pinned to it, and she sat down at a desk with a wobble in it and opened a book about the digestive system of a cow. They did the digestive system of a cow for three weeks. There are four stomachs. She can still name all four and has never once needed to.

Everybody wants to start with the man. The man comes later. The man is not the beginning of anything except his own story.

---

They live in Mihogoni, the down part of town, where the road stops being a road and becomes a suggestion.

Her father sells fruit on Ave de Tanganyique in the mornings and lays his hands on people in the afternoons, and both of these are jobs, and neither is the kind you say out loud at school.

The boats come over from Kigoma with dagaa and secondhand words and they keep both, so half of what gets shouted on that road is Tanzanian and nobody there has ever thought to mention it.

There is a gutter that came off the side of the house the year before last and is still lying where it landed. The woman who sells charcoal on the corner is called Bahati, or that is what people call her, and she has never once heard anybody use it to her face.

In Primary Six Alicia told a whole row that their house smelled of fish, back when she was somebody else's friend and not hers. Everybody laughed, and she laughed too, because that is the fastest way to stop being the thing people are laughing at. Their house does smell of fish. That is not the part that stayed with her.

The town divides in one place and it is not a place anybody names. There is a part of it that gets water through a pipe and a part that carries it up the road in a bucket. When the délestage takes the carried side the pump stops and the queue at the standpipe gets longer and quieter, and the windows on the other side stay yellow. She has stood on the road at night and watched the ghost kids doing their homework in that light. When their dala dala stops beside one of those cars at the lights on Ave de Industrielle they look in and the children do not look out. Not because they are proud. Because we are not there.

Morisho hangs off the side of the dala dala and shouts mia tano mia tano until his voice goes. He told them once that those children are ghosts to them, that they will never see them or talk to them. He meant it as a warning and she heard it as a price list.

> *In the sebene the guitar does not stop, so do not listen for the stopping. Listen for the man who is not playing — he is the one who knows where it ends. Clap on two and four; on one and three you are clapping at the music instead of inside it. And when the shouting starts, do not follow the loudest. The loudest is being paid.*

---

In the evenings Radjabu took the boy from two houses down to the water and taught him things.

He carried stones in his pocket. He did not look for them at the shore like a man who had not planned to teach. He stood side-on and threw flat and it went four, five, six, and Daudi threw and it went under on the first touch.

*Your wrist,* said Radjabu. *Not the water. Never the water.*

Then he talked while the boy threw, which is how he did all of it. He said the lake is cold like some women. He said people come and they go and it stays the same, dark and deep, and it swallows things down. He said a man who understands that will not be surprised by anything in his life.

Some evenings he put his phone face up on the sand and let a long one run into the sebene, and held up a hand at the exact place the thing turned, before it turned, every time. *You heard it after. I heard it before. That is the whole difference and it is not a gift, it is forty years.* Daudi claps on one and three. Radjabu told him that is a Protestant's clap and can be unlearned but not quickly.

*Do not follow the loudest. The loudest is being paid.*

She was usually on the porch for this, nearer the house than the water, hearing it the way you hear a thing you have heard since before you could count. He had a listener and it was not her, and she has never decided whether that was something taken from her or something she got out of.

Four hulls sit off the point. They went down in a season nobody wrote down and the men who could have said are dead. Under the middle of that water nothing has turned over since before the town had this name or the two before it, and a line let down there in 1858 found no bottom at three hundred fathoms, and the men who wrote that down are gone as well.

---

She turned thirteen in March and her father bought a watermelon.

Not a cake. A watermelon, with one candle pushed into the top of it, standing crooked because a watermelon is not built to hold a candle, which anybody could have told him.

*Baba, you could have got a cake. Even a cheap one.*

*Watermelons are healthier. More natural. More African. Cakes are a western thing.*

*Watermelons are sweet,* her mother said, *like you, Djamela* — in the voice she uses when she is helping him be right about something.

She wants to be fair to him, because she has not always been. He is not a stupid man. He was handed a town that had been emptied out twice before he got to it, and he has nothing to give her except proverbs and fruit, and he decided that if he refused the world outside Kalemie hard enough she would stop wanting it. It did not work like that. It only made the wanting private.

*Make a wish. Blow.*

She closed her eyes and said: to be Kim Kardashian, amen.

*What is Kim Kardashian,* her father said.

*A social media influencer,* her mother said, fast, the way you move a knife off a table.

She blew the candle out. The smoke went up and sat under the iron roof and did not leave.

Later she sat on the porch with Daudi with the lake in front of them and the four rusted ships in it that have not moved in her lifetime.

*He thinks I want things.*

Daudi said nothing, which is what Daudi does.

*I do want things. I want to control them. I want to conquer like the Europeans did, except now you do not need weapons or degrees. You only need followers.*

She was thirteen years and one hour old and she believed all of it. Nobody made her believe it. She worked it out herself, from the evidence in front of her, which was a town where the people with cars had taken things and the people without cars had been taken from.

---

Daudi is a year and a half older and he has a wall in his room where he nails things he has found. A hubcap. The skull of an antelope. A bicycle wheel she is fairly sure he did not find. He calls it a collection and everybody else calls it rubbish.

His mother died having him, which is a thing that gets said about him rather than by him, and he has built a whole woman out of that absence, patient, soft, never once disappointed in him, because a woman who never existed does not have to be. He has never said one word of it to her and she has it anyway, entire, the shape of a room nobody has ever put a light on.

When they were small she gave him a motherboard.

She found it behind the internet café that used to be by the market, in a heap of things that had stopped working. It had been rained on for two seasons, which is what had killed it, and she did not know that and neither did he. It was green with little gold roads on it going nowhere and she thought it was the most beautiful broken thing in the heap. She gave it to him because he was the only person who would understand that a thing can be beautiful after it stops being useful.

He nailed it to the wall between the skull and the hubcap. He has never once mentioned where he got it, and she does not think he remembers it was her. Daudi thinks he found everything.

---

> *13 February. Reached the water at Ujiji after eight months, the last of it carried. Soundings taken at four points; no bottom found at three hundred fathoms. It is not laid down on any chart. The natives have no name for the whole of it, only for the parts they use. We are satisfied it is the source, or near it. Named this day.*

---

The car was a BMW, black, with windows you could not see into.

They had got off the dala dala at the dagaa stop, and behind them Morisho was still shouting mia tano mia tano and getting smaller, and the boda boda men were doing what boda boda men do, which is call you beautiful in a way that is not a compliment but a notice, a way of saying that everyone has agreed you are available for comment now.

Then a horn, different from theirs. The window came down with a sound she had never heard a window make, not the clink of a handle going round but a swish, like a word said quietly on purpose.

*Je m'appelle Jean Luc. Tu es d'où, ma belle.*

She wants this part told exactly, because everybody who has heard it since has decided they already know.

*Rue Saint Michel,* she said.

She lied immediately and easily and without deciding to. Rue Saint Michel is where the ghost children live and she has never set foot on it, and it came out of her mouth as though it had been sitting behind her teeth for years, waiting.

Daudi started saying the thing about strangers. *Mama alisema —*

*We shouldn't talk to strangers,* said Jean Luc, finishing it for him. And he smiled.

She has thought about that smile more than about anything else. He knew what he was, and he said it out loud, in a joke, in front of both of them, and he was so certain it would cost him nothing that he was right.

He told them he was a director. Consul général de Belgique. He said it in French with Lingala underneath, and his French was worse than he thought it was, and she noticed, and she decided not to have noticed.

That is the part she would like written down somewhere. Not that she was fooled. That she did the deciding.

He wrote a phone number on a piece of paper and put a ten thousand note under it. Her eyes went wide and Daudi has never asked her why. It was not the money, because ten thousand is a school skirt. It was that a man with a car like that had stopped it, in the road, in front of the boda boda men, for her.

*Je m'appelle,* he asked.

*Chantal,* she said.

Nobody had asked her for a name before. They had only ever used the one she came with. He handed her that name like a gift and she took it like one, and it was a collar with a good buckle.

Daudi stood beside her the whole time and did not say one word after mama alisema. She did not turn her head. The hurt came off him like heat off a wall and she stood in it and warmed her hands, because a girl who has just been chosen wants somebody there to see her be chosen.

She was thirteen. She is not asking to be forgiven for that. She is asking for it to be counted correctly.

She went home and put the ten thousand under the mattress on her side. Her mother does the wash on Thursdays and has done since before she could count, so she had until Thursday to decide where it was going to live.

---

It rained in April, hard, as it does then, and she ran to Daudi's house because it was closer than her own.

His room is one room. Baked brick, a window the size of two hands, a foam mattress on the floor with a grey sheet, and buckets set out under the places where the roof has given up. There are three of them and they are known by the note they make. The first is nearly empty most of the time and rings. The second takes the heaviest of the leaks and does not ring at all. The third stands on a board that is not nailed down, and the earth under that board is the only dry ground in the house, and has been for as long as the house has stood.

He had physics papers spread across the mattress and he moved them for her without being asked.

*Do you understand entropy,* she said.

She had heard him say it eleven times. She asked because she wanted him to be the person who knew something, for once, in front of her.

*It is a natural state. Order needs energy. Disorder is what happens when you stop.*

*My father says the lake is the opposite. He says it puts things back in order, and that it remembers everything in its right place.*

*Your father is a wise man,* Daudi said. Then, a minute later: *it is hydrogen and oxygen, and it has no intelligence.*

They sat there and the rain came through the roof and hit the buckets and the sound filled the room so completely that there was nothing else in it. The note of the second bucket went lower as it filled. Her knee was touching the edge of his knee. She had put it there.

*Then what do you want to remember, Mr Scientist. Encrypted data? Binary codes? Or a colourful moment with no meaning in it?*

He did not answer and he did not move. She watched a boy who wanted something more than he has wanted anything hold completely still, because moving might have cost him the version of her he kept in his head.

So she kissed him.

It lasted about twenty seconds. She counted, because she counts everything.

Then she pulled back and said, *see? Not everything falls apart.*

His face, when she pulled back, was the face of somebody shown the back door of the universe.

That kiss cost her nothing at all. It was the cheapest thing she has ever given anybody and he has carried it around ever since like the down payment on a house, and she let him, and she had his side of it exactly, the whole time he was having it.

The third bucket filled twice that afternoon and the board under it stayed dry.

> *The line terminates at the lake. Beyond the terminus traffic proceeds by steamer. The vessel is delivered in sections by rail — five thousand pieces — and assembled upon the shore. She is armed. Her draught permits her to make any port upon either coast. Journey time is not guaranteed in the season of storms.*

---

She is not going to describe the hotel.

Here is what she has instead, which is the inventory, because a list is the only thing she has ever been able to hold the world in.

A key fob shaped like a lion, cartoon, plastic, the kind you would give a child. It opens a door on the third floor. She keeps it in her left pocket where the seam has gone and never in her right.

A perfume. Shalis by Remy Marquis. Not hers. It gets into the weave of a khanga and will not come out with soap, only with time, so she learned to leave certain clothes at the bottom of the basket for four days before her mother does the wash.

Fingernails, done every Friday and taken off every Monday before school, because at school there are rules and at the hotel there are different rules, and she lives between the two of them like somebody crossing a road that never stops.

A photograph on her phone. Her, and at the edge of the frame a hand on her shoulder with a gold ring on it. The ring is not real gold and he wears it like a crown. She tagged it blessed and forty-one people liked it and none of them asked her anything.

Two vaccinations, low on the arm, near the elbow, not where they usually put them.

Anybody who wanted to could have read that. It was all on the outside of her for months.

---

They went to Ave de Bobozo about the car.

She says the car. There had been a car, and it came off the road at the corner near the market and did not stop, and her leg has a mark on it going yellow at the edges. That was true, and she wants it known that one of the things she said that year was true.

Daudi came because she did not want to go alone. He said it was because his bicycle was stolen once, so he knew the forms.

The station smelled of dust and men. There was a bench along one wall with three of its slats replaced in a paler wood, and a woman in a black dress was explaining to an officer that it was not prostitution, it was escort services, they had cards with a company number on them. He said, *more like your pimp's number.* Everybody laughed except her and Djamela.

The officer who took them was young and typing with one finger, hunting each letter down as though it owed him something. He raised his face and made a sound in his throat. *Mhh. What do we have here.*

*She is reporting a hit and run,* Daudi said.

*So why are you talking. You sound like you know more than she does.* And he winked at Daudi, one man to another, over the top of her.

He asked where and what type of car and who was driving, typing at the speed of a man who has already decided what this is worth. So she held out her phone and showed him the photograph with the hand and the ring, because she had worked out on the way there that a hit and run was worth nothing and a man like that was worth something.

*We deal with hard evidence. Not AI. Not phantom ghosts.*

She leaned close enough to smell onion and whiskey on him, and backed off, and said it.

*My menstrual cycle. There is a latency of more than a month.*

The air in that room changed as though a door had opened somewhere.

The older one came over, big gut on him, the kind you get from a lifetime of small envelopes and large drinks, and he started reciting. The penal code. A teenage minor. Rape. Thirty years and twelve lashes on top. He said it in the voice the priest at St Augustine uses for the parts he has said ten thousand times. *A buccal swab will suffice as conclusive evidence. Shame on the predator, for luring young girls.*

The young one kept typing. Then, quietly: *where is his number. We will call him. We will summon him now. Yes. We will finish the case, if you understand me.*

She understood him perfectly. She has had the measure of men like him since she was eleven, when her auntie's neighbour let his hand sit too long on the back of her chair while a room full of adults agreed together not to see it, the way you decide not to see a crack in a wall you are only renting.

She gave him the phone and dialled the number herself. Jean Luc took twenty minutes.

Twenty minutes means he was already in town, already near, already somewhere he could reach them from, which means that the whole time she had been walking around thinking she was choosing things, she had been the thing being watched.

He came in with his shoulders back and a fat envelope under his jacket and he did not look surprised and he did not look afraid. He looked like a man whose phone had finally rung.

When the older officer said the word minor, something went across his face and was gone, a flicker, like a bulb deciding whether to hold. He had planned for envelopes and not for consequences. Then it closed over and he smiled and it was as though it had never been there.

They took them to the room at the back.

Nothing happened to her body in that room. What happened is that three men worked out what she was worth, out loud, in two languages, in front of her, using the word compensation, and then agreed on it.

She sat on a plastic chair with a crack in it and did the arithmetic along with them, because she cannot help it, she has never been able to help it. And the number they landed on was smaller than the one she had been carrying in her head for eight months.

It came in two parts. The second part was for a doctor in Uvira, and it was the smaller one, and they said it in the same voice they said the first.

She came out of that room bent over. Daudi was there when she came out, and she can tell you what he thought, because he is fifteen and good and he thinks shame is the only thing that can bend a person.

It was not shame. It was arithmetic.

They gave her the envelope on the way out. It had been fat going in, and it was thin coming out, and nobody said anything about that, and nobody had to.

The lake was on her left the whole way home, with the four ships in it.

---

The house was empty when she got home. Her clothes were folded on the end of the bed, all of them, including the two from the bottom of the basket that still had four days to go. Somebody had washed them with soap and then with something after the soap, vinegar, or paraffin, whatever it is you use when soap will not do it.

Somebody had worked out what was in that cloth, and gone up the road for the right thing, and come back, and used it, and said nothing.

She put them in the drawer. Then she stood in the doorway of the kitchen for a while, and her mother was not in it, and she went to bed.

> *MV LIEMBA · Kigoma – Kalemie – Uvira – Mpulungu.*
> *FIRST CLASS, cabin, two berths. SECOND CLASS, cabin, four berths. THIRD CLASS, deck.*
> *Deck passengers should provide their own covering. Livestock by weight. Children under three carried free.*
> *The vessel does not wait. Fares payable before boarding, never after.*

---

She crossed in the third month, on the deck, at night, because that is when it goes.

She sat against the rail on the north side with her back to the town. There were women asleep in a row with their covering over their faces, and a man with two goats tied to the same rail, and the goats did not settle either. The engine is under everything and you stop hearing it after an hour and then you hear it again whenever it changes.

She had a bag with almost nothing in it, which is what people carry when they are going somewhere they intend to leave the same day.

She counted the lights of the towns going past. There is nothing else to do on that deck at night. The lights on the down side of any town go out early and the lights further up stay on, so the count goes wrong in the same way at every one of them, and she did it anyway, at four.

That boat is older than the country and older than the last two names of the country. It came here in five thousand crates on a railway that was built to go further and does not, and it was screwed together on the sand, and the navy that owned it sank it on purpose and the navy that took the country off them pulled it up again, and it has been carrying goats and mattresses and people's aunties across this water ever since.

It came in at Uvira before it was light. She went up the road from the shore and she came back down it about eleven hours later and stood in the queue for the deck and crossed again with the light behind her.

She is not going to describe that either.

The boat went on south the same night with the goats still on the rail.

---

School on the Monday. She took the nails off on the Sunday night, the same as always. Alicia asked her in the corridor whether it was true about the man with the car. She said what man. She said all right, and took her arm as she does, and they walked out to the gate together, and she has not asked her anything since.

Here is the thing she has worked out and cannot put down. When this finishes, and it is going to finish, she has known that since the twenty minutes, somebody will be taken for it, and it will not be him. Him they will call as a witness, if they call him at all. They will take somebody visible and poor who cannot afford an envelope, and the whole town will agree to be satisfied, and she thinks it will be Morisho, because he is the one who shouts mia tano mia tano on the road every morning where everybody can see him.

*Do not follow the loudest. The loudest is being paid.*

Her father said that on a shore two years ago about a man in a song, to a boy who was not his son, and it was never once said to her.

Morisho gave her a coin off her fare once and called her shemeji. That is the whole of what he did.

She went to her locker at the end of the day when the corridor was empty. There was a good coat of dust on the inside of the door and she wrote his name in it with one finger.

Daudi.

It is a short name, and it does not take long to write and it does not take long to look at.

Then she wiped it off with the side of her hand, because a name in dust is evidence, and everything she has learned this year she has learned about what happens to people when there is evidence lying around with their name on it.

---

Her father was on Ave de Tanganyique with the fruit when she came past. He had the watermelons stacked in a pyramid, which takes him twenty minutes and which nobody has ever bought a watermelon because of. She did not cross over.

On the way home she stopped at Daudi's house, and he was out, and his father was in, and his father does not look at her, which suited her.

It was the dry season and nothing was coming through the roof. The three buckets stood empty with a white ring dried into the bottom of each.

She went in and took the motherboard down off its nail.

There is a board under the third bucket that comes up if you know it comes up, and she has known since she was nine. She put the fob under it — the lion, cartoon, plastic, the kind you would give a child — and put the board back, and stood on it, and it did not creak. Nothing has ever got in under there and nothing ever will. It is the driest ground in that house and it will still be dry when the house is not.

Then she hung the motherboard on its nail, straight, as he likes it, between the skull and the hubcap.

He will find it. Not soon. But he is a boy who takes things apart, and one day he will be in that room with nothing to do.

She walked home along the shore counting the ships. There are four of them and there have always been four and they have not moved in her lifetime and they will not move in his.

It came out even, the way it does.

The river goes out at the north end of the town, past the cement works, under the bridge where the boys jump off in December, and down through the forest until it finds the Congo, and the Congo takes it to the sea. It is the only way out there is and it has been there longer than the town and longer than any of the names, and she has walked that shore all her life with it on her right hand, and she has never once looked north.

Under the middle of the water a line went down in 1858 and found no bottom at three hundred fathoms, and nothing there has turned over since before any of this, and that is still true tonight, and it will be true on the night somebody is taken for it.

# ⊹ WHAT THE FOURTH PASS FOUND

**1 · The boundary that stops padding is the protagonist's attention, not geography.**
[[mihogoni-3c]] proved a bounded narrator cannot pad, and paid for it with four scenes. **This gets the same discipline for nothing** — the narrator goes only where she goes and notices only what she notices, so the kettle never comes up, and the room, the kiss, the station and the fob all come back. **A narrator fused to a consciousness is bounded by that consciousness.** That is the finding, and it is the one worth carrying forward.

**2 · Deep time is the seam, and there are seven of them.**
No bottom at three hundred fathoms in 1858 · four hulls down in a season nobody wrote down · water that has not turned over since before the town had this name or the two before it · explorers carried the last part of the way · a ship in five thousand crates on a railway built to go further · a river older than any of the names · and the close. **A thirteen-year-old cannot hold any of that.** They arrive in her register with no seam and the story never says who is holding them. → [[Psychic Distance]]

**3 · The five epigraphs survive the move inland and the climb is unchanged.**
Radjabu's stone instruction → Radjabu's sebene instruction → an explorer's register → an imperial notice → a fare table. **A father's knowledge, given free, displaced across five blocks by people who had just arrived, ending at a price for passage.** The word colonialism does not appear. → [[Dite — Story Sheet]]

**4 · MABANGA's tariff is the hostile one and it now sits directly above the crossing.**
*Children under three carried free* · *the vessel does not wait* · *fares payable before boarding, never after* — the last a deliberate echo of the libanga list's *on paie avant le studio, jamais après.* Above it, a girl on a deck at night with a bag containing almost nothing. **The tariff knows what she does not, which is [[MABANGA (New Story — AI Draft, Not Canon)|MABANGA]]'s whole device.**

**5 · Radjabu's lesson detonates, and the detonation is that it was never aimed at her.**
*Do not follow the loudest. The loudest is being paid* is said on a shore, two years early, about a man in a song, **to a boy who is not his son** — and it turns out to be the precise mechanism by which Morisho is taken. Her line about it is four words long: *it was never once said to her.* His file's Contradiction, *a wise man whose wisdom is also a form of avoidance*, is now on the page.

**6 · Two refusals now, and the second one is new.**
*She is not going to describe the hotel* is Draft 3's, protected. **The crossing gets its own** — *she is not going to describe that either* — placed after eleven hours at Uvira are accounted for by the clock and nothing else. **The refusal becomes a habit of hers rather than a single decision**, which is a different and colder characterisation than one refusal is.

**7 · The dry board is back and it is stronger inland than the Lukuga was.**
Placed at the room, the rain, the kiss, the dried rings, the hiding and the close, and the phrasing carries no narrator: *nothing has ever got in under there and nothing ever will.* The Lukuga survives as the paired motif — one way out, and *she has never once looked north* — so **the story keeps both: the place nothing reaches, and the exit nobody takes.**
