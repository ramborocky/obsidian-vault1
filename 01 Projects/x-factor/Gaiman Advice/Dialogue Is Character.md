# DIALOGUE IS CHARACTER

## The principle

> **"Dialogue is character. The way that somebody talks, what they say, how they say it is character."**

And the method, which is the opposite of how this vault has been building characters:

> **"When he's developing characters, Neil doesn't make lists of personality traits. He approaches character by listening to the way people speak and to what they say, and his characters emerge from the content of their speech."**

> *"The process of writing good dialogue is a listening process. It's the process of: you write the line before, and then you listen, and find out what comes back at you."*

**Note what that conflicts with.** Every character file in `02 Characters/` is a dossier — Core Wound, Lie They Believe, Need, Want, Contradiction, Fear, Secret. That is [[Character Dimension and Cast Design|McKee's method]] and it is a good one. **Gaiman's is the inverse: write them talking and find out who they are.** Both produce characters. Use the dossier when the character is inert on the page; use listening when they are inert in your head.

## The compression tools — the most immediately usable list in the three chapters

People do not speak on the page as they do in life. Gaiman's fixes:

| Tool | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Attack wordiness** | Cut as far as you can while keeping meaning and tone |
| **Use fragments** | *"Sounds great"* beats *"That sounds great"* |
| **Use contractions** | *"we'll go together"* beats *"we will go together"* |
| **Trim fillers** | *uh*, *well* |
| **Police names** | **"It's unusual for people to call each other by name."** Search your dialogue for names and delete most of them |
| **Go non-verbal** | *"Instead of having your character shout 'I hate you!', show them kick a chair"* |
| **Put them in motion** | If dialogue goes repetitive, make them walk, drive, or get distracted by the environment |

**The name rule is the one almost everybody fails.** It is a two-minute search-and-delete pass and it makes dialogue immediately more plausible.

### Three registers of dialogue

- **Direct** — quoted. *"I wish I could see you tomorrow," she said.*
- **Indirect** — narrated without quotes, keeping the feeling of presence. *Had she agreed to meet at six? She couldn't remember.*
- **Summary** — reported from a distance. *They'd discussed meeting up tomorrow.*

**Plus stage direction** — any textual reference to physical movement. It carries mood, frame of mind and response as efficiently as speech does.

**And the pacing rule:** dialogue speeds a story up, exposition slows it down. Long dialogue needs short exposition inserted to keep the reader located in time and place, and vice versa.

## FUNNY HATS — the technique to take away

> Gaiman gives secondary characters **one memorable tag** so a reader can hold them without a paragraph of description. He calls them *funny hats*.

| Type of hat | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Physical quirk** | Old Bailey wears a coat of feathers |
| **Verbal style** | Mr. Croup's loquaciousness |
| **A memorable name** | *"Move your character out of 'Jill' territory"* — Lady Door Portico |
| **Extended metaphor** | Croup is fox-like, and the fox imagery recurs |
| **A past event** | *"the girl whose family died"*, *"the widower"* |
| **Last appearance** | *"She recognized the man from the piano store"* |

> [!important] This is the cheapest available fix to the vault's biggest measured weakness
> **[[15 The Chair Test — Scoring the Hidden Factor|The Chair Test]] scores Chantal 3 on axis 4 (The Person).** Djamela is real; **the mother is furniture, Jean Luc has no object, Morisho is a function, Alicia is a device.** That axis and axis 5 together hold nine of the eleven points Chantal is missing.
>
> **Funny hats costs one detail per character.** No rewrite, no cut, no structural change — and [[Home]] has already forbidden cutting for tightness again, so an additive fix is the only kind available.
>
> Compare what it buys, in the gallery: Lubwama's cobbler is *unkempt white beard* + greets a child like an agemate + **knows Jabulanis**. Three details, two scenes, completely alive.

## Where it shows in the gallery

**[[Mothers Not Appearing in Search — ANNOTATED|Lubwama]] hats every single minor character.** Jjaja Reagan never leaves her porch. Makumbi has dogs. Nabongho has effortless dribbles. Pinto is *the boy who got run over*. **Six people, six hats, and the world holds.**

**[[The Serpent in the Grove — ANNOTATED|Nazir]]** hats Marsha in one clause — *"big in the way of women who never apologise to furniture"* — and Wilfred's rum-shop *"leaned into the road like a rotten tooth."*

**[[Dite — ANNOTATED|*Dite*]]** hats Dadi with the hundred-rupee note in the folds of her sari, produced every single visit, including when the narrator is a professor and protests. **One repeated gesture, three decades.**

## Against the other bases

**[[Characters in Fleeting Profile|Faulkner]] and Gaiman agree completely** and phrase it differently: Faulkner wants the *piercing detail that cuts against expectation*; Gaiman wants a *tag the reader can carry*. **Faulkner's is about depth, Gaiman's is about memory.** Use both — the hat should also surprise.

**[[Character by Salient Detail|Barrett]]** is the 1900 ancestor of both.

**Conflict with [[Character Dimension and Cast Design|McKee]] on method, not on outcome.** Dossier vs listening. **The vault has done it exclusively McKee's way**, and the character files are strong — yet the Chair Test still scores The Person at 3, which suggests the dossiers have not been converted into anything visible on the page. **Gaiman's method is the missing conversion step.**

**The Writing Constitution** — *dialogue reveals class* — is this chapter's house version, and it is already a standing rule.

## The exercises

**1 · Transcribe a real person.** Record a friend for twenty minutes, transcribe, then craft it into fictional dialogue using the compression tools. *"People don't speak in real life like they do on the page."*

**2 · Hat your whole cast.** List every named character and give each one a funny hat. **This is the single highest-value exercise in the seven bases for this project right now.**

**3 · The blind description.** *"Pretend a blind person is describing your main character after having met them."* How do they explain the character using the other senses? Would your character be thoughtful, clumsy, rude toward them?

**4 · Observe a stranger.** Public place, one person, invent a name, a reason, a feeling. One page. **One detail that makes them distinct.** Blend their thoughts into the world around them, and *"don't be afraid to make their inner world completely different from their appearance."*

**5 · Argue.** Write a brief dialogue in which your character disagrees with someone, then add descriptive sentences until it is balanced.

**6 · Interview your character.** The worksheet: how do you feel about your looks · about money · what three objects best describe your childhood · what's the worst thing that ever happened to you · are you religious · who is the most important person in the world to you right now.

> [!quote] On writing people unlike you
> *"You are going into yourself. And you have to not be afraid of yourself. You have to be willing, if you're writing a murderer, if you're a bad person, to go and find that part of you that is the bad person… okay, what would you say? What would you do? Who are you?"*
>
> And the practical half: *"You may need to write about types of people who you do not know… go find them, go talk to them. Talk to people."*
