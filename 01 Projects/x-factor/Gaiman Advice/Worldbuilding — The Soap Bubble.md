# WORLDBUILDING — THE SOAP BUBBLE

## The principle, and it is the best sentence in the four chapters

> **"The worlds that we build in fiction, they're soap bubbles. They can pop really easily… But that one little moment of reality, that one thing that seems to be absolutely true, gives credence, and gives credibility to all of the things that you don't say."**

**One true thing pays for everything unsaid.** That is the whole chapter, and it is the fifth independent arrival at the principle this folder keeps circling — after [[The X-Factor|leakage]], [[Found Objects and the Infraordinary|Perec's infraordinary]], [[The Underground Rubric — Risk, Afterlife and the Room|Gate 3]] and [[The Short Story as an Ending|"they've all been in existence all along"]].

**And the rule that follows from it:**

> ### "Always know more than you tell."
> The iceberg, applied to place: **90% of what you know stays under. 10% reaches the page.**

## The Bradbury demonstration — this is the technique

Gaiman's example, and it is worth memorising. *The Martian Chronicles*, a quiet night in a Mars colony:

> *"here or there a late supper was prepared in tables where lava bubbled silvery and hushed"*

**Bradbury never explains why lava is silver on Mars, or why it bubbles near tables.**

> **The confidence with which an unexplained detail is stated is the thing that convinces.** Not the detail. The confidence.
>
> A writer who explains has told you they were worried you wouldn't believe it. A writer who states it flat and moves on has told you they know a hundred more.

**This is directly actionable for Chantal**, whose [[15 The Chair Test — Scoring the Hidden Factor|Chair Test]] World score is **2**. Not more description. **Unexplained, confidently stated facts about Mihogoni that the story never returns to.**

## The weird questions

Gaiman's, and they are deliberately unglamorous. **Answer them for your setting even though none of the answers will appear on the page.**

- **Where do people go to the toilet?**
- **Where does the food come from? Where does it come *in*?**
- How much farmland? Where are the farms?
- **What does poverty look like in your world?**
- Who has power, and how do they treat it?
- What are the rituals?
- What languages, and how many?
- **What was different fifteen years ago?** *Was that business closed then? Did that street have another name? Was the statue there before your characters were?*

## Rules, and why they matter even in a realist story

> **"Even if the world you're building is exactly like our world, it's going to have rules."**

And the craft instruction attached:

> **"It's good not to spell them out directly to your reader, rather let your characters grapple with them… let the rules become apparent from your characters' experiences."**
>
> *"Ideally your characters will discover those rules the hard way."*

**In the best cases, rules create the drama.** A rule nobody has to discover is furniture; a rule a character breaks and pays for is a scene.

## The other tools

| Tool | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Smuggle from your own life** | Three details you remember from recent experience, described sensorily. *"Don't be afraid to borrow details from your own life"* |
| **Confluence** | Combine unrelated things to get something that is neither |
| **The map** | Draw it, whatever your drawing is like. *"Actually drawing your own world can make it more familiar and trigger fresh insights"* |
| **The ten objects** | Imagine a character's personal space. List ten objects in it. Take the most interesting and build the description around that one |
| **Go there and record nothing** | Visit a place. **No photos, no notes, no phone.** Absorb it. Write it at home, from memory, with the senses |
| **The compost heap** | Jot everything. *"I may never go back to them… but once they're jotted down, they're rotting away, usefully"* |
| **Avoid the rip-off** | The thing you love has been rehashed until it is its own cliché |

> [!important] The one that transfers hardest to a 3,900-word story
> **"Go there and record nothing."** Every other tool produces material. This one produces *selection* — what survives the walk home is what mattered, and that is exactly the filter a short story needs.

## Where to study it in the gallery

> **All three stories build a whole society and none of them describes one.** Every example below is a *clause*, not a paragraph.

### 🏆 *The Serpent in the Grove* — the best worldbuilding of the three

| The clause | What it builds |
| --- | --- |
| *"People said the British had dug those wells… older people said the British found a hole the island had dug for itself"* | **Two competing histories of one object, held by two generations.** A past the village disagrees about. Nothing else in the story needs this |
| *"He could name the price of rice in the shop, the price buyers would give for wet cocoa, and how the distance between the two left a man short"* | **An entire agricultural economy**, and the only explanation of his poverty in the story |
| *"Water was half a mile away; every drop hissed in the pan had been carried on somebody's spine"* | **Gaiman's water question, answered in one clause** — and it makes the well a life-or-death object 20% before anyone falls in it |
| *"Coins meant for rice or kerosene slid across the counter"* | Household budgeting, and what alcohol costs a family |
| *"a ledger whitened by many small tragedies"* | A rural health service, in five words |
| *"a letter she'd promised to write for a mother whose son had been held for cussing a policeman"* | **Illiteracy, policing and the justice system**, in a subordinate clause, mid-crisis |

### *Dite* — worldbuilding as history

| The clause | What it builds |
| --- | --- |
| *the boutikier sinwa "who keeps tabs in a large, tattered notebook: rice, oil, flour, and… exercise books and pencils"* | **The credit economy of a poor household** — and it detonates 79 points later as the indenture ledger |
| *"At the beginning of every month, his pay is swallowed by the previous month"* | **The debt cycle in nine words.** Gaiman's *what does poverty look like*, answered without the word |
| *"those local pastries with French names that are uniquely Mauritian and for which Muslim bakers on the island seem to have a special talent"* | **Colonisation, religion and cuisine in one sentence about cake** |
| The canes *"hewn from dried bamboo culms, coconut fibre rods, or badamier stems"* | **Local flora becoming instruments of discipline.** The landscape enters through violence |
| **Rule 7** (skirt length) and **Rule 2** (only French allowed) | **Gaiman's rules, quoted as rules** — and the narrator grapples with both, hard |

### *Mothers Not Appearing in Search* — worldbuilding as institutions

| The clause | What it builds |
| --- | --- |
| **The driving school leasing the football field** — then the truce, the free car washes, the traffic cones for goalposts | **An institution with its own agenda**, negotiating with children. A page and a half serving no plot |
| *Jjaja Reagan's porch · Makumbi's dogs · Fatima's gate* | **A child's world bounded by three landmarks.** The map, drawn in one sentence |
| *a two-hundred-shilling coin · a five-hundred-shilling coin* | **Real currency in real amounts.** A boy's economics |
| *"polytechnic whore"* | **An education system, weaponised as a slur** — and a word the narrator never learns the meaning of |
| *Ronaldo could not possibly have spoken Luganda* | Media, language and a child's reach, in one joke |

## Against the other bases

**[[Found Objects and the Infraordinary|Faulkner and Perec]]** get to the same place philosophically — the everyday is *"the site of all possible signification."* **Gaiman gets there practically:** answer the toilet question, then don't put it in.

**[[Verisimilitude, Not Verity — The Use of Facts|Barrett]]** wants facts that persuade. **Gaiman's addition is the ratio** — 90/10 — and the Bradbury proof that an *unexplained* fact persuades harder than an explained one.

**[[setting and character.md|McKee on setting]]** treats world as a dimension of character. Compatible, and Gaiman's *"treat your characters as research interests"* is the bridge when the story happens in one room.

**[[The Vivid and Continuous Fictional Dream|Gardner]]** — the dream is sustained by exactly this. A world that doesn't leak is a set.
