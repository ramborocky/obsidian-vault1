# THE SHORT STORY AS AN ENDING

## The principle

> **Write the short story as if it were the last chapter of a novel you already wrote.**

*"Much of what you're creating in terms of your characters and world will not make it into the final story. Instead, you want to hit the most important moment — the story's climax."*

**This is the most practically useful idea in the three chapters**, because it resolves a contradiction every short-story writer runs into: you need a world thick enough to be believed and no room to build one. Gaiman's answer is that **you build it anyway and then don't use it.**

Which produces the line that names what this whole folder has been circling:

> **"You want to feel like these characters didn't just start to exist the moment the story began. You want to know they've all been in existence all along."**

> [!success] Four independent routes to one idea
> **Gaiman:** the characters were in existence all along.
> **[[Found Objects and the Infraordinary|Perec, via Faulkner]]:** the *infraordinary* — *"what's really going on, what we're experiencing, the rest, all the rest."*
> **[[The Underground Rubric — Risk, Afterlife and the Room|Gate 3]]:** Leakage — the world larger than the story.
> **[[The X-Factor]]:** the cut test — *cut it; does the plot notice?*
>
> A novelist, a French experimentalist, this vault's own instrument and a measured experiment all arriving at the same place. **When four unrelated sources converge, stop treating it as a preference and start treating it as a rule.**

### Economy, reframed

> **"Instead of being paid by the word, I am paying by the word — the fewer words I can use to tell my story, the better."**

And the timing rule that goes with it: **compression happens in editing, not in drafting.** *"When you're beginning a project, it's important to banish your inner critic and allow yourself to write freely. Later, you'll be able to trim."*

**Orwell's four questions**, which Gaiman uses as his sentence-level edit pass:

1. What am I trying to say?
2. What words will express it?
3. What image or idiom will make it clearer?
4. **Is this image fresh enough to have an effect?**

### Start in the middle

*In medias res.* Cut the descriptive opening. **Start on a phrase of dialogue or an action.** *"Don't be afraid to disorient your reader… Let them be confused for a moment."* Then weave the cut description back in later.

And the corollary: **"What does the reader want to know most? Whatever it is, it should come at the end — so drag it out."**

### On whether scenes must turn — he refuses to settle it

Gaiman states the traditional rule (shift the emotional charge to its opposite) and then says plainly:

> *"There is a debate among writers whether or not scenes must turn in a short story. Sometimes your protagonist will resist all change, or decline a critical moment of transformation. Some short stories offer snippets of experience that don't adhere to traditional forms."*

**That is [[Plot as Yearning, Not Pyramid|the McKee–Faulkner fight]], acknowledged by a practitioner who declines to adjudicate it.** Take the refusal seriously: it means the answer is per-story, and anyone selling you a universal rule is selling.

## Where it shows in the gallery

**[[The Serpent in the Grove — ANNOTATED|Nazir]] is the clearest "ending of a novel."** Vishnu's father and his father's father, the British digging the wells, Marsha's whole letter-writing practice, Zoongie's return with a baby — **none of it is the story, all of it is the novel behind the story.** The climax lands at 50%, which is what happens when you write only the last chapter.

**[[Mothers Not Appearing in Search — ANNOTATED|Lubwama]] opens *in medias res* on an image the reader must decode alone** — children blowing into used condoms — and does not explain for the rest of the story. Gaiman's *"let them be confused for a moment"*, taken to its limit.

**[[Dite — ANNOTATED|*Dite*]] withholds what the reader most wants to know — the indenture record — until 92%.** Gaiman's rule exactly: *drag it out.*

## The exercises

**1 · The 60-second start.** Answer one of these as briefly as possible, first response only:

- What was the most embarrassing thing you've experienced in the past few years?
- When was the last time you cried, and what caused it?
- What shocked you so much you were speechless?
- What was the very best or worst moment of your childhood?
- Have you fantasised about revenge recently — against whom?
- On your deathbed looking back: who did you love the most? **Be brutally honest.**

Then **30 minutes on a timer** to write the whole story. *"Don't worry about craft or structure. Write until the timer stops."*

**2 · Condense by a third.** Take a recent page and cut 33%. Rewrite completely if necessary.

**3 · Change the setting.** Move a scene somewhere hostile — *"if you wrote about a sniper looking through a rifle scope, move him to a crowded airport."* Take a risk; go past what you are comfortable with.

**4 · THE PREDICTION READ** — and this is the best reading exercise in any of the seven bases:

> **Read the first page or two of an unfamiliar story. STOP. Write down what you think the ending will be and why. Then finish it and compare.**
>
> *"What elements led you to believe in a certain ending? How did the author surprise you?"*

**It measures your own expectations rather than the story's quality**, which is the thing a writer actually needs to know — because the expectations you have are the ones your reader has.

> [!important] For the Afterlife axis
> **"Stories you read when you're the right age never quite leave you. You may forget who wrote them or what the story was called. Sometimes you'll forget precisely what happened. But if a story touches you, it will stay with you."**
>
> That is [[15 The Chair Test — Scoring the Hidden Factor|the Chair Test's unscoreable sixth axis]], described from the reader's side.
