# THE BEAT OF MY HURT
### Draft 2 — the full apparatus

There are eleven beats for the dead and me, I know nine.

My uncle knows eleven. He will not give me the last two, and when I ask he says the same thing he has said since I was nine years old, which is that a boy who learns the tenth beat starts hunting for a reason to play it.

Rule one. The drum is not sad. You are not sad. The people are sad and you are working.

Combien, they ask. Everybody asks it. It is the first word this town learned and the last one it will forget. Combien for the drum, combien for the chairs, combien for the priest, combien for the hole, combien for the lorry to carry the hole-diggers, combien to have your brother's name read out slowly instead of fast.

Combien.

---

We are two towns and we pretend to be one.

Up the hill is Stima, which means electricity, and that is not the name on any map. It is what we call it. Behind the walls up there the generators start at seven and go until eleven and you can hear them from the water, all of them, out of time with each other, like a band that has never rehearsed.

Down here is Giza. Darkness. Also not on the map.

At seven the délestage takes us and Stima's windows come on in the same second, and if you are standing on Avenue du Lac you can watch the whole hill light up in a row while your own street goes out, and nobody ever comments, because what is there to say about a thing that happens every day of your life.

The town's other name is the Terminus, because the railway from Kabalo ends here and does not go on. It was built to go on. The maps in the old station still show where it was going. It has not gone anywhere since before my mother was born, and men still sit at that station in the afternoon the way men sit at a bedside.

Rule four. Never the fast beat for a child. The family will ask. You say no.

---

The maquis where drummers get hired is behind the fish market and it has no name, so we call it Chez Mado, after a woman who sold there in 1998 and is dead.

On any night at Chez Mado you will find, or bicycle mechanics with one good eye or soldiers who have not been paid since March or soldiers who have never been paid or girls of nineteen who have already buried a child or girls of fifteen who work the port road and are not allowed inside and stand at the edge of the light or men who sold their motorcycle to buy a motorcycle or pastors of churches that meet in a yard or pastors of churches that meet in a different yard because of a disagreement about the roof or fishermen back from four days out with dagaa still in their hair or a man who repairs the mobile phones of people who cannot afford to charge them or Tanzanians over from Kigoma with shillings in a sock or the one Chinese engineer everybody calls Papa Zhang who has been here nine years and speaks Swahili better than French or three brothers who all say they are the eldest or widows of soldiers or widows of the lake or the boy who sells single cigarettes and one boiled egg or a teacher owed eleven months or a man with a suitcase of secondhand spectacles who will let you try every pair or thieves resting or thieves working or my uncle, at the back, where he always is, with his hat off and his drum between his feet.

Everybody wants the same two things and nobody in that room would say so out loud.

Rule nine. You eat what they give you and you eat it in the yard.

---

Here is the thing about my work that people from Stima do not understand.

A drummer goes in. Everywhere. The gate opens for the drum. I have stood in kitchens up that hill with taps that run hot, and I have stood in rooms in Giza where the family borrowed the chair I was sitting on. Same fee, more or less. Same nine beats.

Beat four is the old person's beat. Slow, with the lift placed on the third, and you put that lift late — so late a trained musician would call it a mistake — and the room comes with you, and the crying arrives where you have put it. That is the whole craft. You are building somewhere for a thing to be set down.

Beat two is the child. It is almost nothing. A heartbeat and a gap. My uncle plays it and I am not permitted.

Beat six is the drowned. There is a great deal of six in this town.

Fifteen thousand for the house. Fifteen for the walk. The walk is the harder work: you are moving, the road is broken past the water point, and if you let the tempo fall the procession folds up on itself like a concertina and the men at the front begin to argue about whose fault it is.

For a family with nothing, my uncle takes a chicken. He has never said this to anybody and he has never written it down.

Combien.

---

The Ngoie boy died on a Thursday and they came to Chez Mado on the Friday.

Rule twenty-two, my uncle says. In Giza nobody dies of anything. We die of Thursday.

It was the mother's brother. Suit jacket over a football shirt, standing, not sitting, holding his hat in both hands like something he had been asked to carry for somebody else.

Come and beat for us, he said. Sunday. Emmanuel Ngoie.

I knew the name. Everybody on the port road knew the name. Twenty-four years old, worked the crane, the kind of young man mamas point at.

Then he said: beat four.

My uncle was relacing the small drum and his hands did not change speed. I have watched him do that a hundred times and I have never once managed to copy it.

Beat four, my uncle said.

Beat four.

He was twenty-four years old.

He was, said the man.

They stood there. Down the hill a woman was calling her children in, one at a time, by name. There were three. The third did not come.

My uncle said the price and the man paid it at the door, in dollars, which men do when they want the conversation finished.

---

Rule seven. What the family asks for is what the family gets. You are not the police. You are not the priest. You are a man with a drum and a fee.

I have thought about seven more than all the others together.

Here is what rule seven means. The beat is a statement. When the drum says four, the town hears old age, and the town puts its shoulders down, because old age is nobody's fault. It means a family can buy the version of how their son died for thirty thousand francs and a bottle for the drummers.

Article 15 of our constitution is not written in the constitution. Débrouillez-vous. Work it out yourselves. Everybody here can recite it and nobody can find it.

So we work it out. A man works out how to sell you a version of a death. A drummer works out how to play it. This is not corruption. Corruption is when a thing has stopped working properly. Here it is working exactly as built.

I have played four over a boy who was cut. I have played four over a girl who took something out of a bottle. I played four on the hill for a man whose widow stood at the back with her arm in a sling two weeks older than his death.

Each time I took the money. Rule seven. And because we needed it. And because my uncle's hands did not change speed.

---

Sunday came hot and without wind, which is the worst kind for a walk.

I made the fire at six in our yard and held the ngoma sideways over it and turned it, and the skin came up, and you can hear it come up. Goat, not cow. Cow is cheaper and louder and wrong; cow gives you the note and goat gives you the note and the thing underneath it, and the thing underneath it is what they are actually paying for, though not one of them could tell you so.

My mother came out and stood in the doorway and watched me do it. She has watched me do it maybe four hundred times. She still comes out.

The Ngoie house was full by eight. Blue plastic chairs from the man behind the cathedral, who owns perhaps two hundred and is at every funeral and every wedding in this town and has never, as far as anyone knows, attended one.

Emmanuel was in the front room with a cloth over him from the chest down.

Nobody was looking at the cloth. Me, I knew from that.

His mother sat where the mother sits and did not cry once all morning, and people noticed, and I could hear them deciding what it meant, and every one of them decided wrong, because the right answer was not on offer.

At the back, three men from the port stood together, did not sit, did not eat. One of them kept watching the road.

Combien, somebody was asking in the yard, about the lorry.

Beat four, my uncle said.

---

So I played it.

It is a good beat and I am good at it. They cried well. The aunts did the ululation and the church women did their harmony and the mother sat dry as a stone in the middle of it like something the water had gone around.

We walked at eleven.

Up, then along. Past the water point where a lorry broke the road two rains ago and nobody came. On that stretch you drop from the hand to the wrist and you keep it dead flat and you never let the front hear you struggle. I did not struggle. It was a clean walk.

At the grave the mother's brother gave me the second fifteen thousand, dollars again, folded small the way men fold money they have been holding a long time.

Come and beat for us, he said, meaning next time. It is what people say.

---

Back at Chez Mado by four.

This is the part nobody writes down about my trade. You bury a man at noon and you are drinking at four with the money you were paid for it, and there is nothing wrong with this, and everybody there has done the same, and the lutuku costs five hundred and burns like an argument.

Rule thirty-one. Beer in Giza is not drink. Beer in Giza is a receipt.

The girls from the port road stood at the edge of the light where they stand. One of them, Neema, is fifteen and was at school with my sister and now is not. Nobody at Chez Mado says her name in the daytime. In the daytime she is nobody's business, and at night she is everybody's, and the distance between those two facts is about four hours and a generator.

Combien, said a man to her, and she said a number, and he laughed and said half, and she said the number again, and he paid it, and that was the whole conversation and it lasted eleven seconds.

Me, I counted. I count everything. It is the same skill.

My uncle drank and said nothing about the funeral and nothing about beat four, and at some point he put his hand flat on my head the way he does, once, and took it off again.

---

I walked home the long way, on the shore, because after a matanga I cannot take the streets.

The lake was doing its flat afternoon thing. Pirogues out for dagaa and the light on the water hard and white so that it hurt to look at, so I looked at it.

And my hands were still going.

That happens. You come off a walk and the hands do not know it has finished, and they carry on with whatever you played. Mine were not playing four.

I stopped in the road and let them run and listened to my own hands the way you would listen to somebody else's.

It was slower than four. No lift on the third. It sat down on the two and stayed there, and the space after was wrong, too long, the length of a held breath, and my hands knew exactly how long to hold it and I did not.

I stood there and worked out where I had heard it.

I was six. Our yard. There was a fire and my uncle was turning a drum over it and my mother pulled me back from the flame twice by the shoulder. There was a cloth over my father from the chest down.

Nobody was looking at the cloth.

And my uncle played, and the town came, and afterwards everybody said the thing they say, which is that he went quickly and God is good.

Ten years I have asked that man to teach me the tenth beat.

Ten years he has told me a boy who learns it starts hunting for a reason.

---

My mother was at her table when I got back. She sells fried dough at the bus stage from a table with a leg mended with wire, and she has mended that leg four times, and each time she says it will hold until Christmas.

She asked was it a good matanga. I said it was clean. She said good, and took the second fifteen thousand, and put it in the tin.

Me, I did not ask her. I want that written down somewhere. I could have asked her standing right there and I did not, and it was not kindness and it was not fear. It was that I had already done the arithmetic on what the answer would cost and the number came out higher than I could pay that day.

She said the leg had gone again. I said I would look at it.

---

The délestage took us at seven and Stima came on, the whole row, steady as anything.

I went back to Chez Mado because that is where I go, and that is the thing about this town, you leave and you arrive at the same door, and the door is always the same door, and each time you come through it you are carrying more than you were carrying before.

My uncle was at the back with his hat off and his drum between his feet.

I sat. I did not say anything for a long time. Then I said: play me the tenth.

He looked at the table.

Rule two, he said. You do not ask a man to give you a thing he has been carrying for you.

That is not one of the rules, I said.

It is now, he said.

Then he said: your father did not go quickly.

He said it to the table. He did not say the rest of it and I have not asked him and I am not going to, because I already have it, entire, the shape of a room nobody has ever put a light on.

Combien, a man was shouting at the counter. Combien, combien.

---

The seventh night is the last night of a matanga, when the family sits up and the drum comes back for one hour. It is the only hour of the whole business that is not for the dead. It is to send the living to bed.

I went to the Ngoie house alone. My uncle had a wedding at Kabimba and weddings pay better and both of us knew that was not the reason.

Half the chairs had gone back. The mother was in the same seat. The three men from the port had not been seen in seven days, and their names had not been said once, and in Giza seven days is a very long time for a name not to be said.

I sat and put the drum between my knees and warmed my hands on the skin, which does nothing, and which I do.

The mother looked at me for the first time all week.

So I played the other one.

I got it wrong twice, because my hands knew it and I did not, and there is a distance between those two things you have to cross with nobody helping you. The third time it came. It sat down on the two. It stayed. It left the space after it and I let the space be as long as it wanted to be.

Nobody cried. That beat is not built for crying. I understood that while I was playing it.

The mother stood up on the second bar and stayed standing for all of it, hands at her sides, in front of everybody, and she was not looking at me. She was looking at the door, and the road, and the place where three men had stood and had not eaten.

Not one person in that room asked me what I was playing.

When I stopped she sat down and put her hands over her face and made a sound I have not got a beat for.

---

I will not be hired in Stima again. That was decided while I was playing, somewhere underneath the counting, which is where I keep the things I have chosen not to look at yet.

Combien, and this time I know.

Rule one. The drum is not sad. You are not sad. The people are sad and you are working.

I went home along the shore. The lake had gone black and there was no light at all on our side, and up the hill the windows were yellow, the whole row of them, and the generators were going out of time with each other the way they do.

I built a small fire in the yard. Not to warm myself. I held the ngoma sideways over it and turned it, and listened for the note to come up, and it came up, and I kept turning it a long time after it was tight.

My mother came out and stood in the doorway.

She did not ask me why I was warming a drum I was not going to play.
