# THE BEAT OF MY HURT
### Draft 4

There are eleven beats for the dead and me, I know nine.

My uncle knows eleven. He will not give me the last two, and when I ask he says the thing he has said since I was small, which is that a boy who learns the tenth beat starts hunting for a reason to play it.

Rule one. The drum is not sad. You are not sad. The people are sad and you are working.

My name is Ilunga. My father died when I was six and my uncle beat for him. My mother sells fried dough at the bus stage from a table with a leg mended with wire.

Rule four. Never the fast one for a child. The family will ask. You say no.

---

The skin is goat. Cow is cheaper and louder and wrong — cow gives you the note, goat gives you the note and the thing underneath it, and the thing underneath it is what they are paying for, though not one of them could tell you so.

In the wet season it goes slack by ten in the morning. So before every matanga I build a small fire in the yard, not to warm myself, and I hold the drum sideways over it and turn it, and the skin comes up, and you can hear it come up. My uncle says a man who cannot hear his own drum tighten should go and sell airtime.

My mother comes out and stands in the doorway while I do it. She has watched me do it maybe four hundred times. She still comes out.

---

Beat four is the old person's. It is slow and it has a lift on the third, and the whole of it is in where you put that lift. You put it late. So late that a trained man would call it a mistake. And the room comes with you, and about eight seconds after you have started, forty people break at once, because you have built them somewhere to break.

I am going to put the true thing down here because if I leave it out then everything after it is a lie.

That moment is the best thing that happens to me in a week.

It has nothing to do with the person in the box. It is not sorrow and it is not respect and it is not even music. It is the feeling a man gets when a thing he made works. When the room goes, I have done my job, and I know I have done it better than my uncle did it at my age, and I go and eat what they give me in the yard and I am pleased with myself all the way home.

I have had that feeling over a child. I had it, and then I ate the food, and then I slept.

I am not asking anybody to make that all right. I am saying it is what the job is.

---

We are two towns and we pretend to be one.

Up the hill is Stima, which is not on any map — it is only what we call it, because at seven the délestage takes us and their generators start in the same second. Down here is Giza. Darkness. Also not on the map. Stand on the shore road at seven and you can watch the hill come on in a row while your own street goes out, and nobody has ever remarked on it, because what is there to say about a thing that has happened every evening of your life.

The railway ends at this town and does not go on. It was built to go on. Men still sit at the old station in the afternoons the way men sit at a bedside.

A drummer goes in, though. That is what they do not understand up there. The gate opens for the drum. I have stood in kitchens on that hill with taps that run hot and in rooms down here where the family borrowed the chair I sat on, and it is the same fee, more or less, and the same nine beats.

Beat two is the child. Almost nothing. A heartbeat and a gap. My uncle plays it and I am not permitted.

Beat six is the drowned. There is a great deal of six in this town.

Fifteen thousand for the house, fifteen for the walk. The walk is harder — you are moving, the road is broken past the water point, and if you let the tempo fall the procession folds up on itself like a concertina and the men at the front start arguing about whose fault it is.

For a family with nothing my uncle takes a chicken. He has never said this to anybody and he has never written it down.

Most of a matanga is waiting, and nobody tells you that. You get there at eight and you play for forty minutes across seven hours. The rest of it you are on a plastic chair in a yard with the drum between your feet, being bored. There is a particular kind of bored that only happens near a body and I have never worked out what to call it.

---

The Ngoie boy died on a Thursday and they came to us on the Friday.

It was the mother's brother, suit jacket over a football shirt, holding his hat in both hands like something he had been asked to carry for somebody else. He said come and beat for us. He said Sunday. He said the name, Emmanuel Ngoie, and I knew it — everybody on the port road knew it — twenty-four years old, worked the crane, the kind of young man mothers point at.

Then he said: beat four.

My uncle was relacing the small drum and his hands did not change speed. I have watched him do that a hundred times and I have never managed it.

Beat four, my uncle said.

Beat four.

He is twenty-four years old.

He was, said the man.

They stood there. Down the hill a woman was calling her children in, one at a time, by name. There were three. The third did not come.

My uncle said the price and the man paid it at the door in dollars, which men do when they want the conversation finished.

---

Rule seven. What the family asks for is what the family gets. You are not the police. You are not the priest. You are a man with a drum and a fee.

When the drum says four, the town hears old age, and the town puts its shoulders down, because old age is nobody's fault. A family can buy the version of how their son died for thirty thousand and a bottle for the drummers.

I have played four over a boy who was cut. I have played four over a girl who took something out of a bottle. I played four on the hill for a man whose widow stood at the back with her arm in a sling two weeks older than his death.

Each time I took the money, and each time the room went where I put it, and each time I was pleased.

---

Sunday came hot and without wind, which is the worst kind for a walk.

Blue plastic chairs from the man behind the cathedral, who owns two hundred of them and is at every funeral and every wedding in this town and has never, so far as anyone knows, attended one. Somebody once told me it is three hundred. I have never bothered to find out.

Emmanuel was in the front room with a cloth over him from the chest down.

Nobody was looking at the cloth. Me, I knew from that.

His mother sat where the mother sits and did not cry once all morning, and people noticed, and I could hear them deciding what it meant, and every one of them decided wrong, because the right answer was not on offer.

At the back, three men from the port stood together, did not sit, did not eat. One of them kept watching the road.

I played four. I put the lift where I put it. The room went at about eight seconds, the way it does, and the aunts started and the church women came in under them, and I felt what I feel.

The mother did not move.

We walked at eleven. Up, then along. On the broken stretch you drop from the hand to the wrist and keep it flat and never let the front hear you struggle. I did not struggle. It was a clean walk.

At the grave the mother's brother gave me the second fifteen thousand, dollars again, folded small the way men fold money they have been holding a long time.

Come and beat for us, he said, meaning next time. It is what people say.

---

I walked home on the shore because after a matanga I cannot take the streets.

The lake was doing its flat afternoon thing. Pirogues out for dagaa and the light on the water hard and white so that it hurt to look at, so I looked at it.

And my hands were still going.

That happens. You come off a walk and they do not know it has finished and they carry on with what you played. Mine were not playing four.

I stopped in the road and let them run and listened to my own hands the way you would listen to somebody else's.

Slower than four. No lift on the third. It sat down on the two and stayed there, and the space after was wrong, too long, the length of a held breath, and my hands knew exactly how long to hold it and I did not.

I stood there and worked out where I had heard it.

I was six. Our own yard. There was a fire and my uncle was turning a drum over it and my mother pulled me back from the flame twice by the shoulder. There was a cloth over my father from the chest down, and nobody was looking at the cloth, and afterwards everybody said the thing they say, which is that he went quickly and God is good.

Ten years I have asked that man to teach me the tenth beat.

Somebody had been paid for mine.

---

On the Tuesday I had a job at Kabimba, a real one, an old man of eighty-one who died in a bed with his family around him, which happens here and which people do not believe when you tell them.

It was a good house. They fed us before, which is unusual. The eldest son shook my hand with both of his and told me twice that his father had liked drumming, which was not true and which he needed to say.

I built the fire and warmed the skin and heard it come up.

I sat down and put the lift late, later than I have ever put it, later than my uncle would have allowed, and at about eight seconds the room went. All of it. The daughters, the church women, an old man at the back who had not been going to and then did. It was the best four I have ever played in my life and I know that the way you know your own handwriting.

And nothing happened to me.

I waited for it. I sat there with my hands going and I waited for the thing I have been paid in since I was eleven, and the room was crying exactly where I had put it for them to cry, and I felt nothing at all, and after a while I understood that I was going to go on feeling nothing, and I finished the walk and took the money and came home.

That is the part I did not expect. Not the shore. Not the hands. That the wages stopped.

---

I did nothing for four more days.

I fixed the leg of my mother's table with a strip of inner tube, which is better than wire and which I should have done two years ago. I went down to the port and sat with the men who sit there and listened to a long argument about a boat that has not run since before I was born. I did not go to my uncle's and he did not come to ours, and the not-going and the not-coming were the loudest thing in Giza that week and nobody heard them but the two of us.

On the Thursday I passed him at the water point.

He had a jerrican in each hand and he put one down.

The rains will come early, he said.

They might.

He looked up the hill, where the walls are, and he said that when he was my age there was no water point here at all, you went to the lake, and a woman he named who I have never heard of used to sell a cup for five francs to people too lazy to walk, and that she made a living from it for eleven years.

Then he picked the jerrican up again.

Your mother is well, he said. It was not a question and he did not wait, and I stood at that water point for a while after he had gone up, and then I filled my own and went home.

I could have asked him. He was two metres from me and I had four days of it in my mouth and I did not, and it was not kindness and it was not fear. I have never been able to stop doing arithmetic, and what I got was that my mother has one son and one table and a tin, and that the answer takes one of those away from her.

---

The seventh night is the last night of a matanga, when the family sits up and the drum comes back for one hour. It is the only hour of the whole business that is not for the dead. It is to send the living to bed.

He was already there when I arrived.

He had his hat off and the small drum between his feet and he had built the fire himself, out in the yard where they had been cooking, and he was turning my drum over it because he had got there first. He handed it to me tight. He did not look at me while he did it.

Half the chairs had gone back. The mother was in the same seat. The three men from the port had not been seen in seven days and their names had not been said once, and here seven days is a very long time for a name not to be said.

I sat down and put the drum between my knees and warmed my hands on the skin, which does nothing, and which I do.

The mother looked at me for the first time all week.

---

I started it wrong.

My hands knew the thing and I did not, and there is a distance between those two facts that you have to cross with nobody helping you, and I did not cross it. What came out was four with the lift taken off, which is not the tenth beat, which is not anything, which is a man forgetting his job in front of forty people. Somebody in the yard laughed and stopped.

I tried again and got two bars and lost it.

And then, underneath me, the small drum came in.

He did not look up. He came in on the two and stayed there, and he left the space after it, and he held it exactly as long as it wanted to be held. He had not played that beat in ten years and he did not have to find it. He put it down in front of me the way you put a tool in a boy's hand, without ceremony, expecting it to be used.

I came in on top of him.

The first thing that happens when you get inside a beat is that you stop hearing the room. That is not a way of speaking. The room goes. I have had it maybe six times and every time since I have tried to make it come by wanting it, and it does not work that way.

What was left was the beat and what the beat is for.

It is slower than four and that is the whole argument. Four moves people along. It takes the crying and puts it somewhere and everybody goes home lighter and I go home paid. This one does not go anywhere. It arrives on the two and it sits. Then it leaves that space, and the space is not a rest, because a rest is for the player. What goes into it is the thing nobody in the yard has said. The longer you hold it the more of that thing has to go in, and there is a point past which people cannot bear it. That point is a good deal further away than you would think.

I held it. He held it under me. We went round again.

And I saw the fire in our own yard. Not remembered. Saw, the way you see a thing standing in a doorway. My mother's hand coming down on my shoulder and pulling me back from the flame, twice, and me not understanding why she was angry, and her not being angry.

I saw the cloth.

I saw men who came to our house that week and did not eat, and I had never once until that moment thought about the fact that there were men who came to our house that week and did not eat.

I saw my uncle at six in the morning turning a drum over a fire so that he could tell forty people his brother went quickly.

I do not know what he is. I know what he took.

And I know that he sat in that yard with his hat off and played the true one under me for a boy who was nothing to him, with nothing in it for him, knowing what it was going to cost, and that he came in on the two without being asked.

Somewhere in it he dropped out and left me carrying it alone and I did not notice when. That is how good he is.

Nobody cried. I understood that while I was playing. The beat is not built for crying. It is built for the other thing, the one that has no sound of its own and has to borrow.

The mother stood up on the second bar and stayed standing the whole time, hands at her sides, in front of everybody. She was not looking at me. She was looking at the door, and the road, and the place where three men had stood and had not eaten.

Not one person in that yard asked me what I was playing.

When I stopped she sat down and put her hands over her face and made a sound I have not got a beat for.

---

I will not be hired on the hill again. That was decided while I was playing, somewhere underneath the counting, which is where I keep the things I have chosen not to look at yet.

Rule one. The drum is not sad. You are not sad. The people are sad and you are working.

My uncle put his hat on. He did not say anything to me. At the gate he put his hand flat on my head once, the way he does, and took it off, and went up the road.

I went home along the shore. The lake had gone black and there was no light at all on our side, and up the hill the windows were yellow, the whole row of them, steady as anything.

My mother was still at her table. She had sold most of the dough. She asked was it a good matanga and I said it was clean, and she said good.

I built a small fire in the yard. Not to warm myself. I held the drum sideways over it and turned it and listened for the note to come up, and it came up, and I kept turning it a long time after it was tight.

She came out and stood in the doorway.

She did not ask me why I was warming a drum I was not going to play.
