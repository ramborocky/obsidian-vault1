# THE BEAT OF MY HURT

There are eleven beats for the dead and I know nine.

My uncle knows all eleven. He will not teach me the last two, and when I ask he says the same thing every time, which is that a boy who learns the tenth beat starts looking for a reason to play it.

Rule one, he says. The drum is not sad. You are not sad. The people are sad and you are working.

The skin on my ngoma is goat. Cow is cheaper and it is louder and it is wrong, because cow gives you one sound and goat gives you the sound plus the thing underneath it, and the thing underneath it is what people are paying for even though not one of them could tell you that is what they paid for. In the wet season the skin goes slack by ten in the morning. So before every matanga I build a small fire, not to warm myself, and I hold the drum sideways over it and turn it, and the skin tightens and the note comes up, and you can hear it come up, and my uncle says a man who cannot hear his own drum tighten should go and sell airtime.

Rule four. Never the fast one for a child. The family will ask. You say no.

We live above the port, on the third street up, where the tarmac gives out. My father is dead. My mother sells fried dough at the bus stage from a table with a leg mended with wire, and she has mended that leg four times, and each time she says it will hold until Christmas.

Kalemie is a town at the bottom of a hill and everything rolls down to the lake eventually.

Up the hill there are houses with walls and inside the walls there are generators, so that when the délestage comes at seven, which is most days, their windows stay on and ours go out at the same second like somebody agreed it. You can stand on Avenue du Lac and look up and see exactly who has money by who has light. Nobody in this town has ever had to ask.

I have beaten in most of those houses. That is the part people do not understand about my work. A drummer goes in. The gate opens for the drum.

Rule nine. You eat what they give you and you eat it in the yard.

---

Come and beat for us.

That is how it arrives. Somebody's cousin finds you at the stage or at the church and says come and beat for us, and then they say the day, and then they say the name of the dead, and then, if they are the kind of family that says it early, they say the number.

Beat four is the old person's beat. It is slow, it has a lift on the third, and it is the one that lets people cry properly because it gives them somewhere to put the crying. Beat two is for a child and it is almost nothing, it is a heartbeat and a pause, and my uncle plays it and I am not allowed. Beat six is the drowned. There are a great many six in this town.

I charge fifteen thousand for the house and fifteen for the walk to the cemetery. The walk is the harder job. You are moving, the road is bad, and if you drop the tempo the whole procession folds in on itself like a concertina and the men at the front start arguing.

For a family with nothing, my uncle takes a chicken. He has never once said this out loud and he has never once written it down.

---

The Ngoie boy died on a Thursday and they came to us on the Friday.

It was the mother's brother who came. He was wearing a suit jacket over a football shirt and he stood at our door and did not sit down, and he said come and beat for us, and then he said Sunday, and then he said the name, Emmanuel Ngoie, and I knew the name because everybody in the down part of town knew the name. He was twenty-four. He worked at the port on the crane.

Then he said: beat four.

My uncle was fixing the lacing on the small drum and he did not look up, and I watched his hands keep going at the same speed they had been going, which is a thing I have watched him do before and which I have never been able to copy.

Beat four, my uncle said.

Beat four, the man said.

He is twenty-four years old, my uncle said.

He was, said the man.

They stood there. Somewhere down the hill a woman was calling her children in one at a time by name, and there were three of them, and the third one did not come.

My uncle said the price and the man paid it there at the door, in dollars, which people only do when they want the conversation to be finished.

---

Rule seven. What the family asks for is what the family gets. You are not the police. You are not the priest. You are a man with a drum and a fee.

I have thought about rule seven more than about any of the others. My uncle gave it to me when I was eleven, at a matanga in Katuku for a woman whose face I could not stop looking at, and he gave it to me the way you hand somebody a tool, without ceremony, expecting it to be used.

Here is what rule seven actually means. It means the beat is a statement. It means when the drum says four, the town hears old age, and the town relaxes, because old age is nobody's fault. It means a family can buy a version of how their son died for thirty thousand francs and a bottle for the drummers.

I have played four over a boy who was cut. I have played four over a girl who took something. I played four in a house on the hill for a man whose wife stood at the back with her arm in a sling that was two weeks older than his death.

Each time I took the money, because rule seven, and because we needed it, and because my uncle's hands did not change speed.

---

Sunday came hot with no wind, which is the worst kind for a walk.

I built the fire at six in our yard and warmed the skin and turned it and listened to the note come up, and my mother came out and stood in the doorway and watched me do it. She has watched me do it perhaps four hundred times. She still comes out.

The Ngoie house was full by eight. They had hired the plastic chairs with the blue backs, the ones that come from the man behind the cathedral who has maybe two hundred of them and who is at every funeral in this town and at every wedding and who has never, as far as I know, attended one.

Emmanuel was in the front room. There was a cloth over the lower part of him, from the chest.

Nobody was looking at the cloth. That is how I knew.

His mother sat where the mother sits. She did not cry at any point that morning, and people noticed, and I could hear them deciding what it meant, and they all decided the wrong thing because the right thing was not available to them.

At the back, near the door, three men from the port stood together and did not sit down and did not eat, and one of them kept checking the road.

I looked at my uncle. He was tightening the small drum with his thumb.

Beat four, he said.

---

So I played it.

It is a good beat and I am good at it. The lift on the third does the work; you place it slightly late, so late that a musician would call it a mistake, and the room comes with you, and the crying arrives where you put it. That is the whole craft. You are building a place for something to be set down.

They cried well. The aunts did the ululation, and the church women did the harmony they do, and the mother sat there dry as a stone.

We walked at eleven. The road to the cemetery goes up and then along, and there is a bad stretch after the water point where a lorry broke the surface two rains ago and nobody came, and on that stretch you drop from your hand to your wrist and you keep the tempo dead flat and you do not let the front of the procession hear you struggle.

I did not struggle. It was a clean walk.

At the grave I got the second fifteen thousand from the mother's brother, in dollars again, folded small the way men fold money they have been holding a long time.

Come and beat for us, he said, meaning next time. It is what people say.

---

I walked home the long way, along the shore, because after a matanga I do not want the streets.

The lake was doing the flat thing it does in the afternoon. There were pirogues out for dagaa and the light on the water was hard and white and it hurt to look at, so I looked at it.

And my hands were still going.

That happens. You come off a walk and the hands keep on for a while, they do not know it is over, and usually they keep on with what you played. Mine were not playing four.

I stopped in the road and let them do it and listened.

It was not a beat I have ever played.

It was slower than four. It had no lift on the third. It sat on the two and it stayed there, and the space after it was wrong, it was too long, it was the length of a held breath, and my hands knew exactly how long to hold it and I did not.

I stood on the shore road with my hands going and worked out where I had heard it.

I was six. It was our own yard. There was a fire and my uncle was warming the skin and turning it and I was sitting close enough to the fire that my mother pulled me back by the shoulder twice. There was a cloth over the lower part of my father, from the chest, and nobody was looking at the cloth.

And my uncle played, and the town came, and afterwards everybody said the thing they say, which is that he went quickly and God is good.

Ten years I have been asking that man to teach me the tenth beat.

Ten years he has been telling me a boy who learns it starts looking for a reason.

---

My mother was at her table when I got back. She had sold most of the dough. She asked me was it a good matanga and I said it was clean, and she said good, and she took the second fifteen thousand and put it in the tin.

I did not ask her. I want that written down. I could have asked her right there and I did not, and it was not kindness, and it was not fear either. It was that I had already done the arithmetic on what the answer would cost and the number came out higher than I could pay that day.

She said the leg of the table had gone again. I said I would look at it.

---

The seventh night is the last night of a matanga, when the family sits up and the drum comes back for one hour, and it is the only hour of the whole business that is not for the dead. It is for the living, to send them to bed.

I went to the Ngoie house on the seventh night. My uncle did not come; he had a wedding in Kabimba and weddings pay better.

They had taken down half the chairs. The mother was in the same seat. The three men from the port were not there and had not been there since the Sunday, and nobody had said their names once in seven days, and in this town seven days is a very long time to not say a name.

I sat down and put the drum between my knees and warmed my hands on it, which does nothing, and which I do.

The mother looked at me for the first time all week.

So I played the other one.

I got it wrong twice at the start, because my hands knew it and I did not, and there is a gap between those two things that you have to cross with nobody helping you. The third time it came. It sat on the two. It stayed there. It left the space after it and the space was the length of a held breath and I let it be that long.

The room did not cry. That beat is not built for crying and I understood that as I was playing it.

The mother stood up. She stood up on the second bar and she stayed standing for all of it, in front of everybody, with her hands at her sides, and she was not looking at me. She was looking at the door, at the road, at where three men had stood and had not eaten.

Nobody asked me what I was playing. Not one person in that room asked me what I was playing.

When I stopped she sat down and put her hands over her face and made a sound I have not got a beat for.

---

I will not be hired on the hill again. That is already decided and I knew it while I was playing, somewhere under the counting, which is where I keep the things I have decided not to look at yet.

Rule one. The drum is not sad. You are not sad. The people are sad and you are working.

I went home along the shore. The lake had gone dark and there was no light at all on our side because it was after seven, and up the hill the windows were yellow, the whole row of them, steady as anything.

I built a small fire in the yard, not to warm myself. I held the drum sideways over it and turned it, and I listened for the note to come up, and it came up, and I kept turning it long after it was tight.

My mother came out and stood in the doorway.

She did not ask me why I was warming a drum I was not going to play.
